Overview & Epidemiology

Key
Critical illness burden: Up to 7% of hospital admissions with haematological malignancy are complicated by a critical illness episode. Up to 13% of haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) recipients require ICU admission.

Survival to hospital discharge after ICU admission may be as high as 40%. There is presently no reliable way to predict futility of ICU treatment, although tracheal intubation with mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy are associated with poorer outcomes.

Patients may develop critical illness at first presentation or, more commonly, after chemotherapy or HSCT. Respiratory failure is present in approximately half of all ICU referrals; shock in 10–50%; multiple organ failure in about one‑fifth. Other causes include neurological failure, GI bleeding, renal failure, and metabolic derangement.

Source: Beed M, Levitt M, Bokhari SW. Intensive care management of patients with haematological malignancy. Continuing Education in Anaesthesia Critical Care & Pain (BJA Education).

Haematological Malignancies

Commonest types admitted to ICU:

  • Acute myeloid leukaemia (AML)
  • Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL)
  • Non‑Hodgkin's lymphoma

Less common: Hodgkin's disease, myeloma, chronic leukaemias.

Source: Beed et al.

Neutropaenia & Sepsis

Definition: Neutrophil count <1.0 × 10⁹/L is considered significantly low (some definitions use 1.5–2.0 × 10⁹/L). Remember: patients with high white cell counts may still have a low absolute neutrophil count.

Neutropaenic patients are at substantial risk of unusual/atypical infections (fungal, viral). Continue prophylactic antifungal, antiviral, and anti‑pneumocystis medications on ICU unless dose adjustment is needed for active infection.

Reverse‑barrier nursing is essential. Also at particular risk from individuals infected with varicella.

Iatrogenic infections (VAP, CRBSI, urinary catheter infections) are common — avoid invasive procedures (bladder catheterisation, tracheal intubation, central venous access) where possible in early critical illness.

If infection suspected (pyrexia, rigors) — even without a source — perform full septic screen (urine, blood, sputum); may need daily repetition. Start broad‑spectrum antibiotics with anti‑pseudomonal cover. Liaise with microbiology for local resistance patterns (e.g., amikacin may be preferable to gentamicin where resistant pseudomonal infections are prevalent).

Persistent/recurrent fever despite antibacterial cover: consider fungal infection — CT chest and empirical antifungal therapy (covering Aspergillus and resistant Candida).

G‑CSF is often used to promote neutrophil recovery, except where there is a low absolute neutrophil count but high overall white cell count. Rare association with splenic rupture (especially myeloproliferative disorders with splenomegaly).

Source: Beed et al.

Respiratory Failure

Default diagnosis is often infection, but consider pulmonary oedema, GVHD, leukaemic infiltration, pulmonary haemorrhage.

Tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation are associated with poorer outcome except where the cause is transient (post‑surgical, brief neurological failure such as fitting) or associated with pulmonary oedema.

Lung‑protective ventilation strategies should be adopted where possible. There is some evidence that avoiding tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation may improve survival.

Bronchoalveolar lavage may improve detection rates. Diagnosis of lung fibrosis is difficult without open‑lung biopsy; surgical lung biopsy review showed diagnosis was as likely to be infection (29%) as underlying malignancy (27%). Most common infection diagnosed by biopsy was aspergillosis.

Source: Beed et al.
6

Thrombocytopenia & Coagulopathy

The commonest coagulopathy in haematological malignancy is thrombocytopenia. Spontaneous bleeding may occur with platelet counts ≤5 × 10⁹/L, especially from sites of minor trauma (NG tubes, arterial lines).

  • Active bleeding or planned procedures: maintain platelets 50–60 × 10⁹/L.
  • No planned procedures: counts above 10–20 × 10⁹/L are often acceptable.
  • Central venous catheter insertion under ultrasound (elective): minimum 20–30 × 10⁹/L seems prudent. Femoral route is often considered in thrombocytopenic haematology patients despite slightly increased CRBSI risk.

Platelet refractoriness: If poor increment post‑transfusion, check pre‑ and 1‑hour post‑transfusion counts; HLA‑matched platelets may be required.

Other causes of thrombocytopenia may coexist: ITP, heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia, post‑transfusion purpura, drugs (e.g., vancomycin).

Source: Beed et al.

Tumour Lysis Syndrome (TLS)

High yield

Definition (Cairo‑Bishop)

Laboratory TLS: Malignancy plus ≥2 of the following within 1 week of chemotherapy:

  • Phosphate >4.5 mg/dL (>1.45 mM)
  • Potassium >6 mEq/L
  • Calcium <7 mg/dL (<1.75 mM)
  • Uric acid >8 mg/dL (>476 µM)

Clinical TLS: Laboratory TLS plus ≥1 of: acute kidney injury (Cr >1.5× baseline), cardiac arrhythmia, sudden death, seizure.

Pathophysiology (quick version)

  • Rapid tumour cell turnover → release of intracellular contents: potassium, phosphate, nucleic acids (→ urate).
  • Urate crystals precipitate in renal tubules → AKI.
  • Hyperkalaemia → arrhythmias, cardiac arrest.
  • Hyperphosphataemia → hypocalcaemia (tetany, seizures, delirium).

Risk stratification

RiskMalignancy examplesMonitoring
High (>5%)Burkitt lymphoma/leukaemia; ALL with WBC ≥100K; AML with WBC ≥100K; CML blast crisis; lymphoblastic lymphoma advancedLabs q6h
Intermediate (1–5%)ALL with WBC 25–99K; AML with WBC 25–99K; DLBCL stage 3/4; mantle cell blastoid variantsLabs q12h
Low (<1%)Most solid tumours; CLL; CML excluding blast crisis; Hodgkin lymphoma; myelomaLabs daily

General risk factors: bulky disease (LDH >1500 IU/L, WBC >50,000/µL, mass >10 cm), marrow/spleen/liver infiltration, pre‑treatment hyperuricaemia/hyperkalaemia/hyperphosphataemia, baseline renal dysfunction, older age, nephrotoxins, obstructive uropathy, inability to tolerate hyperhydration.

Monitoring

TLS typically occurs 12–72 hours after chemotherapy initiation. Monitor full electrolytes (Ca, Mg, Phos), uric acid, and LDH.

  • Low risk: daily.
  • Intermediate risk: q12h.
  • High risk: q6h.
  • Established TLS: q4h.

Consider screening for G6PD deficiency in case rasburicase is needed.

Prevention

  • Hydration: IV fluids 2–3 L/m²/day (~175 mL/hr) starting the day before therapy until the day after completion. Target urine output >80–100 mL/m²/hr (or 2–4 mL/kg/hr).
  • Diuretics: Loop diuretics may be used if urine output low; they also reduce phosphate and potassium. Thiazides may increase uric acid — avoid.
  • Allopurinol or febuxostat: Start 2–3 days before chemotherapy. Allopurinol 300 mg PO BID (lower in renal dysfunction).
  • Rasburicase prophylaxis: Consider in high‑risk patients or those with baseline uric acid >7.5 mg/dL.
  • Avoid nephrotoxins; avoid hypotension.
  • Urinary alkalinisation is no longer recommended (no proven benefit).

Management of established TLS

Rasburicase: Indicated for TLS with uric acid >8 mg/dL (>476 µM), especially with AKI. Contraindicated in G6PD deficiency.
  • Dose: Conventional 0.2 mg/kg daily up to 5 days; reduced dosing 3 mg fixed dose, repeat if uric acid elevated at 12 h. Consider 4.5–6 mg for uric acid >15 mg/dL or weight >100 kg.
  • Important: Uric acid levels after rasburicase must be collected on ice (pre‑chilled green top tube) and sent directly to lab to avoid falsely low results.
  • Hyperkalaemia: Treat aggressively. Hold potassium‑containing fluids unless K <3.0 mM.
  • Hyperphosphataemia: Renal diet; consider sevelamer 800–1600 mg TID with meals (higher dose if phosphate >7.5 mg/dL).
  • Hypocalcaemia: Only treat if symptomatic (tetany, seizures, arrhythmias).
  • Renal replacement therapy: CVVHDF effectively clears urate, potassium, and phosphate. May be needed acutely while waiting for rasburicase/allopurinol to work. Rasburicase itself is not cleared (molecule too large).
Pseudohyperkalaemia caution: Very high WBC or platelet counts can cause falsely elevated potassium due to cell lysis in the sample tube. Suspect if elevated K with normal ECG. Confirm with STAT potassium on ice in a heparinised tube or point‑of‑care monitor.
Sources: EMCrit IBCC — Tumor Lysis Syndrome; Deranged Physiology — Tumour Lysis Syndrome; Beed et al. (TLS section).

Other Organ Failures (Neuro, GI, Renal)

Neurological

  • Seizures and temporary dysfunction are common; causes include intracranial bleed (thrombocytopenia), hypercoagulable states (cerebral infarction, venous thrombosis), hyperviscosity syndrome (may need therapeutic apheresis), electrolyte imbalance, underlying malignancy.
  • CT head is often required to rule out bleed, especially after any trauma.

Gastrointestinal

  • GI dysfunction associated with chemotherapy, GVHD, infection.
  • Typhlitis (neutropaenic enterocolitis) — occurs 10–14 days post cytotoxic chemotherapy; especially with cytosine, vinca alkaloids, doxorubicin. May mimic appendicitis or C. difficile; abdominal CT helpful.

Renal

  • Acute kidney injury may be due to underlying disease, nephrotoxic agents, sepsis/hypotension, or hepatic failure (especially post‑HSCT).
  • Renal replacement therapy may be required; anticoagulation of filter may be relatively contraindicated in coagulopathy — haemofiltration with pre‑dilution alone may be safer.
  • Need for RRT is an independent predictor of mortality, although renal failure suspected to be due to the disease itself is not necessarily a bar to treatment.
Source: Beed et al.

Graft‑vs‑Host Disease (GVHD)

Tables

Acute GVHD typically presents with skin rash, diarrhoea, hepatitis. Can also cause significant thrombocytopenia, conjunctivitis, microangiopathic haemolysis. Peak 30–50 days post‑transplant (can occur after 100 days, especially with donor lymphocyte transfusions).

Risk factors: HLA incompatibility, unrelated donor, donor‑recipient sex mismatch, inadequate GVHD prophylaxis, myeloablative conditioning, peripheral blood stem cell source, increasing age, CMV‑positive status.

Management of GVHD in ICU

  • Increased doses of steroids.
  • Immunosuppressants: cyclosporin, mycophenolate mofetil.
  • Extracorporeal phototherapy (requires haemodynamic stability).
  • GI involvement may require parenteral nutrition to rest the gut; octreotide for severe diarrhoea.

Chronic GVHD: multiorgan syndrome resembling autoimmune disorders; occurs in 30–50% of matched sibling grafts. Patients are significantly immunocompromised despite normal neutrophil counts.

Outcomes & Survival After ICU Admission

Patients with haematological malignancies in ICU are a heterogeneous population. High APACHE scores, increased number of organ failures, need for mechanical ventilation, and need for renal replacement therapy are associated with increased mortality.

No single clinical feature or combination can reliably predict futility. Decisions not to treat an episode of critical illness should be made with regard to underlying condition, physiological reserve, co‑morbidities, and current condition, after informed discussion with the referring team and the patient/representative.
  • 2003 review: survival to hospital discharge 17–44% for ICU patients with haematological malignancy.
  • 2004 review (HSCT recipients): hospital discharge survival 4–20%.
  • Of those surviving prolonged period after ICU discharge, a significant number are cured or in complete remission. The aetiology and severity of the acute illness does not affect long‑term prognosis or quality of life — these are predominantly affected by the underlying malignancy.
Source: Beed et al.
