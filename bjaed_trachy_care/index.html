Key points

  • Many tracheostomy tubes exist — the correct choice depends on the patient (internal diameter, length, outside diameter, cuff, suction port, fenestrations).
  • The position of a new tracheostomy should be checked with endoscopy via the tracheostomy tube, alongside capnography and cuff pressure.
  • An emergency airway plan should be made for every patient with a tracheostomy undergoing surgery.
  • Critical incidents are common because of a displaced tracheostomy tube when moving a patient (and in obesity).
  • An intermittent cuff leak can indicate a wrongly sized or malpositioned tracheostomy tube.

Standard tubes

  • Share common features: an inflatable cuff, a neck flange and a removable inner cannula (removed for cleaning to reduce blockage — and removed during an emergency).
  • The cuff provides a sealed airway: allows positive pressure ventilation and protects the lungs from oropharyngeal secretions and gastric fluid.
  • Cuffed tubes are the most commonly used and are sized by internal diameter (ID) — but length and outside diameter (OD) vary between manufacturers, which affects selection.
  • Portex Blue Line Ultra: the ID falls once the inner cannula is inserted — a size 7 tube has an ID of 5.5 mm with the inner cannula in place. This can influence weaning from mechanical ventilation.
  • TRACOE tubes: the inner cannula must be inserted before the tube can be connected to a standard 22 mm breathing circuit — important to know in an emergency.

Specialised tubes

  • Extended / adjustable length: for deep-set tracheas (obesity, neck mass) and tracheal pathology such as stenosis, so the tip sits distal to the narrowing. Obesity carries a higher complication risk, usually from a tube that is too short — keep extra-long tubes available. Not all extended-length tubes accept an inner cannula.
  • Subglottic suction port: allows regular clearance of secretions pooling above the cuff — reduces microaspiration and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Also enables "above-cuff vocalisation" (retrograde gas flow past the cords) to aid speech, weaning and communication.
  • Fenestrated tubes: airflow passes through the fenestrations and the vocal cords to assist speech and reduce work of breathing (with the appropriate fenestrated inner cannula). Deflating the cuff is an alternative — it re-accustoms the patient to managing secretions and to airflow through the cords. Avoid fenestrated tubes in a newly formed stoma receiving positive pressure ventilation — risk of surgical emphysema.
  • Uncuffed tubes: for long-term tracheostomy in spontaneously breathing patients who protect their airway and manage secretions. Reduces tracheal damage from an inflated cuff, aids communication and swallowing, and allows respiration via both the upper airway and the tracheostomy.

How to decide

  • Adequate ID (so as not to increase airway resistance and work of breathing) balanced against OD and length (which may be too large for the patient).
  • Need for subglottic suction — beneficial in prolonged mechanical ventilation.
  • Need for a longer, extended-length tube — increasingly required.

Why it matters

  • Too short: the tip may impinge on the posterior tracheal wall or sit in the subcutaneous tissue; the cuff may herniate up into the superior larynx / vocal cords → reduced pulmonary compliance, persistent cuff leak, tracheal damage. Too-short tubes are the main cause of displacement and accidental decannulation.
  • Too long: the tip sits too close to the carina — risk of endobronchial placement.

How to check (use all three together)

  1. Capnography — essential to confirm the tube is in the trachea, but it does not confirm appropriate position.
  2. Endoscopy via the tracheostomy tube — assess the tube tip in relation to the carina and confirm it lies within the tracheal lumen without abutting the wall.
  3. Endoscopy from above (via the larynx) — confirms the whole cuff has passed through the anterior tracheal wall and that tube and cuff sit appropriately in the trachea. Can be done via a partially withdrawn tracheal tube during a surgical tracheostomy.
  4. Cuff pressure — 20–30 cmH₂O with no leak. Higher pressures suggest a malpositioned or herniated cuff within the trachea.

Key practical points

  • Keep the neck neutral during endoscopy — the natural position the tracheostomy sits in.
  • No single technique alone confirms position within the larynx — combine tracheal + laryngeal views, capnography and cuff pressure.
  • Document the tube tip–carina distance so future checks can be compared.
  • Oropharyngeal endoscopy is not needed routinely but is valuable if there is any doubt.
  • If the tube is not correctly positioned, change to a different size or an extended-length tube and recheck.

Who is at risk

  • About 30% of new tracheostomies are inserted surgically — usually as a planned procedure, or in a critical care patient unsuitable for percutaneous insertion.
  • Both groups have an increased incidence of difficult airway.
  • NCEPOD On the Right Trach: nearly 20% of patients required additional difficult airway equipment and more than 6% had at least one failed intubation attempt.

Emergency surgical tracheostomy

  • Usually for failure of the primary airway or upper airway obstruction.
  • Use high-flow nasal oxygen where available to slow the time to desaturation when intubation or face-mask ventilation is difficult or impossible.
  • This can produce an FiO₂ close to 1.0 → risk of airway fire if the surgeon uses diathermy. The tracheal window should be made with a scalpel to reduce this risk.

Head & neck surgery

  • Tracheostomy provides an unobstructed operating field and a secure route for ventilation after surgery — important because swelling and oedema worsen immediately postoperatively, risking airway obstruction.
  • Previously normal airway anatomy can become very difficult to reintubate in an emergency.
  • Usually performed at the start of the operation, after induction and oral intubation.
  • A laryngectomy tube may be used instead for intraoperative ventilation: it can be sutured in place and sits flush to the skin, keeping the circuit and heat-and-moisture-exchange (HME) device away from the operating area. At the end it is exchanged for a tracheostomy tube and position checks are performed.

Patient with an existing tracheostomy coming to theatre

  • Confirm which tube is in place: size, distance from the carina, presence of an inner cannula.
  • Identify the indication for the tracheostomy and whether the upper airway is patent.
  • Decide a backup ventilation strategy in case the tube becomes dislodged or obstructed.
  • Induce anaesthesia i.v. or by inhalation; intraoperative management is otherwise standard (monitoring, analgesia, ventilation strategy).
  • Postoperatively the airway is protected — no extubation required. Transfer to recovery once spontaneous breathing is re-established; later critical care admission depends on the surgery, the patient's condition and local practice.

Up to 30% of patients with a temporary or permanent tracheostomy may have a significant complication. NAP4 showed that most tracheostomy-related critical incidents relate to displacement of the tube — especially on movement, or in obese patients. The National Tracheostomy Safety Project (NTSP) algorithms are colour-coded for patients with a potentially patent upper airway and for laryngectomy patients.

Tube blockage — think & act

A mucus plug is the commonest cause of obstruction. Remove the inner cannula first, then suction. If ventilation is still not possible, the tube must come out — not as a last resort.

Airway clear — air flows freely through the tube.

Key principles of the NTSP algorithm

  1. Waveform capnography has a prominent role at an early stage.
  2. Oxygenation of the patient is prioritised.
  3. Avoid trials of ventilation through a potentially displaced tube to assess patency.
  4. Suction only after removing a potentially blocked inner tube.
  5. Apply oxygen to both potential airways (face and stoma).
  6. Use simple methods to oxygenate and ventilate via the stoma.
  7. A blocked or displaced tube is removed as soon as this is established — not as a last resort.

Laryngectomy patients

  • A separate algorithm applies — the principles and goals are the same, but without the conventional upper airway steps.
  • Laryngectomy patients do not always have a tracheostomy tube in situ — they may have other devices inserted into the airway.
  • Potentially life-threatening — establish the origin of the bleeding. Reverse or treat anticoagulants and clotting abnormalities, assess the airway for obstruction, and seek expert surgical help.
  • Sources: aberrant vessels around the stoma damaged at insertion; rarely a tracheo-innominate artery fistula.
  • If the patient is stable, perform endoscopy via the tracheostomy to establish the cause.
  • Use suction; localised bleeding can be treated with external compression, surgical clips or cauterisation. Gauze soaked with adrenaline or tranexamic acid can be packed around the bleeding site.
  • Deep bleeding within the stoma: hyperinflate the tracheostomy tube cuff to tamponade the vessel temporarily. Inflate slowly with air to prevent cuff rupture — usually 35–50 mL is sufficient, depending on tube size.
  • An uncuffed tube should be replaced with a cuffed tracheostomy tube or tracheal tube, and the cuff inflated.
  • Once bleeding is controlled → urgent surgical exploration and repair.
  • Granulation tissue at the tip of a long-term tracheostomy can bleed — treat with excision or cauterisation via a bronchoscope.
  • Remember: haemoptysis may be confused with bleeding related to the tracheostomy tube.
  • A recognised complication of both surgical and percutaneous tracheostomy insertion.
  • Risk factors: tight closure of the stoma around the tube, packing materials around the stoma, tracheal injury during insertion.
  • Displacement of the tube into pretracheal tissue can cause significant surgical emphysema, especially in a mechanically ventilated patient. The risk is increased by too-short or fenestrated tubes whose openings may lie within pretracheal tissue.
  • If displacement is suspected, do not attempt forceful mechanical ventilation — this can cause extensive surgical emphysema and make front-of-neck access very difficult.
  • Management is usually conservative, treating the underlying cause. Exchange a malpositioned tube for an appropriately sized one and site a non-fenestrated inner cannula.
  • Always assess for a pneumothorax — it often occurs simultaneously.
  • Most initial tracheostomy tubes have an inflatable cuff — usually for short-term use until the patient is weaned from the ventilator and manages their own secretions. Occasionally a cuffed tube is needed long term (reduced consciousness, neuromuscular conditions affecting the pharynx).
  • Target cuff pressure: 20–30 cmH₂O, checked regularly.
  • Too high: increased risk of tracheal stenosis, tracheomalacia and tracheal fistulas.
  • Too low: air leaks and microaspiration of secretions from above the cuff.

Intermittent cuff leak — think of these causes

  • An underinflated cuff.
  • A defective or damaged cuff.
  • High ventilation pressures exceeding the cuff pressure.
  • A tube too small for the trachea — often needs high cuff pressures to seal.
  • Importantly: a cuff leak can indicate a partially displaced tube, with the cuff herniating into the upper larynx.

What to do

  • Checking the cuff pressure is an essential part of the tube position check.
  • Persistent leak with normal pressures and no obvious cause → endoscopic inspection of tube position.
  • The tube may need to be repositioned or advanced; a too-small or too-short tube should be exchanged for a larger or extended-length tube.
  • Exchange may not be possible in patients requiring high levels of ventilatory support — balance this against the risks of a partially displaced tube.

At the bedside — every patient with a tracheostomy

  • Bedside signs displayed next to the patient, so important information about the patient and their tracheostomy can be shared quickly.
  • Spare tubes and inner cannulae — especially one size smaller.
  • Suction equipment and stitch cutters.
  • An emergency bell.
  • Nearby: fibreoptic scope and advanced airway equipment.
