Targeted MTP (T-MTP) — how it differs
  • Rapid availability of blood products (like MTP) but no fixed ratio — clinician determines desired ratio, type and amount.
  • Guided by objective clinical assessment plus POCT: FBC, blood gas, INR, fibrinogen, viscoelastic assays (TEG / ROTEM).
  • Individualises transfusion → reduces unnecessary transfusions and complications.
  • MTP and T-MTP may be activated interchangeably when necessary.

Complications of massive transfusion

Recognise and manage
  • Hypothermia — impairs platelet and coagulation factor function, enzyme inhibition, fibrinolysis, reduced citrate and hepatic metabolism, reduced drug clearance. Prevent: remove wet clothing, raise ambient temperature, heating blankets/lamps, heated humidified oxygen, validated inline blood/fluid warmers.
  • Acidosis / alkalosis — acidosis from hypoperfusion impairs haemostasis; citrate metabolised to bicarbonate may cause metabolic alkalosis. Continue resuscitation.
  • Citrate toxicity, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia — large citrate load + reduced metabolism (hypothermia, hypoperfusion). Hypocalcaemia worsens coagulopathy, cardiac function and systemic vascular resistance.
  • Hyperkalaemia — potassium leaks from stored PRBCs; worsened by tissue damage, renal insufficiency and acidosis.
  • Coagulopathy — dilution, consumption of clotting factors and fibrinolysis; exacerbated by hypothermia. Monitor with laboratory tests and point-of-care testing.
B Perioperative (Anaesthesia)

Preoperative anaemia optimisation

  • All patients for major surgery (anticipated blood loss > 500 mL): FBC, serum ferritin, group & screen (GSH).
  • Preoperative anaemia should be identified, evaluated and managed to minimise RBC transfusion — associated with transfusion risks, increased ICU and hospital length of stay.
  • Evaluate as early as possible; coordinate surgical scheduling with optimisation of Hb and iron stores.
  • Non-urgent elective surgery should be postponed until anaemia is investigated and treated — allow at least 4 weeks (2–4 weeks typically needed to optimise Hb).
  • Time interval between diagnosis of anaemia and cancer surgery should be individualised — delay has measurable impact on survival (e.g. breast cancer optimal within 90 days; colorectal 3–6 weeks; head & neck within 67 days).

Iron therapy

  • Iron deficiency anaemia or depleted stores: treat with iron alone.
  • Anaemia of chronic disease / inflammation: addition of ESAs may be indicated — ESA must be combined with iron therapy.
  • Suboptimal iron stores (ferritin < 100 µg/L) with anticipated substantial blood loss: preoperative iron therapy.
  • Enteral and parenteral iron formulations should be accessible to all disciplines.

Preoperative optimisation of haemostasis

  • Assess bleeding risk with a validated bleeding assessment tool (e.g. ISTH Bleeding Assessment Tool).
  • Coagulation screen not routine unless history suggestive of bleeding disorder or comorbidity with bleeding risk.
  • Aspirin monotherapy can be continued for most invasive non-cardiac surgery including neuraxial anaesthesia.
  • DAPT can be continued for low bleeding risk procedures; high bleeding risk procedures should be postponed on DAPT.

Intraoperative initiatives

  • Prevent hypothermia (Grade A).
  • Position patient to promote good venous return.
  • Acute normovolaemic haemodilution (ANH) — consider where substantial blood loss anticipated, especially rare blood group or religious belief (Grade C).
  • Intraoperative cell salvage — recommended where possible (Grade C).
  • Tranexamic acid — IV recommended in non-cardiac surgery with anticipated substantial blood loss (Grade B). Give prophylactically before incision when anticipated blood loss > 500 mL.
  • Suspected coagulopathy: guide transfusion by coagulation assays or viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) where available.
TXA evidence: POISE-3 — composite bleeding outcome 30 days was 24% lower with TXA vs placebo (9.1% vs 11.7%); however the cardiovascular composite safety outcome did not meet non-inferiority. Balance risk vs benefit.

Intraoperative decisions for transfusion

  • In the absence of haemodynamic instability, use a restrictive transfusion strategy — but clinical situation dictates.
  • Do not give prophylactic blood products preoperatively without evidence of a bleeding phenotype. Investigate abnormal coagulation profiles before surgery.
  • RBC transfusion should not be dictated by an Hb "trigger" alone — assess clinical status.
  • Absent acute myocardial or cerebrovascular ischaemia: postoperative transfusion may be inappropriate if Hb > 8 g/dL.
  • Do not transfuse if Hb ≥ 10 g/dL.
  • Postoperative acute myocardial or cerebrovascular ischaemia with Hb 7–10 g/dL: transfuse a single unit of RBC then reassess clinical efficacy.
  • Generally, platelet ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L or INR ≤ 2 allows invasive procedures without serious bleeding; lower counts / higher INRs may be tolerated.
  • Seek specialist / transfusion medicine / haematology advice for intracranial, intraocular and neuraxial procedures, and for platelets < 100 × 10⁹/L or coagulopathy.
  • rFVIIa and PCC: prophylactic or routine therapeutic use not recommended outside licensed indications. rFVIIa may be considered in life-threatening haemorrhage after conventional measures fail. PCC limited to licensed indications (e.g. warfarin reversal) — consult haematologist/transfusion physician.

Postoperative initiatives

  • Implement restrictive phlebotomy — reduce frequency and volume of blood draws.
  • Routine blood investigations are not advisable; request only with clear indications.

Alternatives & blood conservation

  • Recombinant erythropoiesis-stimulating agents should not be routinely used in critically ill anaemic patients.
  • In the absence of clear evidence of iron deficiency, routine iron supplementation is not recommended during critical illness.
  • Consider blood conservation devices to reduce phlebotomy-associated blood loss; paediatric sampling tubes can be effective. Avoid or reduce routine blood investigations in stable patients.

Blood components

  • FFP / cryoprecipitate: routine use in critically ill patients with coagulopathy is not advised. Identify and treat underlying causes. FFP may be independently associated with ARDS and acute lung injury. Patients with INR ≤ 2 may not benefit from FFP and can generally undergo ICU invasive procedures without serious bleeding.
  • Platelets: in the absence of acute bleeding, transfusion may be appropriate at < 10 × 10⁹/L. Higher count (< 20 × 10⁹/L) may be considered with severe sepsis or haemostatic abnormalities. Prophylactic platelet transfusion for elective CVC placement if < 20 × 10⁹/L. Platelets > 50 × 10⁹/L generally allow ICU invasive procedures.

D Cardiothoracic (CPB & Cardiac ICU)

Preoperative recommendations

  • All patients planned for cardiac surgery with Hb < 13 g/dL should be evaluated for iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) — iron studies (serum iron, TIBC, TSAT, serum ferritin) and reticulocyte haemoglobin (CHr or Ret-He).
  • IDA present if ferritin < 30 µg/L (< 100 µg/L with coexisting inflammatory disease) or reticulocyte Hb < 25 pg.
  • Non-IDA patients: evaluate aetiology of anaemia.
  • Anaemic cardiac surgery patients may be considered for iron supplement (oral or IV) — Class IIb.
  • ESAs with iron supplement for preoperative non-IDA in elective cardiac surgery — Class IIa.
  • Allogeneic packed cell transfusion unlikely to improve oxygen transport when Hb > 10 g/dL — not recommended Class III, Level B-R.
  • Appropriate duration of cessation of antiplatelet agent before elective cardiac surgery is recommended to reduce bleeding Class I, Level B-NR.

Intraoperative recommendations

  • Reduce haemodilution: reduce fluid infusion, priming volume and other CPB strategies Class I, Level C.
  • Red cell salvage for blood conservation in CPB Class II, Level B.
  • Ultrafiltration / modified ultrafiltration (MUF) to minimise haemodilution Class IIb, Level B.
  • Biocompatible ECC coating may reduce perioperative bleeding and transfusion Class IIb, Level B.
  • Haematocrit 21–24% may be considered during CPB when adequate DO₂ (> 273 mL O₂/min/m²) is maintained Class IIb, Level B.
  • Leucocyte-depleted packed cells during CPB to reduce infectious complications Class I, Level B.
  • Synthetic antifibrinolytics (TXA or EACA) indicated to reduce blood loss and transfusion Class I, Level A. TXA reduces bleeding and transfusion in off-pump CABG Class IIa, Level B-R.
  • Restrictive perioperative allogeneic transfusion strategy preferred Class I, Level A.
  • Prophylactic plasma in cardiac surgery without coagulopathy not indicated Class I, Level B-R.
  • Point-of-care viscoelastic testing (ROTEM/TEG) recommended Class IIa, Level B.
  • ANH may reduce postoperative transfusion Class IIa, Level A. Retrograde autologous priming (RAP) should be used whenever possible Class I, Level B-R.
  • Topical antifibrinolytic agents at the surgical site after CPB reasonable to limit chest tube drainage and transfusion Class IIa, Level B-R.

Postoperative recommendations

  • Transfuse packed cells based on clinical condition, not a fixed Hb threshold Class I, Level B.
  • Platelet concentrate if bleeding with platelet < 50 × 10⁹/L, or on antiplatelet therapy with bleeding complications Class IIa, Level C.
  • PCC or FFP if bleeding related to coagulation factor deficiency Class IIa, Level B.
  • Fibrinogen substitution if bleeding with fibrinogen < 1.5 g/L Class IIb, Level B.
  • Off-label rFVIIa may be considered for refractory non-surgical bleeding Class IIb, Level B.
  • Prophylactic DDAVP not recommended; consider in bleeding patients with platelet dysfunction (inherited or acquired) Class IIa, Level C.
  • Restrictive perioperative allogeneic transfusion strategy preferred Class I, Level A.

Haematocrit management on CPB

  • Haemodilution is an inevitable consequence of CPB with conventional priming volumes; degree relates to patient's initial red cell mass and circuit prime volume.
  • Advantages of moderate haemodilution: reduced blood viscosity, improved microcirculatory flow, reduced risk of hypertension at higher bypass flow, reduced haemolysis.
  • Harmful effects: peri/postoperative anaemia, tissue oedema, organ dysfunction (e.g. acute kidney injury). Severe haemodilution may occur in smaller patients.
  • HCT 21–24% considered adequate when DO₂ (> 273 mL O₂/min/m²) maintained. If HCT < 21%, PRBCs may be transfused based on evidence of inadequate organ perfusion: decreased cerebral oximetry > 20% from baseline, reduced urine output, increasing lactate, worsening acidosis, high vasopressor requirements.
  • PRBCs not recommended if HCT > 24% unless worsening clinical condition or bleeding.
  • Antithrombin concentrate is more effective than FFP in restoring heparin responsiveness and reduces FFP transfusion and volume overload.
  • To attenuate harmful effects of haemodilution, improve oxygen delivery during CPB by increasing pump flow.

Detection & management of anaemia

  • FBC at booking, 35–36 weeks, monthly Hb and at 1 month postpartum. More frequent (every 2–4 weeks) if monitoring response to iron therapy.
  • Laboratory tests: FBC · serum ferritin · serum iron, TIBC, TSAT (if anaemia of inflammation / iron sequestration) · RET-He if available (can replace ferritin and iron studies).
  • Oral iron prophylaxis: 40–80 mg elemental iron once daily, as early as possible if tolerated. If intolerant, every-other-day dosing. Avoid divided dosing (increases hepcidin, reduces absorption).
  • Therapeutic oral iron for IDA: 100–200 mg elemental iron once daily, in the morning. Do not exceed once-daily dosing. Expected rise 1.0–2.0 g/dL per month; continue 6 months after normalisation to replenish stores.
  • Parenteral iron: moderate–severe IDA from second trimester who fail/intolerant of oral iron or non-compliant. Avoid in first trimester. IV route only (IM not recommended). 200 mg raises Hb by 1.5–2.0 g/dL per week. Max single dose 20 mg/kg (iron sucrose 7 mg/kg).
  • ESA: consider in moderate–severe anaemia not responding to parenteral iron, after consultation with haematologist; use in combination with iron therapy.

Minimising intrapartum blood loss (Pillar 2)

  • Modified active management of third stage: prophylactic IM oxytocin 10 IU, or IV oxytocin 5 IU slow bolus, or IM Syntometrine 500 µg/5 IU (if not contraindicated) after delivery of the anterior fetal shoulder.
  • Delayed cord clamping — not earlier than 1 minute but before 5 minutes from fetal delivery.
  • Palpate uterus and ensure well contracted; deliver placenta via controlled cord traction (CCT); document timing of placental delivery.

Tranexamic acid in PPH

Dose & timing
Loading: 1 g in 100 mL 0.9% NaCl over 10 minutes
Maintenance: 1 g in 100 mL 0.9% NaCl over 8 hours
Or 1 g in 10 mL 0.9% NaCl (100 mg/mL) at 1 mL/min over 10 minutes, with a second IV 1 g dose if bleeding persists after 30 minutes or restarts within 24 hours of completing the first dose.

Give as early as possible and within 3 hours of onset of PPH. Slow infusion required (rate > 1 mL/min risks transient hypotension). Effect lasts 7–8 hours in serum. Benefit decreases by ~10% for every 15-minute delay; no benefit after 3 hours. May be used prophylactically with uterotonics in high-risk patients.

Other measures

  • Point-of-care testing (ROTEM/TEG) — rapid bedside assessment of all stages of coagulation; safely guides transfusion, especially in massive bleeding; surrogate measure for fibrinogen.
  • Cell salvage — reduces allogeneic transfusion and related adverse events; cost-effective where high transfusion rates predicted; leukofiltration reduces amniotic fluid contaminants.
  • Interventional radiology — uterine artery embolisation or iliac balloon occlusion in haemodynamically stable patients to manage severe postpartum blood loss and conserve fertility.
  • Recombinant Factor VIIa — not licensed for massive bleeding in the maternal population in Malaysia; limit to massive bleeding where standard obstetric, surgical, radiological and transfusion interventions have failed. Prerequisites: haematocrit > 24%, fibrinogen > 0.5–1.0 g/L, platelets > 50 × 10⁹/L, pH ≥ 7.2. Use in consultation with a haematologist.

Massive Transfusion Protocol — obstetrics

  • Each O&G department must develop and maintain an MTP including access to RBC and the dose, timing and ratio of blood component therapy.
  • Massive haemorrhage must be recognised early; MTP activation must be early.
  • Fibrinogen levels approaching 2 g/L are associated with severe haemorrhage — early cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate indicated.
  • Measure early and frequently: temperature · acid-base status · ionised calcium · haemoglobin · platelet count · PT/INR · APTT · fibrinogen.
  • When no further need for blood products — inform blood bank to deactivate MTP. Regular clinical audit of MTP in pregnancy.

F Neonatal & Paediatric

RBC transfusion

  • Restrictive transfusion strategy suggested in neonates and paediatric patients, including the critically ill.
  • Decision should not be dictated by Hb concentration alone — consider underlying condition (acquired/congenital heart disease, respiratory disease), anaemia-related signs and symptoms, and response to previous transfusions.
  • Use an age-specific procedural guideline covering Hb reference ranges, transfusion volume and rate, monitoring during and after transfusion, transfusion technique (e.g. syringe pumps), and recognition/reporting of adverse events.

Special considerations

  • Placental transfusion: preterm infants — deferring cord clamping 30 s to 1 min may reduce transfusion volume and incidence of intraventricular haemorrhage. Term infants — deferring at least 1 min likely reduces risk of iron deficiency at 3–6 months.
  • HDFN: consider IVIg; assess promptly after birth; intensive phototherapy for those at high risk of severe jaundice; weekly IVIg course considered for fetuses at high risk of early hydrops or death.
  • Prevention of hypothermia in paediatric surgery: air forced warming blanket, fluid warmer, radiant heater.
  • Intraoperative cell salvage may be considered in major paediatric surgery — requires local procedural guideline and trained staff.
Selection of blood products
  • Fresh RBCs (< 5 days): intrauterine transfusion (< 5 days if available), exchange transfusion. RBC < 14 days can be used in transfusion-dependent patients (e.g. thalassaemia).
  • Kell antigen: where possible, K-negative RBC for all females of childbearing potential who are K-negative or whose K status is unknown — includes fetal transfusion.
  • Irradiated cellular products (absolute): intrauterine transfusion and recipients of prior IUT up to 6 months of age; suspected/known severe congenital T-cell immunodeficiency; severe acquired T-cell dysfunction; HLA-matched cellular blood products. Relative: neonatal exchange transfusion (if no critical delay); very low birth weight neonates (especially < 28 weeks or < 900 g); certain chemotherapy patients.
  • Stem cells must not be irradiated. Hyperkalaemia may occur with large volumes of irradiated blood — use as fresh as possible (< 7 days) and within 24 hours of irradiation.
  • CMV-negative products: intrauterine transfusion; preterm neonates (up to 28 days after expected date of delivery); severe combined immunodeficiency who are CMV negative; stem cell transplant with CMV-negative donor and recipient; granulocyte transfusions for CMV-seronegative/unknown recipients. In urgent situations, use CMV-unscreened leucodepleted components to avoid delay.
  • HPA-matched platelets for known/suspected fetal and neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia: urgent transfusion if platelets < 30 × 10⁹/L in term infant or < 50 × 10⁹/L in preterm infant, even without clinically significant bleeding; with active bleeding, higher threshold (100 × 10⁹/L for intracranial bleeding, 50 × 10⁹/L for other sites). Consult paediatric haematologist. Check platelet count response within 12 hours. Random donor platelets if antigen-matched unavailable. IVIg may be considered.
  • HLA-matched platelets for platelet refractoriness from non-immune causes (splenomegaly, infection) — fresh, ABO-compatible, single-donor apheresis platelets. Screen for HLA antibodies if cause unclear. Avoid platelet transfusion in inherited platelet disorders (Bernard Soulier, Glanzmann) if possible; if unavoidable use HLA-matched.
  • Washed RBCs: removes antibodies, plasma proteins, additive solutions, electrolytes, metabolites, cytokines. Contains 10–20% less RBCs but depleted of 99% of plasma proteins. May be considered for severe transfusion reactions including IgA deficiency.
Non-pharmacologic blood conservation — phlebotomy loss
  • Use "as-needed" rather than routine sampling
  • Meticulous avoidance of blood overdraw
  • Return of void volumes to sampling lines
  • Use of closed inline sampling devices
  • Judicious use and "on-time" removal of sampling lines
  • Optimal sampling technique and sample handling to minimise rejection
  • Laboratory equipment using the smallest possible sample volumes
  • Non-invasive techniques and point-of-care devices
Fetal transfusion & products for intrauterine transfusion
  • Manage pregnancies at risk of fetal anaemia/thrombocytopenia in facilities with expertise in ultrasound imaging and invasive fetal interventions, with access to specific blood products and neonatal intensive care.
  • Assess with Doppler ultrasound of the fetal middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity to determine need for fetal blood sampling and IUT.
  • Prior pregnancy with fetal/neonatal intracranial haemorrhage or thrombocytopenia due to FNAIT — manage with IVIg. Fetal blood sampling to assess IVIg response, balancing risk of sampling against bleeding risk from suboptimal IVIg response.

  • Decision not based on Hb alone — use clinical status. Single-unit policy followed by reassessment where indicated.
  • Treat the underlying cause of anaemia. Iron therapy for iron deficiency anaemia regardless of whether transfusion is required.
  • FFP: routine use in medical patients with coagulopathy (including liver impairment) not recommended — coagulation tests correlate poorly with bleeding risk in liver impairment. Assess and treat underlying causes; tailor decision on a case basis.
  • Cryoprecipitate / fibrinogen concentrate: routine use not recommended. Assess and treat underlying coagulopathy/DIC. Specialist opinion advised for DIC management.
  • Platelets: establish and treat the cause. Consider for prevention and treatment in bleeding patients with thrombocytopenia or platelet dysfunction. Not all causes require transfusion — contraindicated in TTP and HIT. Avoid long-term platelet prophylaxis (alloimmunisation, refractoriness).

  • Chronic heart failure: increased risk of transfusion-associated circulatory overload — consider in all transfusion decisions. Where indicated, single unit of RBC followed by reassessment of clinical efficacy and fluid status. Identify and treat absolute and functional iron deficiency to improve functional/performance status.
  • ESA in CHF: significantly reduce mortality; improve 6-minute walk test distance and NYHA functional class; no significant difference in stroke, MI or other thromboembolic events.

Nephrology — CKD

Recommendation
Evaluate potential benefits of avoiding transfusions, ESA therapy and anaemia-related symptoms against risks of harm when initiating iron supplementation.
Adult CKD with anaemia not on iron or ESA: consider a trial of IV or oral iron to a targeted Hb if TSAT ≤ 30% and ferritin ≤ 500 ng/mL.
Non-dialysed CKD: consider IV iron dosed to a target ferritin 400–600 µg/L to minimise need for other anaemia management.
Dialysed CKD: consider protocolised IV iron supplementation unless ferritin > 700 µg/L or TSAT > 40%.
Concurrent absolute or functional iron deficiency makes ESA less effective at maintaining targeted Hb.
ESA therapy to a Hb target of 10–11 g/dL reduces RBC transfusion.
Do not initiate ESA in non-dialysis CKD with Hb ≥ 10.0 g/dL. Initiate ESA in dialysed CKD when Hb is between 9.0–10.0 g/dL.
Do not use ESAs to maintain Hb > 11.5 g/dL, or to increase Hb above 13 g/dL (increased morbidity).
Target Hb in CKD with anaemia: 10.0–11.5 g/dL.

Gastroenterology

  • Acute GI loss: well-compensated patients follow general transfusion guidelines; critically bleeding patients follow the massive haemorrhage / massive transfusion module. Restrictive transfusion reduces mortality compared with liberal transfusion.
  • Inflammatory bowel disease: IV iron may be required in patients intolerant of oral iron or to avoid aggravating intestinal inflammation.
  • Ascites in cirrhosis (large-volume paracentesis): necessity of plasma expansion remains controversial. Suggest albumin 6–8 g per litre of fluid removed when ≥ 5 litres of ascites are removed (Grade 2B). Suggest against albumin if < 5 litres removed (Grade 2C).

Haematology

Chemotherapy & haemopoietic stem cell transplant
  • Restrictive RBC transfusion threshold 7–8 g/dL for haemodynamically stable adults; 8 g/dL with existing cardiovascular disease.
  • ESAs may be offered for chemotherapy-associated anaemia when cancer treatment is not curative in intent and Hb < 10 g/dL (strong recommendation).
  • ESAs should not be offered when cancer treatment is curative in intent.
  • Iron replacement may improve Hb response and reduce RBC transfusions in patients receiving ESA with or without iron deficiency — monitor iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation or ferritin at baseline and periodically.
  • HSCT recipients are at risk of TA-GvHD — receive irradiated cellular blood products (gamma rays 25 Gy or X-rays).
  • Prophylactic platelets: trigger 10 × 10⁹/L in intensive chemotherapy. One adult therapeutic dose once daily for adults and children > 15 kg. Increase threshold to 20 × 10⁹/L if febrile and/or on antibiotics for suspected bacterial or fungal infection. Not required for bone marrow aspiration or trephine biopsy (apply local pressure). For lumbar puncture, central line insertion, percutaneous organ biopsies and most invasive surgery, increase platelets to > 50 × 10⁹/L.
Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)
  • Supportive care for all patients with symptomatic cytopenias.
  • Red cell transfusions to improve symptomatic anaemia; transfusion policies including Hb thresholds should take clinical factors into account.
  • IPSS Low / Intermediate-1 (or IPSS-R very low, low or intermediate with score up to 3.5) with symptomatic anaemia, or asymptomatic anaemia and Hb < 100 g/L: consider a trial of ESA.
  • Stable MDS not on intensive chemotherapy and without signs of bleeding: no prophylactic platelet transfusions.
  • Thrombopoietin receptor agonists may reduce bleeding events in thrombocytopenic patients with low or intermediate-1 risk MDS.
  • Do not allow Hb to rise above 120 g/L.
  • Consider iron chelation therapy after 20 units of red cells, or when ferritin > 1000 µg/L, in suitable lower-risk patients.
Thalassaemia
  • Transfusion-dependent thalassaemia (TDT): lifelong regular transfusions every 2–5 weeks to maintain pre-transfusion Hb 95–105 g/L. Higher target 110–120 g/L may be appropriate for heart disease, clinically significant extramedullary haematopoiesis or other conditions, or inadequate marrow suppression at the lower level. Keep post-transfusion Hb below 140–150 g/L. Use leucodepleted packed red cells (pre-storage filtration strongly recommended).
  • All patients should have RBC phenotyping; phenotype-specific packed cells considered in certain high-risk blood groups. Luspatercept may reduce transfusion requirement in non-transfusion-dependent thalassaemia.
  • Initiation criteria for TDT: confirmed diagnosis + Hb < 70 g/L on 2 occasions > 2 weeks apart (excluding other contributory causes), OR clinical criteria irrespective of Hb — significant symptoms of anaemia, poor growth/failure to thrive, complications from excessive intramedullary haematopoiesis (pathological fractures, facial changes), clinically significant extramedullary haematopoiesis.
  • Non-transfusion-dependent thalassaemia (NTDT): Hb level should not be an indicator for initiating transfusion therapy, except in considerably severe anaemia (Hb < 5 g/dL). Occasional transfusions for anticipated acute stress, Hb drop or blood loss (pregnancy, surgery, infections). More frequent transfusions considered for declining Hb with profound splenic enlargement (> 3 cm/year during maximal growth), growth failure, poor school performance, diminished exercise tolerance, failure of secondary sexual development, bony changes, frequent haemolytic crisis (HbH disease), poor quality of life — with reassessment for tapering/withdrawal when sustained benefit achieved.
Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA)
  • Autoantibody usually reacts with all normal red cells — finding compatible blood is almost impossible.
  • The autoantibody may mask a red cell alloantibody capable of causing a haemolytic transfusion reaction.
Community-dwelling elderly
  • Prevalence of anaemia increases with age. Non-transfusion interventions are not significant for reducing mortality, improving functional status or preventing thromboembolic events.
  • Multifactorial aetiologies — thorough investigation leads to correct diagnosis and management.
H Practical Transfusion Safety (Nursing / Bedside)

Before transfusion — the "rights"

  • Right blood group · Right blood product · Right patient · Right duration · Right place (acute setting) · Right vascular access
  • Emergency blood crossmatch form filled and sent as required; consent signed by patient or qualified representative.
  • Blood and blood product volume prescribed by doctor in patient notes.
  • Appropriate blood box used for transport.
  • Baseline vital signs recorded accurately: HR, temperature, RR, BP.

Administration

  • Use standard in-line blood filters; smaller filter (microaggregate) for platelets and FFP in special conditions.
  • Set should not be used for more than 4 hours (bacterial contamination risk).
  • IV set must be primed with 0.9% normal saline or the prior blood product.
  • If an administration set has previously been used for red cells, it should not be used for platelets.
  • Transfusion volume and rate administered as prescribed.

Monitoring during transfusion

  • Vital signs (HR, temperature, RR, BP) checked and recorded at 15 minutes, 30 minutes and hourly throughout, and after completion.
  • Observe continuously for signs of transfusion reactions: apnoea or tachypnoea · tachycardia, bradycardia or arrhythmia · cyanosis · significant change in systolic BP · significant change in temperature · haemoglobinuria · rashes.
Adverse reaction suspected: stop the infusion immediately → inform doctor STAT → send post-transfusion specimens of blood, urine and the balance transfused blood bag for transfusion reaction evaluation in the blood bank.

After transfusion

  • Document volume of all blood and blood products transfused in the intake & output chart and nursing notes.
  • Ensure blood investigations for laboratory assessment are taken: FBC, PT/APTT, INR, fibrinogen.
  • Return unused blood and blood products to the blood bank immediately if no appropriate storage is available.
  • Vital signs checked and recorded after completion of the transfusion.

General considerations

  • Each hospital should comply with its own local Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP).
  • The ward shall ensure only validated blood warmers are used.
  • A blood transfusion checklist should be used and completed for every blood and blood product transfusion.
