Bottom line — the 60-second version

  • What it is: new, symmetrical, predominantly proximal limb weakness in a critically ill patient with no cause other than the critical illness itself. Facial muscles are typically spared; tendon reflexes are reduced; the diaphragm may be involved.
  • Subtypes: critical illness polyneuropathy (CIP), critical illness myopathy (CIM), or both (CINM) — collectively “CRIMYNE”.
  • Diagnose with the MRC sum score (<48/60) if the patient is awake, cooperative and not sedated. If not, use the peroneal nerve test (PENT) ± neuromuscular ultrasound (NMUS).
  • No specific drug works. Prevention is everything: treat sepsis, shorten ventilation/ICU stay, early mobilization, glycaemic control, avoid over-sedation and unnecessary neuromuscular blockade/steroids, and feed sensibly (EN within 24–48 h, protein 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day; caution with high early protein in sepsis).
  • Mobilization + nutrition together — neither alone is sufficient.

1 · Overview & Epidemiology

Definition: ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) is a clinically relevant muscle weakness that appears in patients with critical illness and has no underlying cause other than the critical illness itself. It is a medium- and long-term complication that increases morbidity, mortality and healthcare costs.

Clinical picture

  • Symmetrical, predominantly proximal limb weakness.
  • Facial muscles typically spared; osteotendinous reflexes reduced.
  • Respiratory muscles and the diaphragm may be affected → difficult weaning.
  • Onset follows the critical illness (unlike Guillain-Barré syndrome, which precedes it).

Numbers you should know

  • ~20 million ICU admissions worldwide per year.
  • ICU-AW incidence ~25–30% overall (≈3–6 million patients/year); up to 50–70% in surgical ICU cohorts.
  • Prevalence in the critically ill can reach 50%, with up to 2% of muscle mass lost per day.
  • During COVID-19, muscle dysfunction and reduced mobility were more prevalent and could persist up to 6 months after discharge.
  • ICU-AW significantly increases ICU mortality — most patients die of irreversible (usually septic) shock.

Subtypes

  • CIP — critical illness polyneuropathy: axonal damage on electrophysiology.
  • CIM — critical illness myopathy: muscle damage on biopsy/electrophysiology.
  • CINM — critical illness neuromyopathy: both.
  • Umbrella term for the acquired neuromuscular diseases of the ICU: CRIMYNE (critical illness myopathy and/or neuropathy).

2.1 Premorbid health status

  • Incidence of ICU-AW increases with age; frailty prolongs ICU stay and worsens severity and mortality.
  • Frailty is closely linked to sarcopenia, which further amplifies the complication spiral.
  • Sex: no consensus — some studies identify female sex as an independent risk factor, others find no significant difference.
  • Obesity appears protective — reduced muscle mass loss via increased fatty acid and glycerol metabolism.

2.2 Multiple organ failure (MOF)

  • Long regarded as a crucial risk factor; usually caused by a septic state in ICU patients.
  • Weakness also complicates MOF from non-septic causes (acute pancreatitis, trauma, cardiac arrest).
  • Higher APACHE II and SOFA scores are seen in patients who develop ICU-AW.
  • ICU-AW and MOF exacerbate each other; MOF (usually from irreversible septic shock) is the predominant cause of death in ICU-AW patients.

2.3 Sepsis and septic shock

  • Up to 50% of septic patients require intensive care; sepsis kills up to one third of those affected.
  • Sepsis activates specific mechanisms: the main cause of muscle loss is an imbalance between increased protein degradation and decreased synthesis of new muscle protein.
  • Septic patients frequently carry nearly all other ICU-AW risk factors simultaneously.

2.4 Immobilization

  • A well-established risk factor. Muscle atrophy develops acutely from reduced use, or chronically with ageing.
  • In acute lung injury survivors, reduced mobility during hospitalization predicted persistent muscle weakness at 24 months.
  • Prolonged ICU stay compounds it with infection, potentially myotoxic drugs and mechanical ventilation.

2.5 Mechanical ventilation

  • One of the main risk factors; risk increases with duration, though even a few hours may act as a trigger.
  • Mechanism is multifactorial: immobilization, sedatives and muscle relaxants, malnutrition, hypoxia.
  • Prolonged ventilation is associated with diaphragmatic weakness and atrophy, complicating weaning.

2.6 Glycaemic control

  • Poor glycaemic control in the acute phase is associated with polyneuropathy, muscle weakness and worse prognosis.
  • Hyperglycaemia alters neuronal excitability, is toxic to neurons and impairs nerve microcirculation.
  • Good glucose control (insulin therapy ± non-pharmacological approaches such as early mobilization) improves outcomes and reduces ICU-AW incidence.

2.7 Drugs

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBA)

  • Traditionally considered a risk factor, but data are inconsistent.
  • 2016 meta-analysis (2254 patients): only a modest association, significantly biased by inclusion of critically ill/septic shock patients.
  • 2023 meta-analysis: potential association, but evidence remains weak (heterogeneous observational studies).
  • Older multicentre ARDS study: NMBA improved mortality without increasing muscle weakness risk.
  • Isolating the NMBA contribution from other coexisting risk factors is difficult.

Glucocorticoids

  • Increase myopathy risk through enhanced muscle catabolism, but the role in ICU neuromyopathy is unclear and controversial.
  • A 2018 meta-analysis found a strong correlation and recommended careful use; studies of low steroid doses do not confirm the association.
  • Because steroids are key in ARDS and septic shock, the benefit (controlling systemic inflammation, reducing ventilation duration) may outweigh uncertain neuromuscular harm.

2.8 High lactate level

  • Lactate is a crucial biomarker in the critically ill; both the absolute value and its kinetics indicate prognosis and treatment response.
  • A 2021 observational study (n = 68) showed hyperlactataemia (>2 mmol/L) is an independent risk factor for ICU-AW.

2.9 Parenteral nutrition

  • Prolonged and early parenteral nutrition exacerbates multi-organ dysfunction and prolongs ICU stay, thereby increasing ICU-AW risk.
3 · Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology is multifactorial: systemic inflammation, bioenergetic failure, oxidative stress and microvascular changes all contribute to muscle and nerve dysfunction. The key factor is an imbalance between protein synthesis and muscle protein degradation.

3.1 Protein degradation systems

  • Ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS): the proteasome recognises and degrades proteins tagged with ubiquitin by E3 ubiquitin-protein ligases. Two of these — MAFbx (Atrogin-1) and MuRF1 — are upregulated in muscle atrophy and are central mediators of protein breakdown.
  • Autophagy–lysosome system: degrades cellular components during stress or nutrient deficiency via autophagolysosomes. Skeletal muscle is highly active. Mitophagy (selective autophagy of mitochondria) is involved in remodelling of atrophic muscle; uncontrolled/dysregulated action contributes to protein degradation in atrophy.

3.2 Systemic inflammation and cytokine dysregulation

  • Critical illness — particularly sepsis — triggers a widespread inflammatory response with TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6.
  • These cytokines drive muscle catabolism by stimulating the UPS, accelerating protein degradation.
  • They also impair mitochondrial function, promoting oxidative stress and further atrophy.
  • Activation of the TGF-β/MAPK pathway together with the UPS may also contribute to muscle loss.

3.3 Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction

  • Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production with reduced antioxidant defences.
  • ROS damage proteins, lipids and mitochondrial DNA → exacerbates wasting.
  • Oxidative stress from muscle-specific mutant superoxide dismutase causes atrophy primarily through autophagy activation.
  • Impaired ATP production → bioenergetic failure → impaired contractility and repair.

3.4 Microvascular and endothelial dysfunction

  • Endothelial dysfunction reduces muscle blood flow → ischaemia and damage.
  • Capillary leak and microvascular thrombosis impair oxygen and nutrient delivery.
  • Inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress disrupt endothelial nitric oxide production, impairing vasodilation and microcirculation.

3.5 Immobilization and muscle disuse

  • Accelerates atrophy by enhancing proteolysis and reducing protein synthesis.
  • Rapid loss of type II (fast-twitch) fibres, which are particularly vulnerable — further impairing strength.
  • IGF-1–Akt pathway: Akt (protein kinase B) responds to IGF-1 and regulates both synthesis and degradation. Akt promotes synthesis via mTOR and inhibits degradation by suppressing FoxO. When FoxO is active it drives expression of muscle-specific ubiquitin ligases (Atrogin-1, MuRF1). Disuse → ↓ IGF-1/Akt → ↑ FoxO → ↑ proteolysis.

3.6 Neuromuscular involvement

  • Peripheral nerve dysfunction leads to CIP, driven by inflammation, oxidative stress and metabolic disturbance.
  • Axonal degeneration of motor and sensory fibres is commonly observed.
  • Electrophysiology shows reduced nerve conduction velocities and denervation potentials.
4 · Diagnosis

Diagnosis of ICU-AW is primarily clinical: symmetrical, progressive muscle weakness affecting all four limbs, typically sparing the facial muscles. The diaphragm is often involved, complicating weaning. Unlike Guillain-Barré syndrome, onset follows the critical illness rather than preceding it.

4.1 Medical Research Council (MRC) scale

  • The first step: assess muscle strength in patients who are awake and cooperative.
  • Manual muscle testing across 12 muscle groups (6 bilateral), each scored 0–5, total possible 60.
  • A sum score < 48/60 is diagnostic of ICU-AW.
  • Limitation: reliability depends on consciousness, cooperation and the absence of sedation.

4.2 Electrophysiological studies

  • Used primarily to distinguish CIP from CIM. Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG) are valuable adjuncts.
  • NCS: measures compound motor action potentials (CMAPs) and sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) — motor and sensory nerve function respectively.
  • EMG: assesses motor unit potential (MUP) amplitude, duration and fibre recruitment patterns; also spontaneous activity (fibrillation potentials, sharp waves).
  • PENT (peroneal nerve test): a simple, standardised electrophysiological method with good accuracy, usable regardless of patient cooperation.

Memory hook: SNAP is spared in CIM (myopathy = muscle only). If SNAPs are reduced, think CIP (or CINM).

4.3 Neuromuscular ultrasound (NMUS)

  • Increasingly studied as a non-invasive support to clinical diagnosis — no reference standard established yet.
  • Muscle: assesses mass reduction (quantity) and increased echogenicity (quality). Target muscles: biceps brachii, quadriceps femoris, tibialis anterior.
  • Nerve: transverse-section echogenicity (typically reduced) and cross-sectional area help estimate polyneuropathy, but do not differentiate its type.
  • Diaphragm: thickness at end-expiration and end-inspiration → thickening fraction (TF), predicting ventilatory response and weaning success.

4.4 Screening with PENT

  • Reduced peroneal CMAP amplitude has a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 85.2% for identifying CIP or CIM versus standard electrophysiological studies.
  • In septic shock patients, PENT predicted these conditions effectively and was paired with sural SNAP measurement to differentiate CIM from CIP.
  • Allows early, simple detection regardless of consciousness or cooperation; gains value when combined with NMUS.
  • In cooperative patients, the MRC scale remains a valid alternative.
5 · Management & Therapeutic Strategies

Management requires a comprehensive, 360-degree, multidisciplinary approach and can be divided into three fundamental phases — which are not necessarily sequential and often occur simultaneously: prevention, early recognition and effective treatment.

5.1 Prevention

  • Comprehensive assessment of patients, particularly geriatric/frail patients — functional assessment predicts in-hospital death risk and may be extended to all critically ill patients.
  • Treat sepsis early and effectively to minimise multi-organ dysfunction and prevent prolonged ICU stay; reducing the cytokine storm may mitigate neuromuscular harm.
  • Reduce ICU length of stay — less immobility and fewer nosocomial infections.
  • Early mobilization — a fundamental preventive and therapeutic strategy that preserves muscle strength and function.
  • Reduce duration of mechanical ventilation — protects the diaphragm, speeds recovery and lowers ICU-AW risk.
  • Glycaemic control — insulin therapy benefits both the central and peripheral nervous systems and reduces ventilator dependence. Early mobilization also promotes euglycaemia and reduces insulin resistance, offering a valid alternative to insulin therapy.
  • Pharmacological prudence — avoid overtreatment; individualise dose and duration of corticosteroids and neuromuscular blocking agents.
Practical tip: treat the patient's metabolic status as a priority — hyperglycaemia is one of the most extensively studied and frequently observed modifiable risk factors.

5.2 Early recognition

  • Prompt recognition allows early intervention to halt the underlying pathogenic mechanisms. No gold standard exists, which complicates identification.
  • Validated methods: MRC scale (requires active participation — a challenge in critical care), plus EMG/NCS (complex to perform).
  • PENT is effective as a screening method — early and straightforward, independent of consciousness or cooperation.
  • Combine with NMUS for added diagnostic value; simple and quick to perform.

5.3 Effective treatment

Mobilization

  • Initiate physical therapy and encourage movement as soon as medically feasible.
  • Improves glycaemic and metabolic compensation, respiratory muscle strength and ventilator-free days.
  • In septic patients ventilated >48 h and in ICU >4 days, adding functional electrical stimulation (FES)-cycling to traditional physiotherapy improved muscle performance on awakening from sedation and reduced delirium incidence.

Nutrition

  • Adequate protein and calorie intake is crucial to preserve muscle mass and support recovery.
  • Avoid both complete starvation and overfeeding in the early phase — both are harmful.
  • Guidelines recommend protein 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day, associated with reduced mortality; some studies suggest >1.5 g/kg/day.
  • Caution with high early protein (enteral or parenteral): increased intake has been linked to prolonged ICU stay, higher mortality and even reduced muscle mass — possibly through suppression of beneficial autophagy (which clears damaged cells and, in sepsis, intracellular bacterial products).
  • In a 2014 study, high protein intake (>1.2 g/kg) as early as day 4 was associated with lower hospital mortality — but only in non-septic, non-overfed patients. In sepsis the picture is more complex; further research is needed.
  • In the absence of contraindications: start enteral nutrition within 24–48 h at a low dose, gradually escalating to meet rising metabolic demands for tissue repair.
Key principle: mobilization and nutrition are two cornerstones — one without the other is not sufficient. Only proper integration of both improves muscle function. Prolonged immobilization reduces muscle protein synthesis after protein ingestion (anabolic resistance), a mechanism underlying disuse atrophy.

Emerging therapies

  • Beta-hydroxybutyrate (3-HB): produced from fatty acid metabolism during ketogenesis; parenteral supplementation showed muscle protection in animal models — a potential preventive strategy.
  • Targeting the ubiquitin–proteasome system appears promising.
  • IGF-1 analogues selective for muscle Akt (lacking the cancer-promoting risk of IGF-1) could combat muscle loss — though inhibiting protein degradation may cause accumulation of misfolded proteins.

5.4 Multidisciplinary team

Coordination between intensivists, physiotherapists, nutritionists and nursing staff is required to develop and implement individualised care plans.

6 · Pitfalls, Caveats & Differential Diagnosis
Do not miss: Guillain-Barré syndrome (onset before or at ICU admission, ascending, areflexia, albuminocytological dissociation), spinal cord injury, myasthenia gravis, drug-induced myopathy (statins, steroids), rhabdomyolysis, hypophosphataemia/hypomagnesaemia, and prolonged neuromuscular blockade (residual blockade).
  • Sedation confounds the MRC score. Do not diagnose ICU-AW in a sedated or delirious patient — use PENT or NMUS instead.
  • NMUS has no gold standard — treat it as an evolving research tool and an adjunct, not a definitive test.
  • Steroid and NMBA evidence is conflicting. Do not withhold indicated steroids or NMBA purely to prevent ICU-AW; instead, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary duration.
  • More protein is not automatically better — early high protein intake in septic patients may be harmful via autophagy suppression.
  • Facial sparing and symmetry help separate ICU-AW from central and other peripheral lesions.
  • Diaphragm involvement is easily overlooked and is a common reason for weaning failure — assess diaphragm thickening fraction.
  • MOF is the predominant cause of death in ICU-AW patients — usually irreversible septic shock.
