1 · Platelet physiology & haemostasis

Production & lifespan

  • Pluripotent stem cells → megakaryocytes; each megakaryocyte releases 1,000–5,000 platelets.
  • Thrombopoietin (TPO) is the key regulator: produced constitutively by liver and kidney; a minor inducible fraction from spleen/marrow during thrombocytopenia.
  • TPO is cleared by TPO receptors on circulating platelets — so the higher the platelet count, the lower the free TPO.
  • Normal count 150–450 × 109 L−1; ~100 billion platelets produced daily; ~⅓ pooled in splenic sinusoids.
  • Lifespan ~10 days (7–10 days often quoted), governed by pro-/anti-apoptotic balance.
Why this matters: platelets are anucleate and cannot synthesise new proteins. An irreversible receptor blocker (aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel) disables that platelet for its entire remaining lifespan — recovery requires new platelet production (7–10 days).

Structure

  • Small anucleate discs, 2–4 µm diameter.
  • Open canalicular system — invaginations of the membrane giving a large surface area; allows shape change on activation.
  • Dense tubular system — calcium store and site of prostaglandin synthesis.
  • Mitochondria, lysosomes, plus the two key granule populations below.

Adhesion

  • Vessel injury exposes subendothelial collagen. Platelets bind via GP VI and change shape.
  • Under high shear, VWF is essential: it binds collagen and creates a scaffold; platelets attach via GP Ib–IX–V.
  • Binding is stabilised by GP VI–collagen and (high shear) GP Ia–IIa.
  • VWF has two roles: carrier protein for factor VIII (protects it from proteolysis) and mediator of platelet adhesion.
  • Intact endothelium actively inhibits activation via prostacyclin (PGI₂) and nitric oxide.

Activation — three agonist pathways

  • Thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂): COX converts arachidonic acid → PGH₂ → TXA₂. Binds the TP receptor (G-protein coupled). Platelets express more thromboxane synthase; endothelium expresses more PGI₂ synthase. TXA₂ is a potent vasoconstrictor and platelet activator.
  • ADP: released from dense granules; acts on P2Y₁ (Gq → phospholipase C pathway) and P2Y₁₂ (Gi → ↓adenylate cyclase → ↓cAMP → ↑Ca²⁺). Both receptors must be activated for a maximal response.
  • Thrombin: binds PAR-1 and PAR-4 (G-protein coupled, similar second messenger to P2Y₁).
  • Common final pathway: PLC → PIP₂ → IP₃ + DAG → IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from the dense tubular system; DAG + Ca²⁺ activate protein kinase C → protein phosphorylation and degranulation.
  • cAMP is inhibitory: phosphodiesterase (PDE) degrades cAMP; ↑cAMP ↓Ca²⁺ and inhibits activation.

Aggregation & clot stabilisation

  • Activation causes conformational change and surface expression of GP IIb/IIIa.
  • Fibrinogen bridges GP IIb/IIIa receptors between adjacent platelets → aggregation.
  • Fibrin-coated activated platelets provide a scaffold for the coagulation cascade → "thrombin burst" → fibrinogen → fibrin → stable clot.
  • Aggregating platelets are key in clot retraction.

2 · Platelet function tests
Current methods are designed to detect reduced platelet function. There is no consensus on interpreting apparently increased function. All functional tests become invalid at critically low platelet counts (thresholds vary by method). Involve a haematologist before requesting differentiated platelet function tests.

Practical tip: in a patient with an unexplained low platelet count, always exclude pseudothrombocytopenia first — it is common, harmless, and prevents unnecessary transfusion or cancelled surgery.

3 · Platelet disorders

Quantitative — thrombocytopenia (<150 × 109 L−1)

Also consider splenic sequestration (hypersplenism) and dilution after massive transfusion.

Treatment principles

  • Treat the underlying cause.
  • Options: platelet transfusion · immunosuppression (steroids, IVIg) · TPO receptor agonists (romiplostim, eltrombopag) · stop the suspected drug · plasma exchange.

  • Reactive: treat the underlying cause; consider extended thromboprophylaxis and/or aspirin.

Qualitative — inherited

Von Willebrand disease (VWD) — commonest inherited bleeding disorder

Not strictly a platelet disorder: a defect of VWF, the chief platelet adhesion factor. Platelet function is normal (except type 3).

Qualitative — acquired

  • Antiplatelet agents — the commonest cause.
  • Extracorporeal circuits (CPB, ECMO) — initial activation and granule depletion, plus physical trauma to platelets.
  • Uraemia (end-stage renal disease) — multifactorial defects in adhesion, aggregation and secretion.
  • Severe liver disease — abnormal aggregation and reduced TXA₂ production.

Treatment: treat the underlying cause; DDAVP and tranexamic acid can be useful; seek haematology advice in complex patients.

4 · Antiplatelet agents — pharmacology
Receptors of interest: TP (thromboxane A₂ receptor), ADP receptors (P2Y₁ and P2Y₁₂), PAR (protease-activated receptor — PAR-1 and PAR-4 in humans), and GP IIb/IIIa (αIIb/β3 integrin, transmembrane). TP, PAR and ADP receptors are all G-protein coupled.

▸ Aspirin
  • Cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitor: COX converts arachidonic acid → PGH₂ → TXA₂ (via thromboxane synthase, in platelets) and PGI₂ (via prostacyclin synthase, in endothelium).
  • TXA₂ → platelet aggregation + localised vasoconstriction. PGI₂ → inhibits aggregation + vasodilatation.
  • COX-1 mainly responsible for TXA₂; COX-2 (abundant in endothelium) mainly for PGI₂.
  • Low dose (75–300 mg) selectively inhibits COX-1 → antiplatelet effect. High dose (1 g/day) also inhibits COX-2.
  • Low dose is preferred: higher doses increase upper GI bleeding without increased efficacy.
  • Indications: secondary prevention of coronary events and stroke in CAD, cerebrovascular and peripheral vascular disease.
  • Adverse effects: GI bleeding — reduce with a PPI or H₂ blocker.
  • Contraindicated <16 years (Reye's syndrome). Caution in elderly patients and asthma.
  • Neuraxial: no additional precautions.

▸ Dipyridamole & cilostazol (phosphodiesterase inhibitors)
  • Dipyridamole — pyrimidopyrimidine derivative; prevents cAMP degradation by inhibiting PDE. ↑cAMP inhibits platelet activation by lowering intracellular calcium. Also inhibits adenosine uptake by platelets and other cells, and inhibits PDE-5 → ↑cGMP → vasodilatation similar to nitric oxide.
  • Oral absorption variable; modified-release preparations have better bioavailability. Extensive enterohepatic circulation; elimination half-life ~19 h (10 h often quoted).
  • Indications (current, limited): prevention of cerebral events in TIA and stroke. NICE recommends modified-release dipyridamole + aspirin where clopidogrel is contraindicated or not tolerated; and as second line in ischaemic stroke if clopidogrel is contraindicated.
  • Cilostazol — PDE-3 inhibitor; similar mechanism to dipyridamole; more potent than aspirin and ticlopidine; metabolised by CYP3A4 (drug interactions). Headache and hypotension from vasodilatation limit compliance. Currently limited to peripheral vascular disease.

▸ Ticlopidine (first-generation thienopyridine)
  • Irreversibly binds the P2Y₁₂ ADP receptor → inhibits platelet aggregation. Very effective at reducing thrombotic events.
  • Use greatly diminished by serious side-effects: thrombocytopenia, neutropenia and, most importantly, agranulocytosis.
  • 250 mg twice daily; well absorbed orally; bioavailability 80%; highly protein bound; half-life 12 h; IPA 50% at 5 days.
  • Replaced by clopidogrel because of its side-effect profile. Not licensed in the UK.

▸ Clopidogrel (second-generation thienopyridine)
  • Prodrug requiring hepatic conversion to an active metabolite. Six times more potent than ticlopidine.
  • Active metabolite forms a disulphide bridge with cysteine residues on the P2Y₁₂ receptor, preventing ADP binding → irreversible inhibition for the platelet's lifespan (7–10 days), despite a parent drug half-life of 6–7 h. New platelet generation is required for recovery.
  • Dose: oral loading 300–600 mg, then 75 mg once daily. With a loading dose, 50–60% IPA is achieved in 4–6 h rather than 5 days.
  • Bioavailability ~50%, highly protein bound. Two metabolic pathways: (1) esterases → inactive metabolite; (2) hepatic CYP450, mainly CYP2C19, two-step → active form.
  • Excreted equally in faeces and urine. Use with caution in renal impairment.
  • Limitations:
    • Time lag from administration to inhibition of aggregation — not ideal for primary PCI.
    • CYP2C19 genetic variation → "poor metabolisers" (low responders) with higher rates of cardiovascular events after ACS/primary PCI.
    • Drug interactions: omeprazole competitively inhibits CYP2C19 and reduces clopidogrel efficacy; esomeprazole has similar effects. Use a different PPI (not omeprazole or esomeprazole).
  • Adverse effects: increased bleeding (particularly perioperative), diarrhoea, rash. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is rare.

▸ Prasugrel (third-generation thienopyridine)
  • Prodrug; 80% absorbed orally; rapidly hydrolysed by esterases to a thiolactone, then converted to the active metabolite in the liver mainly by CYP3A4 and CYP2B6.
  • No reported difference in efficacy due to CYP genetic variation (unlike clopidogrel).
  • 98% protein bound. Loading dose 60 mg, maintenance 10 mg. Achieves 50% IPA within 1 h.
  • Active metabolite irreversibly binds P2Y₁₂. Metabolised to two inactive compounds by S-methylation or cysteine conjugation.
  • Two-thirds excreted in urine, one-third in faeces as inactive metabolite. No renal dose adjustment needed.
  • Active metabolite elimination half-life 7 h; platelet function returns to baseline ~7–10 days after cessation.
  • Higher bleeding risk than clopidogrel. Contraindicated with a history of TIA or stroke.

▸ Ticagrelor
  • Cyclopentyltriazolopyrimidine (CPTP). Oral, reversible P2Y₁₂ antagonist — mechanism differs from the thienopyridines.
  • Binds an allosteric modulation site, causing a conformational change in P2Y₁₂. It does not prevent ADP binding but inhibits ADP-induced signalling.
  • Dose: oral loading 180 mg, then 90 mg twice daily for up to 12 months. >70% IPA at 2 h post-loading in 90% of patients.
  • Oral bioavailability 30–40%; rapidly metabolised by CYP3A4 to a circulating active metabolite. Avoid strong CYP3A inhibitors (ketoconazole, clarithromycin) and inducers (phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampicin).
  • Both parent and metabolite >99% protein bound. Primary elimination via biliary secretion. No renal dose adjustment.
  • Terminal half-life 7 h (parent) and 9 h (active metabolite).
  • Adverse effects: bleeding (class effect) plus transient dyspnoea.
  • PLATO trial: 18,624 ACS patients (35% STEMI) — ticagrelor showed significant reductions in cardiovascular events vs clopidogrel.

▸ Cangrelor
  • ATP analogue; short-acting IV P2Y₁₂ antagonist with rapid onset.
  • IV loading dose 30 µg kg−1, then 2–4 µg kg−1 min−1. Maximal inhibition within 15 min; rapid reversal after discontinuation.
  • Elimination half-time <9 min; platelet function normalises within 60 min.
  • Metabolised by dephosphorylation — not dependent on liver or kidney.
  • Attractive for bridge therapy in the perioperative period, or in ACS when surgery is a possibility. (At the time of the source review, FDA marketing approval had been declined pending conclusive phase III data.)

▸ GP IIb/IIIa receptor antagonists

GP IIb/IIIa receptors are present on the platelet surface in an inactive form. On activation they change conformation, binding fibrinogen and von Willebrand factor, which bridge adjacent platelets. Antagonists block this binding and prevent aggregation.

  • Abciximab — Fab fragment of a chimeric murine (humanised) monoclonal antibody. Also binds and inhibits the vitronectin receptor on platelets, endothelial and smooth muscle cells. Bolus 0.25 mg kg−1 then 0.125 µg kg−1 min−1. Plasma half-life 10 min (phase I) due to rapid platelet binding, phase II 30 min. Platelet function recovers over 48 h; drug detectable in the circulation up to 15 days. No renal dose adjustment.
  • Eptifibatide — cyclic heptapeptide; selectively binds GP IIb/IIIa only (no other receptors). Bolus 180 µg kg−1 then 2 µg kg−1 min−1. Dose- and concentration-dependent inhibition; 80% IPA within 15 min. Dissociates rapidly on stopping; mostly excreted unchanged in urine; plasma elimination half-life 2.5 h. Reduce to 1 µg kg−1 min−1 if creatinine clearance <60 mL min−1.
  • Tirofiban — reversible non-peptide antagonist. Loading 25 µg kg−1 over 3 min, then 0.15 µg kg−1 min−1. 90% IPA within 10 min. Excreted unchanged in urine and faeces; half-life 2 h. Halve the dose if creatinine clearance <60 mL min−1.
GP IIb/IIIa antagonists are associated with more bleeding complications than other antiplatelet agents, and thrombocytopenia has been reported with all three. They are not effective as monotherapy — the significant benefit is during PCI. In PCI they are reserved for specific patient populations.

▸ Vorapaxar (PAR-1 blocker)
  • Tricyclic himbacine-derived drug; selectively inhibits thrombin-mediated platelet aggregation via PAR-1. No effect on ADP-mediated aggregation.
  • Reversible antagonist at PAR-1 but behaves like an irreversible drug due to its long half-life.
  • 2.5 mg once daily; bioavailability almost 100%; achieves 80% IPA within 1 week.
  • Metabolised by CYP3A4; main route of elimination is faecal.
  • No dose adjustment in renal impairment or mild-to-moderate hepatic impairment. Contraindicated in severe hepatic impairment (increased bleeding).
  • Terminal half-life 8 days; IPA can still be ~50% weeks after stopping.
  • Approved (FDA, May 2014) for use with aspirin or clopidogrel for secondary prevention in patients with a history of MI or peripheral vascular disease.

▸ Newer agents in development
  • Terutroban — thromboxane receptor antagonist.
  • Elinogrel — P2Y₁₂ receptor blocker; can be given orally and intravenously.
  • Atopaxar — PAR-1 antagonist.

6 · Antiplatelets, surgery & neuraxial anaesthesia
The central dilemma: continuing dual antiplatelet therapy increases bleeding; stopping it risks stent thrombosis, MI and death. Each patient must be managed individually by a multidisciplinary team (surgeon, anaesthetist, cardiologist, haematologist).

Factors to consider

  1. Emergency, urgent (e.g. cancer-related) or elective surgery.
  2. Type of surgery — low, intermediate or high risk of bleeding.
  3. Time from ACS or PCI to surgery; if primary PCI, the stent type (DES vs BMS).
  4. Type of coronary lesion — global coronary disease vs specific lesions.

Practical points

  • Aspirin can usually be continued until the day of surgery; it may need stopping for specific procedures such as intracranial surgery.
  • Platelet transfusion may not rapidly reverse antiplatelet effects. Drugs with long half-lives remain in circulation; transfused platelets are also inhibited by the circulating drug.
  • Seek haematology/cardiology input in complex or difficult situations.

Antiplatelets and regional anaesthesia

There is no evidence that aspirin needs to be stopped before regional anaesthesia. For all other antiplatelet agents, allow sufficient time for platelet function to recover — determined by the elimination half-life and mechanism of action.

7 · Perioperative thrombocytopenia
Core concept: the platelet count does not correlate linearly with bleeding risk. The widely used severity grading (mild 100–149, moderate 50–99, severe <50 × 109 L−1) has limited clinical utility. The count at which any given procedure is "safe" is unknown and is unique to each patient.

Epidemiology & outcomes

  • Thrombocytopenia (<150 × 109 L−1) occurs in ~5–10% of perioperative patients.
  • A normal platelet count is 15–40× higher than needed for haemostasis — platelets also have inflammatory, immune and wound-healing roles.
  • Large observational study of asymptomatic non-hospitalised surgical patients:
    • Platelets 101–150 × 109 L−1 → ~30% higher likelihood of 30-day death (adjusted OR 1.31; 95% CI 1.11–1.56).
    • Platelets ≤100 × 109 L−1 → nearly doubled likelihood of death (adjusted OR 1.93; 95% CI 1.43–2.61), plus more sepsis, renal and pulmonary complications.
  • The relationship between count and bleeding becomes unpredictable until counts fall below ~10 × 109 L−1.
  • In a study of >1,200 cancer patients with platelets ≤10 × 109 L−1 for ≥5 days: 70% had ≥1 WHO Grade 2+ bleed, but only 2% Grade 4 and one death from haemorrhage. Compared with platelets ≥81 × 109 L−1, those with ≤5 × 109 L−1 had OR 3.1 for bleeding — but no distinct pattern between 6 and 80 × 109 L−1.
  • Age matters: in ITP, major bleeding risk was almost 30× higher in patients >60 years than in those <40 years.
  • Aetiology matters: ITP patients bleed less than other patients at the same count (larger, more functional platelets).
  • Perioperative fluids, blood products and drugs further attenuate haemostatic effectiveness.

Procedure-specific evidence

  • Non-cardiac surgery: single-centre retrospective cohort of 13,978 surgical patients — 860 had platelets ≤100 × 109 L−1, 71 received preoperative platelet transfusion. Transfusion did not improve patient outcomes or reduce perioperative red cell requirements. Transfused patients were sicker (higher ASA, lower baseline haemoglobin and platelets, more ICU admissions, longer stays).
  • Cirrhosis / end-stage liver disease: in 246 patients, platelet count was not associated with higher blood product requirements — findings do not support the UK recommendation of >50 × 109 L−1 for liver biopsy.
  • Interventional radiology: 18,204 patients, 2,060 with platelets ≤100 × 109 L−1 before needle placement; 203 transfused. Prophylactic transfusion did not reduce bleeding or improve outcomes when counts were >50 × 109 L−1; even at ≤50 × 109 L−1 it did not reduce red cell transfusion frequency.
  • Airway: evidence is lacking. In 166 patients with median platelets ≤30 × 109 L−1 for 18 days, only one non-epistaxis airway bleed occurred (throat/mouth).
  • Flexible bronchoscopy: appears safe in most thrombocytopenic patients. Retrospective series (150 patients) — one patient needed continuous suctioning, resolved spontaneously; 35 patients with platelets ≤30 × 109 L−1 had no clinically significant bleeding. BTS: lavage can be safely performed at platelets >20 × 109 L−1.
  • Acute abdominal surgery in haematological malignancy: retrospective review of 58 patients found intraoperative thrombocytopenia was not associated with prognosis.
  • Inherited platelet disorders: retrospective analysis of 423 patients — bleeding in 24.8% of procedures with inherited platelet function disorders vs 13.4% with inherited thrombocytopenia. The latter had infrequent surgical bleeding until platelets fell below 68 × 109 L−1. Highest risk: Bernard–Soulier syndrome, autosomal-variant Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome.

Anti­platelet agents and bleeding — an important caveat

PATCH trial: in patients with acute spontaneous primary intracerebral haemorrhage while on antiplatelet agents, platelet transfusion was inferior to standard care — more serious adverse events (haematoma enlargement or infection) in the transfusion arm (42% vs 29%). Patients in this trial did not undergo neurosurgical intervention. This challenges the assumption that patients on aspirin with cerebral haemorrhage benefit from platelet transfusion.

Evaluation of the thrombocytopenic perioperative patient

  1. Exclude pseudothrombocytopenia first. ~0.1% of individuals have clinically insignificant antibodies causing platelet clumping in EDTA (or platelet satellitism around leukocytes) → falsely low count. Repeat the count in a citrated or heparinised tube.
  2. Take a thorough haematological history — prior count trends, previous thrombocytopenic episodes.
  3. Take an exacting drug history, including timing of administration.
  4. Classify by mechanism: ↓production · ↑destruction · sequestration · dilution (or a combination).
  5. Examine the peripheral blood film — large platelets (↑MPV, consumptive) and schistocytes (microangiopathy or endovascular device destruction) are the most relevant findings.
  6. If platelets were transfused, obtain a count within 30 min of the first transfusion to calculate the corrected count increment (CCI). Failure to rise suggests antiplatelet antibodies / alloimmunisation.
  7. Bone marrow examination is not routine — reserve for suspected acute marrow failure (e.g. drug-induced aplastic anaemia).
  8. Involve haematology for unclear aetiology, severe thrombocytopenia, unexpected bleeding, non-emergent surgery, or postoperative thrombocytopenia.

Drug-induced thrombocytopenia

Mechanisms: immune-mediated destruction (e.g. vancomycin), marrow suppression (e.g. linezolid), precipitation of thrombotic microangiopathy (e.g. cyclosporine), and induction of platelet activation (e.g. heparin).

  • Commonest in the postoperative setting: heparin agents and antibiotics.
  • Drugs with the strongest evidence of association: GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors, amiodarone, ampicillin, haloperidol, ibuprofen, naproxen, piperacillin, ranitidine, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, vancomycin.
  • Each agent only rarely causes thrombocytopenia — timing of drug initiation vs onset of thrombocytopenia is critical to establishing the link.
  • If the count is high enough that bleeding is not a concern, monitor. If bleeding becomes a concern, stop the most likely offending drug(s); recovery after withdrawal supports the diagnosis.
  • Testing for drug-dependent antiplatelet antibodies may be performed but results rarely return in time to guide urgent decisions. A positive test confirms; a negative test does not exclude.
  • Corticosteroids have not demonstrated efficacy in drug-induced ITP (unlike primary ITP). IVIg may work. If ineffective, a TPO receptor agonist is a reasonable off-label consideration.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)

  • Occurs in up to 2.5% of inpatients receiving heparin; most common in surgical patients (especially major trauma or major surgery).
  • Results from immunological recognition of heparin–platelet factor 4 (PF4) complexes, typically 5–10 days after heparin initiation → platelet activation, thrombocytopenia and high thrombotic risk.
  • In HIT: venous thromboembolism 17–55%, arterial thrombosis 3–10%, mortality 5–10%.
  • Distinguish from heparin-associated thrombocytopenia (HAT) — a clinically insignificant modest count fall in the first few days. In cardiac surgery, ~half develop HAT but only 1–2% develop HIT.

HIT management

  1. Apply the 4Ts score. Intermediate or higher probability → test for anti-heparin–PF4 antibodies (IgG-specific ELISA).
  2. Perform four-limb Doppler ultrasonography to screen for occult venous thrombosis.
  3. Stop heparin and replace with an alternative — typically a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban or bivalirudin); fondaparinux can be used off-label.
  4. If ELISA is suggestive, confirm with a functional assay (serotonin release assay or heparin-induced platelet aggregation).
  5. If confirmed, avoid all heparin-containing products except in specific, time-limited, controlled circumstances where no alternative exists (e.g. cardiopulmonary bypass).
  6. The platelet count normalises over 3–4 days after heparin withdrawal. Once ≥100 × 109 L−1, transition the IV direct thrombin inhibitor to an oral anticoagulant or subcutaneous fondaparinux (warfarin has the greatest experience; a DOAC is reasonable).
  7. For patients without thrombosis, guidelines suggest anticoagulation for a minimum of 4 weeks.

Alternatives to platelet transfusion

Platelet transfusion is the only therapy that rapidly and predictably raises the platelet count, but it carries risks: volume overload, febrile non-haemolytic reactions, TRALI, allergy, immunomodulation, post-transfusion purpura, infection and alloimmunisation. Platelets cannot be frozen, so they carry the highest bacterial sepsis risk of any blood product. Transfusion-related immunosuppression may increase infection risk and cancer relapse after oncological surgery.

8 · Obstetric haemostasis & central neuraxial block
Key principle: haemostatic disorders are not an absolute contraindication to central neuraxial block (CNB). Risk is a continuum, not binary. The risk of CNB must be balanced against the risks of general anaesthesia in pregnancy (failed intubation, awareness, aspiration). Decisions need senior clinicians and a documented discussion with the patient.

Normal haemostasis in pregnancy

  • Haemostasis depends on interactions between vessels, platelets, clotting factors, anticoagulants and the fibrinolytic system.
  • Vessel injury → platelets bind exposed collagen → activation and initial aggregates. Circulating VWF also binds collagen and aids adhesion.
  • Coagulation centres on thrombin, which cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin — the scaffold stabilising platelet aggregates.
  • Inhibitory anticoagulant pathways confine the response to the site of injury.
  • Pregnancy: several clotting factors increase, while fibrinolytic and anticoagulant (specifically protein S) activity is reduced → minimises bleeding at delivery but increases thrombotic risk.

General management of the obstetric patient with a haemostatic disorder

  • Multidisciplinary approach is crucial.
  • Counsel before conception about risks to pregnancy, delivery and the neonate.
  • Review early by obstetricians and haematologists once pregnant.
  • Early in the third trimester, make a multidisciplinary delivery plan including: monitoring required, mode of delivery, fetal precautions, method of analgesia/anaesthesia, haemostatic management, postnatal care, neonatal management.
  • Still follow the departmental major obstetric haemorrhage protocol, incorporating disease-specific management.
  • NAP 3 data: death or paraplegia secondary to CNB in normal pregnancy is 1 in 80,000 to 1 in 360,000. The extent to which a haemostatic disorder increases this is hard to quantify but likely small.

Von Willebrand disease in pregnancy

  • VWF is a large glycoprotein required for platelet adhesion and to stabilise factor VIII.
  • Plasma VWF concentrations increase 2–3 fold in normal pregnancy, returning to baseline by ~7 days postpartum.
  • VWD is the commonest inherited bleeding disorder — estimated prevalence 1%, but clinically significant disease in only 1 in 10,000.
  • Diagnosis: VWF activity <0.3 IU mL−1 with a bleeding history.
  • Type 1 — 75% of symptomatic patients; partial quantitative deficiency; VWF usually rises into the normal range during pregnancy.
  • Type 2 — ~20%; functional defects classified by impact on VWF binding to platelets, collagen or FVIII.
  • Type 3 — total VWF deficiency; FVIII survival substantially reduced.
  • Low VWF — bleeding history with VWF activity 0.3–0.5 IU mL−1; a risk factor for bleeding, but seek other causes.

Management

  • Severe type 1, and all type 2 and 3, should be managed in a high-risk obstetric unit with haemophilia expertise.
  • Treatment must be guided by a haemophilia-experienced haematologist: desmopressin (stimulates release of VWF from Weibel–Palade bodies; mainly useful in mild type 1); plasma-derived FVIII and VWF concentrates in severe forms. No commercially available recombinant VWF concentrate.
  • Platelets contain ~10% of total blood VWF; platelet transfusion is used as adjunctive therapy in some patients.
  • Avoid IM injections when FVIII:C is low.
  • Avoid NSAIDs when VWF activity <0.5 IU mL−1.
  • Postpartum thromboprophylaxis with LMWH can be used in VWD patients with adequate correction of VWF activity and FVIII.
Neuraxial anaesthesia and VWD: CNB can be offered in type 1 VWD when VWF activity and FVIII:C are >0.5 IU mL−1 with a normal coagulation screen and platelet count. In types 2 and 3, UK guidance advises against CNB, even after VWF replacement — achieving normal laboratory VWF values does not usually equate to restored normal haemostasis. Because VWF falls early postpartum, remove the epidural catheter soon after delivery or consider repeat treatment before removal.

Haemophilia A and B

  • X-linked congenital bleeding disorder: factor VIII deficiency (haemophilia A) or factor IX deficiency (haemophilia B).
  • Typically affects males, but female carriers may have factor concentrations below the normal range.
  • Occurs in 1:5,000 male births; 80% have haemophilia A.

Antenatal management of carriers

  • Care in an obstetric unit with 24-hour laboratory monitoring and close liaison with a haemophilia centre.
  • Most carriers have normal factor concentrations before pregnancy. FVIII rises during pregnancy; FIX does not.
  • Check baseline factor concentrations before conception, then at booking, in the third trimester, before any invasive procedure, and if bleeding occurs.
  • Target factor concentrations ≥0.5 IU mL−1; treat if below.
  • Desmopressin increases FVIII but not factor IX. Recombinant factor concentrates are the treatment of choice when desmopressin cannot be used; monitor factor concentrations after replacement.
  • Tranexamic acid — as sole agent if factor concentrations >0.5 IU mL−1, or as an adjunct if below. Continue postpartum until lochia is normal.

Delivery

  • Determine fetal sex and haemophilia status where possible. Neonatal intracranial bleeding rates are lowest with elective Caesarean section — consider for known affected male fetuses or where antenatal diagnosis has not been performed.
  • If vaginal delivery is planned, avoid ventouse extraction and mid-cavity forceps.
  • No special measures are generally required for delivery of a female fetus to a carrier; may be managed in a local unit.
  • Maintain FVIII or FIX >0.5 IU mL−1 for ≥3 days after vaginal delivery and 5 days after Caesarean section.
  • FVIII falls rapidly after delivery — risk of primary and secondary postpartum haemorrhage is high, even in treated women or those with FVIII >0.5 IU mL−1.
  • IM injections and pharmacological thromboprophylaxis can be used when factor concentrations are normal.
CNB and haemophilia carriers: FVIII and FIX concentrations should be >0.5 IU mL−1 before CNB, and should be rechecked and normalised before epidural catheter removal.

Thrombocytopenia in pregnancy

Defined as <150 × 109 L−1. Affects 7–11% of pregnancies — after anaemia, the commonest haematological disorder in pregnancy. Causes may be pregnancy-specific, pregnancy-associated, or pre-existing.

Target platelets >50 × 109 L−1 before delivery (surgical bleeding less likely above this) — assuming platelets are functionally normal. Some hereditary thrombocytopenias also affect function.

Gestational thrombocytopenia (GT)

  • Accounts for 75% of patients presenting with low platelets in pregnancy.
  • Most have platelets >100 × 109 L−1, but 10% are below this.
  • Presents in the third trimester, resolves spontaneously after delivery, no adverse fetal effects, asymptomatic. Tends to recur in subsequent pregnancies but the count is normal between pregnancies.
  • Pathophysiology unknown — theories include insufficient thrombopoietin and increased consumption/turnover.
  • Platelet counts fall by an average of 17% during pregnancy, so women starting with a low-normal count often fall into the thrombocytopenic range.
  • Management: observational, no specific treatment. Counts <70 × 109 L−1 not associated with pre-eclampsia warrant further investigation (rare in GT).

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

  • Affects 1 in 1,000 pregnancies; commonest cause of low platelets in the first and second trimesters.
  • May decrease only in the third trimester, making it hard to distinguish from GT.
  • Autoimmune: IgG antibodies against glycoprotein complexes on the platelet membrane → sequestration and destruction by the spleen and reticuloendothelial system.
  • Pre-existing in 60–70%; isolated thrombocytopenia <100 × 109 L−1. Most are asymptomatic or have minor bruising; no specific tests.
  • Serious bleeding (intracranial haemorrhage, GI bleeding) usually occurs only when platelets fall below 30 × 109 L−1.
  • Antibodies cross the placenta → neonatal thrombocytopenia (rarely clinically significant). Mode of delivery should be determined by obstetric indications alone; avoid ventouse and use mid-cavity forceps with caution.

Management of ITP: monitor regularly — around 30% need treatment during pregnancy.

  • First and second trimesters: aim to prevent major bleeds. Treat if platelets <30 × 109 L−1 or if there is any bleeding.
  • First-line: corticosteroids (e.g. prednisolone 10–20 mg daily). Two-thirds improve within 5–7 days.
  • Non-responders or those needing a rapid response: IV IgG (single dose 0.5 g kg−1) — rapid rise but variable, often short-lived effect; causes malaise, headache, risk of haemolysis.
  • Platelet transfusion is not generally recommended except in an emergency, and should be given with IVIg — transfused platelets are rapidly consumed.
  • Dexamethasone or high-dose methylprednisolone with IVIg for uncontrolled bleeding.

Pre-eclampsia and HELLP

  • Part of the same disease spectrum — multisystem disorders in which abnormal haemostasis is one component.
  • Pre-eclampsia: hypertensive disorder after 20 weeks with proteinuria; second commonest cause of thrombocytopenia in pregnancy.
  • HELLP complicates 10–20% of severe pre-eclampsia. Endothelial damage activates the coagulation cascade → consumption of platelets and clotting factors. Severity of thrombocytopenia correlates with disease severity; counts can fall rapidly.
  • Management is supportive (blood pressure control, seizure prophylaxis, careful fluid balance) — the disease will not reverse until the fetus is delivered.
  • Coagulation screen (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen) if platelets <100 × 109 L−1.
  • Because counts can fall rapidly, a platelet count and coagulation screen within the previous 6 h should be available before CNB.
CNB thresholds — summary
  • GT / ITP: platelets >75 × 109 L−1 (stable, no bruising/bleeding history, normal coagulation) — no increased risk. An experienced anaesthetist may consider CNB at >50 × 109 L−1 after careful risk–benefit assessment. A smaller needle (spinal rather than epidural/CSE) is less likely to cause vascular injury.
  • Pre-eclampsia: >100 × 109 L−1 — no increased risk. More caution between 75 and 100.
  • HELLP: check the count immediately before siting a CNB.
  • Catheter removal: a count satisfactory at insertion may fall subsequently. A proportion of vertebral haematomas in NAP 3 occurred at catheter removal. Apply the same criteria for removal as for insertion.

Five things not to forget
  1. Exclude pseudothrombocytopenia before acting on a low count.
  2. Aspirin rarely needs stopping — not for neuraxial block, and usually not for surgery.
  3. Platelet count is a poor linear predictor of bleeding risk; function, aetiology and age matter.
  4. Platelet transfusion is not a benign reversal agent — it may be harmful in antiplatelet-associated ICH.
  5. Use the same platelet criteria for epidural catheter removal as for insertion.
