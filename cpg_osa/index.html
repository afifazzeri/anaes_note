1 Screening & Risk Stratification
KEY RECOMMENDATION STOP-BANG should be used for screening adults with clinical suspicion of OSA — including preoperatively. Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) may be used to assess daytime sleepiness.

Referral trigger: clinical suspicion of OSA ± cardiometabolic risk factors and STOP-BANG ≥ 3 → refer for a diagnostic sleep test.

Interpretation: STOP-BANG is highly sensitive but poorly specific — excellent for ruling out, poor for ruling in. A negative score does not exclude OSA.

Clinical suspicion — symptoms

Daytime

  • Excessive daytime sleepiness
  • Unrefreshed sleep, tiredness / fatigue
  • Early morning headache, dry mouth
  • Poor attention / concentration, memory impairment
  • Mood disturbance or irritability
  • Decreased libido / erectile dysfunction

Night-time

  • Snoring
  • Witnessed apnoea (cessation of breathing)
  • Gasping / choking
  • Nocturia
  • Difficulty initiating and maintaining sleep
  • Fragmented sleep (frequent wakening)

Clinical signs

Raised blood pressure · large neck circumference · obesity · crowded oropharynx (modified Mallampati grade 3–4) · micrognathia / retrognathia · high-arched palate · enlarged tonsils · macroglossia.

Preoperative screening specifics

  • OSA screening should be part of the standard pre-anaesthetic evaluation.
  • Alternative tools: P-SAP score, Berlin Questionnaire, ASA checklist.
  • STOP-BANG score 5–8 correlates with AHI > 15/hr (OR 2.9 vs score 3–4); score 3–4 alone did not correlate with AHI > 15/hr.
  • Insufficient evidence to cancel or delay surgery to confirm OSA, unless there is evidence of uncontrolled systemic disease.
  • Mandatory preoperative PSG is not recommended (inadequate evidence) — most surgical patients are undiagnosed and lack time for formal testing.
Do not delay: a high STOP-BANG score alone is not a reason to cancel. Optimise comorbidities and plan postoperative monitoring instead.
2 Diagnosis & Severity Grading
KEY RECOMMENDATION OSA should be diagnosed using polysomnography (PSG) level 1, 2 or 3. PSG should be performed within 6 months of initial consultation. Scoring by trained personnel; interpretation verified by a trained specialist.

Sleep study levels

LevelDescriptionChannelsRole
1Full attended PSG≥ 7 (EEG, EOG, EMG, ECG, airflow, effort, SpO₂)Diagnostic (gold standard)
2Full unattended PSG≥ 7Diagnostic
3Portable monitor / HSAT (partial PSG)≥ 4 (effort, airflow, HR/ECG, SpO₂)Diagnostic (uncomplicated cases)
4Continuous single/dual bio-parameter≥ 1 (SpO₂ ± effort)Screening only

Caveat: portable monitors use apnoeas + hypopnoeas / total recording time (vs total sleep time for PSG) and therefore tend to underestimate severity. They cannot assess sleep quality or non-respiratory sleep disorders.

Level 1 PSG (attended) is indicated in: significant cardiorespiratory disease · potential respiratory muscle weakness (neuromuscular disease) · awake hypoventilation or suspected sleep-related hypoventilation · chronic opioid use · history of smoking · severe insomnia.

A second PSG may be considered if the first is negative but clinical suspicion persists.

Diagnostic criteria (ICSD-3)

  • AHI ≥ 5/hr with typical symptoms or associated medical/psychiatric disorder, OR
  • AHI ≥ 15/hr even in the absence of symptoms.

Paediatric diagnosis: symptoms plus AHI ≥ 2 or obstructive apnoea index ≥ 1, or AHI ≥ 1 (including central events).

3 Preoperative Management
KEY RECOMMENDATION Patients at risk of OSA should be screened preoperatively. The preferred screening tool is STOP-BANG.

Risk quantification

  • OSA is an independent risk factor for difficult intubation (OR 3.46, 95% CI 2.32–5.16) and difficult mask ventilation (OR 3.39, 95% CI 2.74–4.18) compared with non-OSA patients.
  • No significant difference in supraglottic airway failure rates between OSA and non-OSA.
  • Known or suspected OSA should be treated as an independent risk factor for difficult intubation, difficult mask ventilation, or both — prepare accordingly.

Preoperative checklist

  • Screen with STOP-BANG (or P-SAP / Berlin / ASA checklist).
  • Document known OSA, prior PAP settings and adherence, and last sleep study results.
  • Assess for uncontrolled systemic disease — the only clear reason to delay surgery.
  • Confirm availability of a PAP device (hospital-supplied or patient's own) for the perioperative period.
  • Consider difficult airway precautions and appropriate equipment.
  • Plan postoperative monitoring level and analgesia strategy (opioid-sparing) before induction.
Untreated OSA patients with optimised comorbidities may proceed to surgery, provided strategies to minimise postoperative complications are implemented.

Paediatric preoperative assessment

  • Detailed medical history: screening questionnaire, review of records, clinical risk factors, comorbidities, targeted physical examination.
  • Document from PSG: severity, lowest and duration of oxygen desaturation, CO₂ measurement.
  • Lowest oxygen desaturation < 70% → refer for further cardiopulmonary assessment.
  • Sedative premedication with caution; if given, monitor with continuous pulse oximetry.
4 Intraoperative Management

Anaesthetic technique — preferences

Agent / techniqueRecommendationRationale
Regional anaesthesiaPreferred over general anaesthesia where applicableReduced incidence of major complications; fewer adverse respiratory events
TIVAPreferred over volatile anaesthesiaSignificantly reduced PACU recovery time across OSA severity; recovery time increased with severity on volatile agents
IV benzodiazepinesCautionIncreased risk of adverse respiratory events during sedation
Opioids / propofol (procedural sedation)CautionIncreased risk of adverse respiratory events
Neuromuscular blocking agentsCautionIncreased risk of residual blockade, hypoxaemia, respiratory failure. Insufficient evidence to prefer any specific reversal agent
Inhalational agentsNo specific recommendationLack of evidence on residual effects

Short-acting vs standard agents

A randomised triple-blind trial found no difference between desflurane–remifentanil and sevoflurane–fentanyl in mean supine AHI on the first and third postoperative nights. However, the prevalence of severe OSA on the third postoperative night was significantly higher than preoperatively (OR 7.00, 95% CI 2.07–23.60) — suggesting monitoring should continue for at least three postoperative nights.

Airway management

  • Anticipate and prepare for difficult mask ventilation and difficult intubation.
  • Ensure availability of: fibreoptic bronchoscope, video laryngoscope, difficult airway cart.
  • Adequate preoxygenation; consider ramped position in obese patients.
  • Awake extubation is advocated in the paediatric OSA population.
  • Opioid-sparing technique preferred.

Sedation outside the operating theatre

In bariatric patients undergoing pre-operative oesophagogastroduodenoscopy, use of a sealed nasal positive airway pressure mask (vs passive oxygenating device) significantly reduced desaturation events and produced a higher median lowest SpO₂ — despite these patients having higher BMI, higher ASA class and greater likelihood of OSA.

Key intraoperative summary

  • Regional > general anaesthesia, where applicable.
  • TIVA > volatile anaesthesia.
  • IV benzodiazepine with caution during sedation.
  • OSA is associated with difficult mask ventilation and intubation — plan the airway.
  • Extubate awake; consider continued PAP/BiPAP postoperatively.
5 Postoperative & ICU Monitoring
KEY RECOMMENDATION Patients with, or suspected of having, a high risk of OSA should be monitored closely postoperatively. Hospitals should have suitable PAP devices available for perioperative use, or ensure patients bring their own device.

Quantified postoperative risk

OSA vs non-OSA surgical patients
OutcomeEffect size
Postoperative respiratory failureOR 2.42 (95% CI 1.53–3.84)
Postoperative cardiac eventsOR 1.63 (95% CI 1.16–2.29)
Unplanned transfer to ICUOR 2.46 (95% CI 1.29–4.68)
Opioid-induced respiratory depression (OIRD)OR 1.4 (95% CI 1.2–1.7)
Higher morphine-equivalent daily dose in OIRDMD 2.8 (95% CI 0.4–5.3)
Postoperative complications (high-risk STOP-BANG)OR 3.93 (95% CrI 1.85–7.77)
Length of hospital stay (high-risk STOP-BANG)MD +2.01 days (95% CrI 0.77–3.24)

PACU findings

  • Adverse respiratory events: 39% in high-risk (STOP-BANG > 3) vs 10% in low-risk (p < 0.001).
  • Inability to breathe deeply: 34% vs 9% (p = 0.001).
  • Median PACU stay: 120 min vs 99 min (p = 0.035).
  • High-risk patients presented with mild–moderate hypoxaemia.
85% of opioid-induced respiratory depression occurs within the first 24 postoperative hours. Patients with pre-existing cardiac or respiratory disease, or OSA, are at increased risk.

Postoperative care bundle

  • PAP device: hospital should supply, or the patient brings their own.
  • Patients should wear their PAP device at appropriate times during their hospital stay — both preoperatively and postoperatively.
  • Continue BiPAP/CPAP at preoperative settings in the paediatric population.
  • Continuous monitoring strongly recommended in OSA patients — general or critical care ward.
  • Do not discharge to an unmonitored setting after anaesthesia recovery until the patient is no longer at risk of postoperative respiratory complications.
  • Analgesia: NSAIDs and paracetamol; opioid-sparing. Consider local peripheral nerve blocks and central neuraxial anaesthesia when indicated.
  • Dexmedetomidine is associated with an overall perioperative opioid-sparing effect in children undergoing adenotonsillectomy.

Suggested critical care admission criteria (paediatric, CPG consensus)

FactorCriteria
PatientNeuromuscular disorder · craniofacial anomalies · requirement of oxygen preoperatively
SurgicalSurgery near or above the diaphragm
AnaestheticPostoperative IV narcotic or patient-controlled analgesia

Follow-up after upper airway surgery

  • A follow-up PSG may be considered 3 months after surgery for reassessment.
  • Surgical success = postoperative AHI reduction ≥ 50% and/or AHI < 20/hr.
  • Surgical cure = AHI < 5/hr after any sleep surgery.
  • Assess clinically on a periodic basis — OSA may recur postoperatively.
  • Repeat PSG/HSAT may be considered after a 10–20% change in weight, or after at least 3 months of recovery following weight-loss surgery.
6 PAP Therapy, CPAP/BiPAP & Ventilation Notes

Core principles

  • PAP is the gold standard and primary therapy across the spectrum of OSA severity.
  • Two types: CPAP and BiPAP; both may be automated (APAP) or fixed-pressure.
  • Nasal mask is the preferred interface.
  • PAP should be offered upon diagnosis, especially in moderate to severe OSA.

When to switch CPAP → BiPAP

  • Patient complains of being uncomfortable or intolerant to high CPAP pressures, or
  • CPAP level is ≥ 15 cm H₂O and respiratory disturbances continue.

APAP vs fixed CPAP

  • APAP increased machine usage by approximately 13 minutes/night (MD 0.21 hr/night, 95% CI 0.11–0.31).
  • Small reduction in ESS (MD −0.44 units, 95% CI −0.72 to −0.16) — not clinically significant.
  • Fixed CPAP achieved slightly lower AHI (MD 0.48/hr, 95% CI 0.16–0.80).
  • Adverse events: nasal blockage, dry mouth, pressure intolerance, mask leak.

Adherence interventions

InterventionIncrease in CPAP usage
Behavioural+1.31 hr/night (95% CI 0.95–1.66)
Educational+0.85 hr/night (95% CI 0.32–1.39)
Mixed+0.82 hr/night (95% CI 0.20–1.43)
Supportive+0.70 hr/night (95% CI 0.36–1.05)

Telemonitoring has also been shown to improve adherence.

Cardiometabolic effects relevant to the intensivist

  • Resistant hypertension: CPAP reduced 24-hour SBP by MD −5.06 mmHg (95% CI −7.89 to −2.13) and 24-hour DBP by MD −4.21 mmHg (95% CI −6.5 to −1.93).
  • Arterial stiffness: improved pulse wave velocity (MD −0.44, 95% CI −0.76 to −0.12).
  • Stroke prevention: benefit seen with good adherence (> 4 hours and ≥ 70% of usage days) in moderate to severe OSA (OR 0.530 and 0.505 respectively).
  • CPAP did not improve major adverse cardiovascular events overall; interpretation limited by risk of bias.
  • Depression: significant improvement in psychological symptoms, particularly with > 4 hours/night use.

Obesity Hypoventilation Syndrome (OHS)

Definition: obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) + daytime hypercapnia (PaCO₂ ≥ 45 mmHg) + sleep-disordered breathing + exclusion of alternative causes of alveolar hypoventilation.

Approximately 90% of patients with OHS have OSA. OHS is typically diagnosed during an episode of acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure.
KEY RECOMMENDATION CPAP is the preferred initial treatment in stable ambulatory patients with OHS and severe OSA. CPAP was conditionally recommended over NIV: similar effectiveness, less costly, fewer resources.

No significant difference between NIV and CPAP in mortality, resolution of hypercapnia, PaO₂ improvement, supplemental oxygen requirement, AHI change or daytime sleepiness.

Complications to monitor on PAP

  • Nasal bridge pressure sores
  • Oronasal dryness
  • Eye irritation / discomfort
  • Flattening of the midface (long-term interface use on growing facial structures in children)
  • Mask interface and humidification issues
7 Paediatric Perioperative Care
KEY RECOMMENDATION Perioperative assessment should be thorough in children at high risk of, or diagnosed with, OSA. Paediatric patients at increased perioperative risk from OSA should be closely monitored following recovery from anaesthesia.

Screening & diagnosis

  • Paediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ) is the screening tool of choice. Score ≥ 8 indicates sleep-related breathing disorder.
  • PSQ sensitivity: 73% (mild), 80% (moderate), 89% (severe). Validated in English and Malay.
  • PSG level 1 is the diagnostic gold standard — use paediatric scoring criteria.
  • If level 1 PSG is unavailable, overnight pulse oximetry may be used as an alternative.

Oximetry thresholds suggesting moderate-to-severe OSA: ≥ 3 clusters of desaturation events and ≥ 3 SpO₂ drops below 90%; ODI₄ > 2 episodes/hr with symptoms (PPV 84% for AHI > 1/hr). Technical requirements: sufficient recording capacity and short averaging time (< 3 seconds at HR 80 bpm).

High-risk groups to screen

Obesity · prematurity · adenotonsillar hypertrophy (and family history) · nasal septum deviation · allergic rhinitis · craniofacial anomalies · midface hypoplasia in craniosynostosis syndromes · mandibular hypoplasia ± cleft palate · neuromuscular disorder · achondroplasia · Chiari malformation · Down syndrome · Ehlers-Danlos syndrome · mucopolysaccharidoses · Prader-Willi syndrome.

Perioperative management

PhaseKey actions
Preoperative Detailed history + screening questionnaire + targeted examination · document PSG severity, lowest and duration of desaturation, CO₂ · lowest SpO₂ < 70% → cardiopulmonary assessment · sedative premedication with caution + continuous pulse oximetry
Intraoperative No evidence/consensus for a specific technique · difficult airway adjuncts available (fibreoptic bronchoscope, video laryngoscope, difficult airway cart) · opioid-sparing technique preferred · awake extubation advocated
Postoperative Continuous monitoring strongly recommended (general or critical care ward) · continue BiPAP/CPAP at preoperative settings · NSAIDs and paracetamol; consider peripheral nerve blocks and central neuraxial anaesthesia · dexmedetomidine is opioid-sparing · do not discharge to unmonitored setting until no longer at risk

Critical care admission considerations

  • Patient factors: neuromuscular disorder, craniofacial anomalies, preoperative oxygen requirement.
  • Surgical factors: surgery near or above the diaphragm.
  • Anaesthetic factors: postoperative IV narcotic or patient-controlled analgesia.

Treatment overview (for context)

  • Adenotonsillectomy (AT) is the treatment of choice for OSA due to adenotonsillar hypertrophy (~75% surgical success).
  • Post-AT residual disease → reassess upper airway and multilevel obstruction; consider lingual tonsillectomy, supraglottoplasty, epiglottopexy, tracheostomy.
  • CPAP is offered if persistent symptoms/signs after surgery, or surgery is contraindicated. Nasal CPAP is preferred; manage by experienced multidisciplinary clinicians.
  • Oral montelukast and/or nasal corticosteroids may be considered.

Follow-up

  • Monitor between 6 weeks and 12 months after each treatment intervention.
  • Assess symptoms, objective OSA severity, cardiovascular/CNS complications, quality of life, enuresis, growth rate.
  • Reassessment intervals: persistent SDB symptoms or at risk of persistent OSA → 26 weeks after AT; mild OSA on montelukast/nasal steroids → after 12 weeks; post-RME → 12 months (or earlier if symptoms persist).
  • Repeat PAP titration at least annually, or earlier if clinically indicated.
  • Mask fit must be assessed at least 6-monthly because children grow rapidly.
8 Special Groups

Pregnancy

  • Worldwide prevalence of OSA in pregnancy: 15% (95% CI 12–18); per trimester 15–19%.
  • Associated risks (OR): pregnancy-induced hypertension 1.97 · pre-eclampsia 2.35 · gestational diabetes 1.55 · pulmonary oedema 6.35 · Caesarean section 1.42 · postoperative wound complications 1.87 · preterm birth 1.62 · NICU admission 1.28.
  • Screening questionnaires perform poorly to fairly in pregnancy (Berlin: sensitivity 0.66 / specificity 0.62; ESS: 0.44 / 0.62). None accurately identify OSA.
  • Level 1 PSG is the gold standard, but is time-consuming, labour-intensive and costly. AASM home PSG guidance excludes pregnant women.
  • CPAP in pregnancy: a small RCT in gestational diabetes showed significantly lower rates of preterm delivery, unplanned Caesarean section and NICU admissions. A cohort study in hypertensive disorders showed reduced severe hypertension and improved lowest SpO₂ and ESS after one month.
KEY RECOMMENDATION Pregnant women with suspected OSA based on symptoms and signs should be referred for further management by a multidisciplinary specialist team.

Craniofacial anomalies

  • Pierre Robin Sequence (micrognathia, retrognathia, glossoptosis ± cleft lip/palate) carries a high OSA risk; obstruction can be severe and cause intermittent hypoxia with long-term cognitive impairment.
  • Almost 30% of children with syndromic cleft lip/palate screen positive for OSA.
  • Cephalometric risk markers: Class II skeletal pattern (reduced SNB, increased ANB), more vertical growth (increased MP-SN), reduced upper airway sagittal width (reduced PNS-AD1/PNS-AD2).
  • Treatment principle: secure a safe airway. Tracheostomy may be necessary in neonates/infants with severe obstruction until corrective surgery. Surgical options include mandibular distraction osteogenesis, AT and rapid maxillary expansion.
  • Non-surgical: nasal CPAP, oxygen therapy, proton pump inhibitors (if surgery not possible or in mild–moderate OSA).
KEY RECOMMENDATION Patients with OSA and craniofacial anomalies should be assessed for the benefit and risk of a surgical intervention.

Down syndrome

  • Prevalence of OSA: 45–82%.
  • Mechanisms: midfacial and mandibular hypoplasia with soft tissue crowding, adenotonsillar hypertrophy, relative macroglossia and glossoptosis, laryngomalacia, subglottic and tracheal stenosis.
  • Infants and children < 2 years tend to have more severe disease than those ≥ 2 years (p = 0.005). Of those monitored, 78% had moderate or severe OSA at some point.
  • AT is a mainstay; significant improvement in obstructive AHI and minimum SpO₂ — but almost half have persistent OSA postoperatively.
KEY RECOMMENDATION Patients with Down syndrome should be screened for OSA and treated accordingly.

Obesity hypoventilation syndrome

See PAP Therapy & Ventilation Notes — BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², PaCO₂ ≥ 45 mmHg, sleep-disordered breathing, exclusion of other causes. CPAP is the preferred initial treatment in stable ambulatory patients with severe OSA.

Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) — scoring

  • 8 situations scored 0–3 (0 = would never doze, 3 = high chance of dozing); total 0–24.
  • Score ≥ 11 = high risk (excessive daytime sleepiness).
  • Situations: sitting and reading · watching TV · sitting inactive in a public place · as a passenger in a car for an hour · lying down to rest in the afternoon · sitting and talking to someone · sitting quietly after lunch without alcohol · in a car stopped for a few minutes in traffic.
