Asthma — Quick Reference for Anaesthetists & Intensivists

01 Key principles — the 10-second version

Never SABA alone. Inhaled short-acting β₂-agonist (SABA) must not be used as monotherapy — it is always given with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS). Overuse (≥3 canisters/year) increases exacerbation and death risk.

  • All asthma patients should be on ICS-containing therapy — including in the peri-operative and ICU setting; do not stop maintenance ICS.
  • Assess control before stepping up or down treatment (use a validated tool: ACT, GINA or RCP-3 questionnaire).
  • Check inhaler technique and adherence at every visit and before escalating treatment — these are the commonest reversible causes of "uncontrolled" asthma.
  • Exacerbation: rapid severity assessment → initiate treatment immediately based on severity. Do not wait for investigations.
  • Systemic corticosteroids should be given in ALL patients with asthma exacerbation, as early as possible. Continue their maintenance ICS.
  • After every exacerbation: provide a written follow-up plan on discharge (appointment within 7 days, asthma action plan, adherence and inhaler technique review).
  • Smoking/vaping: strongly encourage quitting at every clinic visit.
Source: MOH/P/PAK/567.24(GU)-e, Clinical Practice Guidelines — Management of Asthma in Adults (Second Edition), 2024. Key Recommendations, p.1–2; Recommendation 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 14.
02 Diagnosis essentials (what confirms it)

There is no gold standard test. Diagnosis = typical clinical history + physical examination + evidence of variable and reversible airway obstruction.

TestThreshold / finding
Spirometry — obstructionFEV₁/FVC < 0.7 or < lower limit of normal (LLN)
Spirometry — reversibility↑ FEV₁ or FVC ≥12% AND ≥200 mL after bronchodilator or after 4 weeks on ICS
PEFR≥20% improvement after bronchodilator, or ≥20% from baseline after 4 weeks ICS; diurnal variability ≥20% over 2 weeks (twice-daily readings)
Bronchoprovocation not routineMethacholine PC₂₀ ≤ 8 mg/mL; mannitol ≥15% fall at ≤635 mg; exercise ≥10% fall in FEV₁
T2 markersBlood eosinophils ≥150 cells/µL or >4%; total IgE >100 kU/L; FeNO ≥50 ppb (suggests T2-high, predicts ICS response)

FeNO is not diagnostic. A low FeNO must never be used to withhold ICS. If a patient is already on treatment and shows no airflow variability, consider repeating spirometry after withholding bronchodilator (4 h for SABA, 24–48 h for LABA) or during symptoms.

Clinical clues: wheeze, cough, chest tightness, breathlessness; episodic/diurnal variation; triggered by viral colds, allergens, cold air, smoke, haze, strong smells, exhaust fumes; personal/family history of atopy.

Signs during an attack: tachypnoea, rhonchi, prolonged expiratory phase, decreased breath sounds, use of accessory muscles, audible wheeze. Examination may be normal between attacks.

Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Section 3 (Diagnosis), Tables 2 & 3, Figure 1, Recommendation 1, p.4–6.

05 Oxygen, HFNC, NIV & intubation
  • Target SpO₂ ≥94% in all hypoxic asthma patients. (In pregnancy: 94–98%.)
  • HFNC should be considered over conventional oxygen therapy (COT) in the ED — higher comfort score (MD 3, 95% CI 2–4), greater reduction in dyspnoea (MD 4.7, 95% CI 1.5–7.8), higher PaO₂ and lower HR/RR at 24–48 h in severe exacerbation with respiratory failure. Overall clinical response was not significantly different.
  • NIV — in a retrospective ICU cohort, NIV reduced risk of intubation (OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.32–0.40) and in-hospital mortality (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.40–0.58) versus no NIV.
  • Predictor of invasive mechanical ventilation: 1–2 asthma exacerbations in the past 12 months (OR 3.12, 95% CI 1.19–8.21).

Consider early intubation and critical care referral in life-threatening exacerbation with: respiratory distress, worsening hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, altered mental status, or failure to respond to maximal medical therapy.

Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Section 7.2 (i) Oxygen therapy, Recommendation 9, p.39–40.

07 Monitoring & evaluation during exacerbation
  • Monitor mental status, vital signs and respiratory assessment. Restlessness is an early sign of hypoxia.
  • SpO₂ <90% indicates the need for aggressive management. Assess saturation before oxygen is commenced, 5 min after oxygen is removed, or when stable.
  • ABG — not routine. Indicated if PEFR <50%, or no response to initial treatment, or deterioration. PaO₂ <60 mmHg with normal or raised PaCO₂ (especially >45 mmHg) indicates respiratory failure.
  • CXR — consider if not responding, or if an alternative diagnosis/complication is suspected (pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, pneumonia).

Frequency varies with severity, response to treatment and institutional protocol. Severe exacerbations often require close, continuous observation until stabilised.

08 Admission, critical care & discharge criteria

Admit

  • All patients with progressive deterioration following initial treatment.
  • All patients with severe or life-threatening asthma.
  • Consider also: older age; >8 SABA puffs in the previous 24 h; past severe exacerbations (intubation, asthma admissions); past unscheduled clinic/ED visits requiring OCS; living alone / socially isolated; psychological problems; physical disability.
  • Early referral to the critical care team for patients responding poorly to optimal treatment and at risk of respiratory failure.

Discharge

  • No specific parameter assures safe discharge — it is a clinical decision. Patients may be discharged if they can follow prescribed treatment at home, have few symptoms, and need less reliever medication.
  • Arrange a follow-up appointment before discharge from the ED; the follow-up plan should include a referral letter for review within 7 days.
  • Discuss medication adherence, correct inhaler technique, and provide a written asthma action plan.

Following asthma exacerbation, ALL patients should be given a follow-up plan upon discharge.

Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Sections 7.4 & 7.5, Recommendation 14, p.42–43.
09 Severe asthma, biologics & long-term OCS

Severe asthma affects ~5–10% of asthma patients; up to 40% rely on maintenance or frequent systemic corticosteroids, with serious adverse effects.

Definitions (quick)

TermMeaning
Uncontrolled asthmaPoor symptom control in the past 4 weeks (≥3 of: daytime symptoms >2×/week, reliever >2×/week, activity limitation, night waking) and/or frequent exacerbations (≥2/year requiring OCS and/or ≥1/year requiring hospitalisation)
Difficult-to-treat asthmaUncontrolled despite medium- or high-dose ICS + a second maintenance agent (usually LABA) or maintenance OCS; or requires high-dose treatment to maintain control. Modifiable factors: incorrect inhaler technique, poor adherence, smoking, co-morbidities; may be misdiagnosis or an asthma mimicker
Severe asthmaA subset of difficult-to-treat asthma: uncontrolled despite adherence to maximal optimised high-dose ICS-LABA and management of contributory factors, or worsening control when high-dose treatment is reduced

Relationship of asthma phenotypes

Treatment

  • Systemic corticosteroids (long-term): last therapeutic option; use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration. Exposure to long-term and repeated acute courses (>4 lifetime OCS courses) carries high risk of osteoporosis, fracture, pneumonia, heart failure, cardio-/cerebrovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, depression/anxiety, cataract, renal impairment and weight gain.
  • Biologics: add-on treatment after optimising therapy. Phenotype assessment is mandatory before selection. Approved agents: anti-IgE (omalizumab), anti-IL-5/5R (mepolizumab, reslizumab, benralizumab), anti-IL-4Rα (dupilumab), anti-TSLP (tezepelumab). They reduce exacerbations, hospitalisation/ED visits, improve lung function, asthma control and QoL, and reduce OCS use — especially with blood eosinophils ≥300 cells/µL.
  • Azithromycin: 500 mg three times weekly or 250 mg daily, up to one year (longer per specialist). Check for non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection before starting; consider ECG if long-QTc medications or risk factors, and audiogram if hearing loss risk.
  • Refer to a respiratory physician and discuss within a multidisciplinary team for persistent uncontrolled asthma on high-dose ICS-LABA-LAMA triple therapy.

Risk factors for severe asthma

Incompletely reversible airflow limitation · prone to exacerbation · neutrophilic airway inflammation · obesity · vocal cord dysfunction · obstructive sleep apnoea · depression · systemic inflammation · GERD · inhaler device polypharmacy · Aspergillus sensitisation. T2 inflammation is present in ~95% of severe asthma.

Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Section 8.1 Severe Asthma, Figure 3, Recommendation 15, p.44–49; Appendix 10 (biologic selection).
10 Pregnancy, labour & breastfeeding
  • Treat the same as non-pregnant patients — both in the stable state and during exacerbation. Bronchoprovocation tests are contraindicated in pregnancy.
  • ICS-containing therapy should be initiated and continued if already in use. Inhaled β₂-agonists and LTRAs are safe and should be continued.
  • Do not step down treatment during pregnancy — defer until after delivery.
  • Monitor every 4–6 weeks. Pregnant women are particularly susceptible to exacerbation, especially in the second trimester. Poor control and exacerbations increase risk of pre-eclampsia, preterm delivery, low birth weight and perinatal mortality.
  • Exacerbation treatment is the same as in non-pregnant patients: prompt bronchodilators, oxygen and systemic corticosteroids to avoid foetal hypoxia. Magnesium sulphate may be given if indicated. Target SpO₂ 94–98%. Consider continuous foetal monitoring. Early referral to critical care for moderate-to-severe exacerbations.

Labour & anaesthesia

  • Continue the maintenance inhaler; reliever as needed.
  • Regional blockade is preferred for patients requiring anaesthesia.
  • If on prednisolone >7.5 mg/day for more than 2 weeks prior to delivery: give IV hydrocortisone 100 mg every 6–8 hours during labour to prevent adrenal insufficiency.
  • Use prostaglandin F2α with caution — risk of bronchoconstriction.

Breastfeeding: encourage; continue asthma medications during lactation.

Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Section 8.2 Pregnancy and Breastfeeding, Recommendation 16, p.49–51.

13 COVID-19, nebulisers & aerosol precautions
  • Patients with asthma are not at increased risk of COVID-19; risk of COVID-19-related death is increased only in severe asthma or those on long-term OCS.
  • Continue ICS-containing medications during COVID-19 infection.
  • Nebulisers can transmit respiratory viral particles, but published literature was inconclusive or did not substantiate a direct relationship. If clinically indicated, nebuliser use should not be discouraged — especially in life-threatening asthma — with adherence to recommended safety measures.
  • pMDI + spacer is an alternative to nebulisers. Use single-patient use, or autoclave if the manufacturer permits. Homemade/custom-made spacers (e.g. plastic bottles) are not equivalent replacements — variable size, shape and electrostatic charge; lower emitted dose fractions.
  • Asthma in COVID-19 patients is treated the same as in non-COVID-19 patients.

Drug interaction: ritonavir-boosted nirmatrelvir (Paxlovid) with ICS-salmeterol or ICS-vilanterol can cause increased cardiac toxicity of the LABA. Assess for interactions.

Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Section 8.6 Asthma and COVID-19, p.56–57.
14 Referral criteria

Refer to a respiratory physician or a physician experienced in asthma management when there is:

  • Difficulty confirming the diagnosis of asthma
  • Severe or uncontrolled asthma
  • Severe or life-threatening asthma exacerbation
  • Asthma in pregnancy
  • Frequent unscheduled healthcare visits for exacerbations
  • Frequent bursts or ongoing use of OCS
  • Asthma with multiple co-morbidities
  • Asthma with concurrent food allergy or anaphylaxis
  • Suspected occupational asthma

Occupational asthma: ask all working-age adults with new or worsening asthma symptoms, reappearance of childhood asthma, or unexplained airflow obstruction about their occupation. Suspected cases → refer to a specialist; confirmed cases → notify the Department of Occupational Safety and Health (WEHU-L1 / JKKP7 form).

Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Section 9 Referral, p.58; Section 8.3 Occupational Asthma, Recommendation 17, p.51–53.

Risk factors for future exacerbation — screen at every review

  • Previous severe exacerbation requiring systemic corticosteroids or hospitalisation in the past year
  • SABA overuse (≥3 canisters/year)
  • Inadequate ICS use or not on ICS; poor adherence to maintenance therapy
  • Incorrect inhaler technique
  • Current smoker, e-cigarette or vape user
  • Co-morbidities: obesity, GERD, chronic rhinosinusitis, confirmed food allergy
  • Pregnancy
  • Type-2 inflammatory markers: high FeNO, blood eosinophilia
  • Lung function: low FEV₁ (especially <60% predicted), high bronchodilator responsiveness

16 Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB)
  • Defined as acute, transient and reversible airway narrowing occurring during or after exercise; occurs in patients with and without chronic asthma.
  • Diagnosis: post-exercise fall in FEV₁ >10%, measured 30 minutes after exercise cessation. Alternative: indirect bronchoprovocation (e.g. mannitol challenge).
  • Patients with well-controlled EIB can exercise safely. Advise gradual increase in warm-up intensity before sport.
  • Reliever: SABA (in non-asthmatic EIB), or ICS-SABA, or low-dose ICS-formoterol as needed prior to exercise, with a regular daily ICS regimen to reduce EIB. Recommendations for LTRA in EIB are inconsistent.
  • Patients with EIB whose asthma is not well controlled may experience symptoms — avoiding physical activity is not necessary with proper treatment.
Source: CPG Management of Asthma in Adults (2nd Ed) 2024, Section 8.4 Exercise-induced Bronchoconstriction, p.53–54.
17 Peak expiratory flow variability — how to calculate

Use the same PEF meter morning and evening at the same time of day; three readings each time, record the highest. Record for 1–2 weeks.

Diurnal PEF variability (%) = (day's highest − day's lowest) ÷ (mean of day's highest and lowest) × 100

Then average the daily values over the recording period. Diurnal PEF variability ≥20% in adults is significant (suggestive of asthma).
