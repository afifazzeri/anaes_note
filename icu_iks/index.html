Quick reference — anaesthetist & intensivist

1 · Bottom line — what you must know in 30 seconds Start here
Definition Invasive Klebsiella syndrome (IKS) = severe, metastatic infection caused by hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hVKp). It spreads to liver, lungs, eyes and brain, and can occur in previously healthy or hospitalised patients.
  • Suspect it in any diabetic or septic patient with cavitary lung lesions, liver abscess, prostate abscess, endogenous endophthalmitis or meningitis — especially with more than one focus.
  • Cultures are not enough. A negative string test does not exclude hVKp — this patient's string test was negative and PCR still confirmed a hypervirulent isolate.
  • Treatment is long. Source control + weeks to months of antibiotics. This case needed 12 weeks of total effective therapy before radiological resolution.
  • Most hVKp are still susceptible to routine agents (inherent ampicillin resistance only) — but MDR-hVKp is emerging and limits options.
  • Expect the ICU. Sepsis, cavitary pneumonia, pleural effusion, pulmonary embolism, and possible metastatic foci.
Diabetes = major risk Metastatic spread in 8.7–17% Mortality ≈ 10% Prolonged antibiotics Drain every collection
2 · Who is at risk — screening triggers
  • Diabetes mellitus — present in 29.3–44.3% of K. pneumoniae liver abscess cases. Poor glycaemic control impairs neutrophil phagocytosis of K1/K2 strains.
  • Sepsis with cavitary pneumonia / lung abscess — the presentation in this case.
  • Liver abscess — the classic IKS phenotype (78.2% of liver abscesses in a Korean series were K. pneumoniae).
  • Endogenous endophthalmitis — K. pneumoniae was the commonest pathogen (32.5%) in a local series.
  • Prostate abscess — rare but strongly associated with K. pneumoniae in diabetic patients in Taiwan.
  • Meningitis, septic pulmonary emboli, empyema — other metastatic manifestations.
  • Geography — emerging infectious disease, particularly in the Asia region (Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia).
Remember About one-third of metastatic infections are already present at admission, and most are diagnosed within 3 days of first presentation. Do not delay imaging.
3 · Diagnostics — what to send and how to interpret it

Microbiology

  • Blood cultures before antibiotics — in this case K. pneumoniae grew after 48 hours of incubation.
  • String test (hypermucoviscosity): stretch a colony with a loop/needle — a viscous string > 5 mm is positive. Simple, but imperfect sensitivity.
  • PCR for virulence genes — the definitive discriminator between hVKp and classical K. pneumoniae (cKP):

Pitfall A negative string test does not rule out hypervirulence. Confirm with PCR for rmpA/rmpA2, iroD, peg and capsular typing.

Laboratory clues from this case

  • CRP 96.7 mg/L (normal < 5) — markedly elevated.
  • WBC 6.7 × 10⁹/L — normal. A normal white count does not exclude severe invasive disease.
  • Pleural fluid: exudative (protein 54 g/L, LDH 839 U/L, fluid : serum LDH 2.0, protein 0.8) — but culture-negative with only a few pus cells and no organisms on Gram stain.
4 · Metastatic survey — imaging & review checklist
  • CT thorax / abdomen / pelvis with contrast — defines the primary focus and metastatic deposits (this case: bilateral cavitary pneumonia with a dominant right upper lobe nodule).
  • Bedside / formal ultrasound — pleural collections and hepatic abscesses; guides drainage.
  • Ophthalmological review — endogenous endophthalmitis is sight-threatening and easily missed in a sedated patient.
  • Neurological assessment / neuroimaging — if meningitis is suspected.
  • Prostate imaging in men with urinary symptoms or persistent sepsis.
  • Repeat imaging at the end of therapy to confirm resolution before stopping antibiotics.
Team A multidisciplinary approach — infectious diseases, microbiology, radiology, and ICU / anaesthesia — improves outcomes.

Practical rules

  • Start prompt, appropriate antimicrobials after cultures — do not wait for molecular results in a septic patient.
  • De-escalate once susceptibilities are known; hVKp is usually susceptible to common agents apart from inherent ampicillin resistance.
  • Prolonged therapy is the norm — both IV and oral phases. Short courses risk relapse and incomplete resolution.
  • Plan early for OPAT if the patient is otherwise stable — this patient completed 2 weeks of IV ceftriaxone as an outpatient.
  • MDR / hVKp: optimal therapy is unclear. Combination regimens (high-dose meropenem, colistin, fosfomycin, tigecycline, aminoglycosides) have been used with suboptimal results — involve ID early.
6 · Source control — drainage & monitoring
  • Drain every accessible collection — pleural, hepatic, prostatic. In this case an ultrasound-guided pigtail catheter was inserted for the pleural effusion.
  • Remove the drain when output is minimal (here, after one week).
  • Check drain patency and air leak before and during positive-pressure ventilation — a cavitary lesion or drained collection may behave as a bronchopleural fistula.
  • Repeat cross-sectional imaging to document resolution (this case: 4 weeks → smaller thick-walled cavity; 12 weeks → resolved).
High index of suspicion Management requires prompt antimicrobials plus thorough investigation to identify and drain abscesses or other foci. Imaging and image-guided intervention are central, not optional.
7 · Anaesthetic considerations Theatre
Please read These points are extrapolated from this case report plus general anaesthetic/ICU principles. They are not a substitute for local protocols, senior opinion, or ID/microbiology advice.

Pre-operative

  • Resuscitate first. Confirm sepsis bundle compliance, fluid status, lactate trend, vasopressor requirement before induction.
  • Glycaemic control — hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophil phagocytosis of K1/K2 K. pneumoniae. Aim for reasonable perioperative control; avoid both hyper- and hypoglycaemia.
  • Review cultures and sensitivities and confirm the current antimicrobial agent, dose and timing — ensure the intra-operative dose is not missed.
  • Anticoagulation — this patient received oral anticoagulants for 3 months for pulmonary embolism. Plan neuraxial/regional timing and holding decisions accordingly; check renal function and last dose.
  • Assess right heart strain / PE burden if pulmonary embolism is present — exercise caution with fluid loading and with sudden increases in pulmonary vascular resistance.

Airway & ventilation

  • Cavitary lung lesion — risk of rupture into the airway with haemorrhage or spillage; avoid unnecessarily high airway pressures and anticipate soiling.
  • Lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes; consider lung isolation (double-lumen tube or bronchial blocker) if surgery is on or near the affected lobe.
  • Pleural drain in situ — check patency and air leak; do not clamp during positive-pressure ventilation without a clear plan.
  • Bilateral disease (this patient had bilateral cavitary pneumonia) — expect reduced compliance and impaired gas exchange; pre-oxygenate thoroughly.

Induction & maintenance

  • Expect vasodilatation and myocardial depression; reduce induction doses and titrate. Have vasopressors drawn up and running before induction.
  • Prefer agents with haemodynamic stability in the shocked patient; avoid large boluses of propofol.
  • Maintain normothermia, normoglycaemia, and adequate perfusion.

Post-operative

  • HDU/ICU bed — these patients are at high risk of deterioration and of metastatic complications.
  • Continue antimicrobials on time; ensure no missed doses across the theatre/recovery handover.
  • Re-examine for new metastatic foci: visual symptoms, meningism, new focal neurology, new abdominal or prostatic symptoms.
8 · ICU management & monitoring
  • Sepsis bundle: cultures before antibiotics, lactate, fluid resuscitation, early vasopressors, source control.
  • Glycaemic control — a modifiable risk factor for severity; poorly controlled diabetes is associated with more complex infection.
  • Respiratory support — lung-protective ventilation; anticipate prolonged weaning with cavitary or bilateral disease.
  • VTE — pulmonary embolism occurred in this case; ensure appropriate therapeutic anticoagulation and monitor for right heart strain.
  • Serial imaging at planned intervals (this case: 4 weeks and 12 weeks) to confirm resolution before stopping antibiotics.
  • Antimicrobial stewardship — de-escalate on susceptibilities, then continue for the full prolonged course; plan OPAT early.
  • Screen for metastatic foci daily — eyes, CNS, abdomen, prostate.

Author's conclusion IKS is complex and requires comprehensive, timely management. The clinical presentation varies from pneumonia to intra-abdominal infection, so IKS should be considered — particularly in patients with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus presenting with sepsis. Further research is needed on pathogenesis and more effective therapeutic approaches.
