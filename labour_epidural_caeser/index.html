Conversion of Labour Epidural Analgesia to Surgical Anaesthesia for Emergency Intrapartum Caesarean Section

Bedside summary for anaesthetists & intensivists — risk factors, catheter checks, drug choice, block assessment, failed top-up management and follow-up.

Bottom line & key numbers
Bottom line: A labour epidural that is not working well enough for labour pain will not provide adequate surgical anaesthesia. Top up a good epidural as soon as it is safe to do so — but stay with the patient, assess bilaterally and cephalad, and be ready to induce general anaesthesia.
  • Decision-to-delivery (RCoA): Category 1 ≤ 30 min; Category 2 ≤ 75 min (for ≥90% of cases).
  • Conversion neuraxial → general anaesthesia (RCoA target): < 15% for Category 1; < 5% for Categories 1–3 overall.
  • Incidence of failed labour epidural conversion: 0–21%.
  • Scale: >100,000 emergency Caesarean deliveries in England (2017–18); 21% performed with epidural anaesthesia alone.
  • Decision-to-delivery: epidural top-up mean 19 min vs general anaesthesia 17 min — comparable.
  • Operating-room-to-incision: general anaesthesia 6 min vs epidural top-up 11 min — the longer interval did not produce inferior neonatal outcomes.

Risk factors for failed conversion

Consistent factors

  • Greater number of unscheduled epidural top-ups needed to maintain labour analgesia (OR 3.2)
  • Parturient-reported pain in the 2 h before Caesarean section
  • Management by a non-obstetric anaesthetist (OR 4.6)
  • Urgency of the Caesarean section (OR 40.4)

Inconsistent factors

  • Increased BMI or weight
  • Cervical dilatation at commencement of the labour epidural
  • Epidural rather than combined spinal-epidural (CSE) for labour
  • Increasing duration of epidural analgesia
Practical point: Breakthrough pain in labour may mark a poorly functioning epidural, but many epidurals that needed unscheduled boluses still work well when topped up for Caesarean section. Specialist obstetric anaesthetists may have lower failure rates because they replace problematic catheters before Caesarean section is needed.

Assessing a labour epidural before conversion
  • Careful assessment of epidural function is needed before deciding whether to top up.
  • Look for missed segments, unilateral blockade or inadequate sacral cover on objective testing.
  • An epidural judged inadequate for labour cannot be expected to provide surgical anaesthesia.
  • If the obstetrician is concerned about slow progress or the fetal heart rate tracing, the anaesthetist must re-evaluate epidural function in anticipation of needing to convert.
  • Inspect the epidural catheter in theatre to confirm it has not migrated since placement.

Category 1 Caesarean section — epidural or general?
  • General anaesthesia is consistently associated with a shorter decision-to-delivery interval than CSE, spinal or epidural conversion.
  • However, labour epidural conversion can achieve a comparable decision-to-delivery interval (mean 19 vs 17 min).
  • General anaesthesia carries many potential drawbacks for mother and neonate.
Authors' view: If a Category 1 Caesarean section is required, a labour epidural judged adequate for conversion should be topped up as soon as practically and safely possible in preference to general anaesthesia. Continue to evaluate for bilateral and progressively cephalad blockade — but be prepared and ready to induce general anaesthesia if adequate density and level are not obtained.

Confirming the correct location of the epidural catheter
Why it matters: Between 1970 and 1998 in the USA, almost one-fifth of maternal deaths associated with obstetric anaesthesia were related to epidural anaesthesia — many following bupivacaine 0.75% and caused by inadvertent high/total spinal blockade or local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST).

Aspiration

  • Gentle aspiration, looking for blood or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), identifies a misplaced catheter effectively and immediately.
  • Sensitivity 98%, specificity 100% — but false negatives occur.
  • In 2003 in the UK, only 34% of respondents with an interest in obstetric anaesthesia gave an epidural test dose for emergency Caesarean section.

Test dose

  • Defined as a small amount of local anaesthetic (LA), with or without adrenaline, to determine whether the catheter lies in a blood vessel or the subarachnoid space.
  • Controversial — many argue it is unnecessary because catheter position was confirmed in labour.
  • But multicompartmental block (distal orifice intravascular/subarachnoid while a proximal orifice remains epidural), arachnoid rupture after subdural placement and secondary catheter migration can still occur.
  • Balance the test dose against the delay incurred in establishing blockade.

Onset data

  • Intrathecal 2 mL lidocaine 1.5%: mean onset of objective block 1.5 min.
  • Epidural 3 mL lidocaine 1.5%: 9 min, with no detectable sensory blockade at 1.5 min.
  • If the test dose uses a slower-onset LA (e.g. levobupivacaine), excluding intrathecal placement takes longer than one is willing to wait.
  • Intrathecal 3 mL lidocaine 2% — unlike 3 mL bupivacaine 0.25–0.5% — reliably produces motor blockade.

Adrenaline marker

  • Epidural adrenaline 10–15 µg with lidocaine, given in uterine diastole.
  • Criterion: rise in maternal heart rate of ≥10 beats min⁻¹ within 1 min after injection.
  • Sensitivity up to 100%, specificity up to 96% when combined with clinical evaluation.
  • Not universally supported — in one study adrenaline with lidocaine did not compare favourably with simple aspiration.
  • I.V. adrenaline 10–15 µg is not harmful to the fetus.
Authors' view: Even with a well-functioning labour epidural, every epidural top-up dose must be considered a test dose, so that incremental dosing causes no harm even if the catheter is intrathecal or intravascular. Lidocaine 2% with or without adrenaline, fentanyl, or both, serves as both the test dose and the Caesarean top-up dose.

Where to initiate the conversion (delivery room vs theatre)
  • 2003 UK survey: 81% initiated extension of the block in the delivery room.
  • 2014 Scandinavian survey: 33% did so.
  • Delivery room argument: allows a test dose and reduces the decision-to-delivery interval during concurrent urgent transfer.
  • Theatre argument: better monitoring, and earlier identification of high/total blockade, hypotension and LAST.
Authors' practice: After negative aspiration, give the epidural test dose in the delivery room as soon as Caesarean section is decided (where possible), then transfer immediately for full conversion in theatre with safe monitoring and resuscitation facilities, once the test dose effect is evaluated.

For a Category 1 Caesarean section, a more aggressive strategy of epidural top-up with increased dosing in the delivery room may be suitable. The anaesthetist must stay with the patient at all times once an epidural top-up has started.

Evaluating the adequacy of neuraxial blockade
Gold standard: Loss of sensation to TOUCH up to and including T5 (between the nipple line and the xiphisternum) is the most reliable modality to prevent pain during Caesarean section.
Sensitivity 98%, specificity 53% (in the absence of neuraxial opioids) for discriminating effective from ineffective blockade.

Defining the level

  • Russell: the cephalad dermatomal level to touch = the first sensation of light touch.
  • Yentis: the point at which the sensation of touch no longer changes when the stimulus is applied yet more cranially.
  • These contrasting definitions cause confusion in interpretation.

Why T5 and not T10?

  • Afferent pain fibres from pelvic organs are thought to accompany sympathetic nerves and enter the spinal cord around T10–L1.
  • Blockade must be much higher than T10, so other non-conventional pathways must transmit Caesarean pain — possibly pelvic afferents following sympathetic nerves through intra-abdominal plexuses and the greater splanchnic nerve to reach the cord as high as T5, or visceral pain from other intra-abdominal structures entering at T5.

Other modalities — do not substitute

Cold12%Only 12% of parturients who experience pain will have loss of cold sensation below T4.
  • In 2010 in the UK, more than two-thirds of respondents who evaluated a single modality used loss of cold sensation to T4 — a poor discriminator.
  • Adequate anaesthesia means lack of awareness to the sensation — not merely absence of cold or sharp pinprick.
  • Loss of cold is found many dermatomes higher than loss of sharp pinprick, which in turn is several dermatomes higher than loss of touch — and the relationship is not constant within or between individuals. Testing one modality does not predict the level of another.

Caudal and motor assessment

  • Assess caudal spread from the lateral margin of the foot (S1) to the anterior leg and thigh (lumbar dermatomes).
  • Sacral segments must be blocked to prevent pain during pressure and traction on the lower uterus, cervix and vagina.
  • Motor block of the lower limbs is secondary to lumbar blockade — assess with the Bromage scale or straight leg raising.

Clinical management of a failed epidural top-up

Optimal management is controversial and best-practice guidelines are lacking. Every option has drawbacks and introduces risk to mother and neonate.

Unilateral sensory blockade

  • May be due to the catheter lying too lateral in the epidural space, or outside the space after passing through the intervertebral foramen.
  • Withdrawal of the catheter followed by additional LA in surgical concentrations was effective in >4/5 of failed conversions in a retrospective analysis.
  • But it can take a long time, causing delays, and further LA increases LAST risk.

Spinal after a failed top-up

  • Technically challenging — difficulty obtaining CSF, possibly due to collapse of the subarachnoid space below the termination of the cord from the volume effect of the epidural bolus.
  • Spinal anaesthesia within 30 min of a failed top-up is associated with increased failure risk — free flow of clear fluid may be previously injected LA in the epidural space, not CSF.
  • Increased likelihood of high or total spinal may relate to: pre-existing subclinical analgesia from prior epidural LA; compression of the dural sac by residual epidural LA causing cephalad displacement of intrathecal drug; and leakage of LA through the dural hole into the subarachnoid space.
  • Risk-reduction measures: perform the spinal sitting; reduce intrathecal bupivacaine dose by 20%; delay supine positioning after injection.
  • Caveat: dose reduction may not prevent high/total spinal and can increase later intraoperative block failure.

CSE instead of spinal

  • Allows a decreased initial intrathecal LA dose with reduced risk of block failure, because additional LA can be given through the epidural catheter.
  • Concerns about the untested epidural catheter are not substantiated — the incidence of a failed epidural component after a successful CSE is unlikely.
  • Longer performance times than spinal reported, but only one trial showed a clinically meaningful difference of 11 min.
General anaesthesia risk: Accidental awareness under anaesthesia and complications related to aspiration and failed intubation — with critical incidents mainly occurring after failed conversion of neuraxial anaesthesia rather than primary general anaesthesia.

Recommendations to decrease the risk of failed conversion

Delivery room — before deciding on Caesarean section

  • Early recognition of poorly functioning epidural analgesia, giving the anaesthetist an opportunity to manipulate or replace the catheter.
  • If the obstetrician expresses concern about slow progress or the fetal heart rate tracing, re-evaluate how well the epidural is functioning in anticipation of needing to convert.

Operating theatre — after deciding on Caesarean section

  • Inspect the catheter to check it has not migrated since placement in labour.
  • If sufficient time is available, test function by giving one-quarter to one-third of the full LA dose, examining initially and then every 3–5 min for bilateral level and density of sensory blockade.
  • In the absence of definite evidence of bilateral, progressively cephalad blockade of adequate density, do not administer more than half of the full LA dose.

Follow-up
  • All women who receive intrapartum anaesthetic care — analgesia for labour or anaesthesia for operative procedures — should be followed up routinely.
  • Aims: determine general well-being; exclude complications such as epidural haematoma or postdural puncture headache (PDPH); monitor adverse effects such as pruritus or vomiting; obtain feedback on quality of analgesia and anaesthesia service.
  • Serious complications such as epidural abscess may not manifest clinically until after hospital discharge — verbal and written information must be provided on when and how to seek further advice and help.
  • Those with identified complications (e.g. PDPH) should be contacted daily and offered postpartum follow-up review as an outpatient with a consultant anaesthetist.

1The ideal Category 1 top-up

28-year-old, working labour epidural, no breakthrough pain, no missed segments. Sudden fetal bradycardia → Category 1 Caesarean section.

What is your plan?

Answer: Negative aspiration → lidocaine 2% + adrenaline (≈5 µg mL⁻¹) ± fentanyl 50–75 µg. This gives the fastest onset of surgical blockade (mean 4.5 min) and acts as both test and top-up dose. Give the test dose in the delivery room if possible, transfer immediately, then top up incrementally in theatre with full monitoring. Assess touch to T5 bilaterally and progressively. Stay with the patient. Be prepared to induce general anaesthesia.
2Three unscheduled boluses in 2 hours

Epidural required three unscheduled top-ups in the last 2 h. Now Category 2 for failure to progress.

How does this change your approach?

Answer: Two consistent risk factors are present — multiple unscheduled top-ups (OR 3.2) and pain in the 2 h before Caesarean section. Failure risk is high. If time permits, consider replacing the catheter or placing a CSE before/at the time of the decision. Test with ¼–⅓ of the full LA dose and reassess every 3–5 min; without definite bilateral cephalad block of adequate density, do not exceed half the full dose. Have a low threshold for general anaesthesia.
3Unilateral block after top-up

After the epidural top-up, blockade is dense on the right but absent on the left.

What are the options?

Answer: The catheter is likely too lateral in the epidural space or outside it via the intervertebral foramen. Withdrawal then re-dosing with surgical concentrations was effective in >4/5 of failed conversions in a retrospective analysis — but it takes time and increases LAST risk. Alternatives: CSE or spinal, or general anaesthesia if the situation is time-critical. Do not persist with an obviously one-sided block for a Category 1.
4Spinal 20 minutes after a failed top-up

Epidural top-up failed. Twenty minutes later you decide to perform a spinal.

What must you anticipate?

Answer: Spinal within 30 min of a failed top-up carries increased risk of block failure and high/total spinal. Free flow of clear fluid may be previously injected LA in the epidural space, not CSF — do not be falsely reassured. Perform the spinal sitting, reduce intrathecal bupivacaine by 20%, and delay supine positioning. Note that dose reduction may not prevent high block and can increase later intraoperative failure — a CSE may be a better option.
5Test dose — is it intravascular?

One minute after 3 mL lidocaine 2% via the epidural catheter, the patient reports a metallic taste and palpitations; heart rate has risen 20 beats min⁻¹.

What do you do?

Answer: Treat as a positive test dose / LAST prodrome. Stop injecting, re-aspirate, do not give further LA through the catheter, call for help, and manage per your LAST protocol (including lipid emulsion where indicated). Systemically, adrenaline 10–15 µg as a marker produces a rise in heart rate of ≥10 beats min⁻¹ within 1 min (sensitivity up to 100%, specificity up to 96%) — but the same rise can be caused by pain. Use clinical judgment and consider an alternative anaesthetic technique.
6Conflicting block levels

Assessment shows loss of cold sensation to T4, loss of sharp pinprick to T8, and loss of light touch only to T10.

Is this block adequate to start surgery?

Answer: No. Loss of sensation to touch must reach T5. Cold and pinprick are not substitutes or surrogate measures — cold has only 12% sensitivity and pinprick 55%. The dermatomal relationship between modalities is not constant within or between individuals, so a high cold level does not predict an adequate touch level. Top up further and reassess, or change technique.
7Speed is everything — bicarbonate?

Category 1 Caesarean section, good working epidural, experienced obstetric anaesthetist on site.

Is adding bicarbonate worth it?

Answer: Bicarbonate 8.4% (2 mL added to 20 mL lidocaine, then discard 2 mL of the mixture) reduces onset by a mean of 4.5 min. In familiar hands, the extra preparation time is <1 min; in unfamiliar hands it is 4× that of bupivacaine — and mixing drugs in an emergency carries error risk. Never add bicarbonate to bupivacaine, levobupivacaine or ropivacaine (precipitation).
8Ropivacaine 0.75% — when to choose it

Category 2 Caesarean section, predicted to be surgically difficult, epidural functioning well.

Is ropivacaine 0.75% a good choice?

Answer: Ropivacaine 0.75% produces the lowest need for intraoperative supplementation, which suits a longer or more difficult operation — but onset is slower than lidocaine 2% + adrenaline. Unlike in elective Caesarean section, adding fentanyl does not reduce supplementation in the emergency setting, possibly because labour epidural fentanyl has already produced a near-maximal effect.
9Non-obstetric anaesthetist on call

You are the general on-call anaesthetist covering obstetrics overnight. A Category 2 Caesarean section is called for a woman with a labour epidural.

What is the key risk factor and what should you do?

Answer: Management by a non-obstetric anaesthetist carries an OR of 4.6 for failed conversion (alongside urgency, OR 40.4). Involve a specialist obstetric anaesthetist early, re-evaluate epidural function before the decision, consider replacing a poorly functioning catheter before Caesarean section is needed, and be explicit about your threshold for general anaesthesia.
10Day 6 readmission after discharge

A woman discharged on day 2 after an epidural top-up for Caesarean section returns on day 6 with back pain and fever.

What is the lesson?

Answer: Serious complications such as epidural abscess may not manifest clinically until after hospital discharge. Every woman receiving intrapartum anaesthetic care must receive verbal and written information on when and how to seek further advice, and routine follow-up should look for haematoma, PDPH, pruritus and vomiting. Women with identified complications (e.g. PDPH) should be contacted daily and offered outpatient consultant anaesthetist review.
