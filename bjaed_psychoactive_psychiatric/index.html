Psychoactive Drugs

Anaesthetic & critical care implications — rapid reference

Perioperative • ICU • Emergency

Key Points
  • All commonly used psychoactive medications have the potential to cause dangerous interactions with drugs used routinely in anaesthesia.
  • There are very few evidence-based guidelines on the management of psychoactive drugs in the perioperative period.
  • Caution must be exercised if a psychoactive drug is stopped — risk of withdrawal symptoms and recurrence of the psychiatric illness.
  • Patients must have a plan in place for restarting their medications where appropriate.
  • Perioperative management should involve a multidisciplinary team.
~10% of the UK population has some form of mental health condition, yet guidance is scarce. Challenges arise from the psychiatric condition itself, drug interactions, and surgical problems (electrolyte derangement, prolonged fasting).

Classification: (i) antidepressants, (ii) mood stabilisers, (iii) antipsychotics, (iv) anxiolytics. Significant overlap exists — a drug may treat more than one disorder. Benzodiazepines are assumed knowledge and are not covered in detail here.

Antidepressants

Depression is thought to result from a deficiency of norepinephrine, serotonin, or both within the CNS. Drugs that increase these neurotransmitters take up to 2 weeks to produce clinical improvement — suggesting receptor number or sensitivity changes are also required.

Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)

  • Examples: amitriptyline, nortriptyline, dosulepin.
  • Uses: depression, chronic pain, some forms of acute pain.
  • Mechanism: prevent presynaptic re-uptake of norepinephrine and serotonin (uptake 1). Also have anti-muscarinic, antihistaminergic and anti-α₁-adrenergic effects.
  • Metabolism: hepatic, via cytochrome P450 — considerable inter-patient variation.
  • May cause sedation and reduce the seizure threshold.
  • Anticholinergic side-effects may be potentiated by other anticholinergic agents.
  • Cardiovascular: QRS widening and QT prolongation (particularly in overdose). Halothane-related arrhythmia concern is largely historical.
  • Postural hypotension may be significant in the elderly (α-receptor block).
TCA + tramadol → seizures and serotonergic crisis.
Potentiation of indirectly acting sympathomimetics (ephedrine, metaraminol) → hypertensive crisis. Avoid if possible; use directly acting sympathomimetics cautiously.
Avoid abrupt withdrawal — mainly cholinergic symptoms, plus relapse of the treated condition. The risk of stopping a stable psychoactive drug may outweigh the benefit; assess each case individually.

Selective Serotonin Re-uptake Inhibitors (SSRIs)

  • Now the most commonly used antidepressants — good efficacy with a preferable side-effect profile.
  • Mechanism: presynaptic serotonin re-uptake inhibition.
Serotonin syndrome when tramadol or meperidine is given with an SSRI — features hyperreflexia, agitation, hyperthermia.
  • Fluoxetine inhibits CYP2D6 → interferes with metabolism of codeine to morphine → patients given codeine may receive inadequate analgesia.
  • Increased bleeding risk with NSAIDs or warfarin (interference with platelet function).
  • Withdrawal: dizziness, gastrointestinal upset, and a variety of psychiatric symptoms.
  • Risks of continuing are relatively low → generally acceptable to continue throughout the perioperative period.

Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOIs)

  • Less frequently used, but the severity of interactions makes them important. Their use may indicate refractory illness — so withdrawal risk is greater.
  • MAO-A prefers norepinephrine and serotonin; MAO-B deaminates tyramine and phenylethylamine. Antagonism of MAO-A produces the antidepressant effect.
  • Irreversible: phenelzine, tranylcypromine, isocarboxazid (inhibit both MAO-A and -B) — duration of action weeks.
  • Reversible (RIMA): moclobemide — MAO-A specific, shorter duration, no washout needed before starting another antidepressant.
Profound pressor effect after both indirectly and directly acting sympathomimetics. Indirect agents have inhibited metabolism → potentiated action. Direct sympathomimetics are preferable (also metabolised by COMT, less MAO-dependent) but still require caution — hypertensive crisis risk remains.

Meperidine can precipitate a serotonergic crisis (interrupts presynaptic serotonin uptake) — avoid in known MAOI patients.
  • Elective: traditionally stop irreversible MAOIs 2 weeks before operation. Omitting the dose of moclobemide on the day of surgery is acceptable.
  • Consider switching from an irreversible MAOI to moclobemide to avoid a prolonged discontinuation period.
  • Emergency: no time for withdrawal — avoid meperidine and use only direct-acting sympathomimetics with extreme caution.
Mood Stabilisers

Lithium

  • Uses: mania, bipolar disorder, adjuvant for refractory depression, some pain conditions (e.g. cluster headache).
  • Mechanism: poorly understood; believed to mimic sodium in vivo, entering excitable cells during depolarisation → reduced neurotransmitter release in both CNS and PNS. May also be neuroprotective via inhibition of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor.
  • Narrow therapeutic ratio; excreted solely by the kidneys.
  • Side-effects: interference with ADH action, cardiac dysrhythmias, gastrointestinal disturbance, tremor.
  • Toxicity: lethargy or restlessness → ataxia, dysrhythmias, renal failure, coma. Toxic symptoms usually at plasma levels > 1.5 mmol/L.
Prolongs depolarizing neuromuscular block and reduces anaesthetic agent requirements.
NSAIDs reduce renal lithium excretion → toxic plasma levels.
Prudent to stop lithium at least 24 hours before surgery.

Carbamazepine & Valproate

More commonly used for epilepsy, but also as mood stabilisers and for pain management. They are not first-line for these indications — their use suggests more resistant illness and therefore an increased potential for recurrence if stopped.

Valproate

  • Anti-epileptic effect via inhibition of neuronal sodium channels and thalamic calcium channels.
  • Side-effects range from minor GI disturbance and platelet dysfunction to life-threatening idiosyncratic reactions.
  • Potential for increased perioperative bleeding due to platelet dysfunction.
  • No reports of adverse outcomes after neuraxial block.

Carbamazepine

  • Stabilises neuronal cells by blocking inactivated sodium channels.
  • Side-effects: syndrome of inappropriate ADH, hepatitis, life-threatening blood dyscrasias.
  • It is a hepatic cytochrome P450 inducer → reduces the effect of other drugs metabolised by that system.
  • In particular, the duration of effect of non-depolarizing aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers (e.g. vecuronium) is shortened.
Antipsychotics

Psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia impair the patient's ability to comprehend reality. The pathophysiology is poorly understood but is hypothesised to involve imbalances in dopaminergic pathways in the limbic system. Antipsychotic (neuroleptic) drugs are efficacious; they are classified as typical or atypical. These patients have a high relapse rate when medication is discontinued.

Typical Antipsychotics

  • Examples: prochlorperazine, chlorpromazine.
  • Block dopamine receptors in the limbic system and histamine, α₁-adrenergic and cholinergic receptors — hence the large number of side-effects.
  • Most troubling effects are from dopamine receptor blockade in the basal ganglia: akathisia, parkinsonian symptoms, tardive dyskinesia.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) — rare but significant; occurs with both typical and atypical agents. Characterised by hyperthermia, autonomic dysfunction and muscle rigidity. Differential diagnosis includes malignant hyperthermia. Can occur at any point in treatment. Mortality 20–30%. Treatment is supportive; dantrolene and non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking drugs have been used to reduce rigidity.
  • No well-established interactions with anaesthetic agents, although seizures have been reported with desflurane.
  • Anti-adrenergic and anti-cholinergic effects are unpredictable — use similar drugs with caution or avoid.
  • Avoid drugs with anti-dopaminergic effect, which could enhance side-effects of this class.

Atypical Antipsychotics

  • Examples: quetiapine, risperidone.
  • Still block dopamine, but differ by blocking subtypes of the dopaminergic receptor family (especially in the prefrontal cortex) and affecting the serotonin-2A receptor.
  • Result: significantly lower propensity for extrapyramidal side-effects at clinically relevant doses → now first-line for schizophrenia.
  • Important side-effects: NMS, postural hypotension (α₁-adrenergic block), weight gain.
  • Anaesthetic implications are as for typical antipsychotics.
Anxiolytics
  • Anxiety becomes a clinical disorder when too severe, too protracted, or too pervasive.
  • Benzodiazepines remain the mainstay of treatment, balanced against the risk of dependence, tolerance and overdose.
  • Anaesthetists should already be familiar with their actions and interactions.
  • Monitor for signs of withdrawal, particularly in patients who remain fasted for prolonged periods.
Other Anaesthetic Implications

Drug Withdrawal

  • Several psychoactive drugs in combination with perioperative drugs can cause dangerous effects — but the decision to discontinue should not be taken lightly.
  • For complex psychiatric illness, involve the patient's usual mental health support team.
  • Where drugs have been stopped for surgery, there must be a plan to restart them after operation.
  • Particular thought is needed for patients unable to take oral medication postoperatively — this could result in relapse.
  • Emergency anaesthesia does not allow a withdrawal period — awareness of potential drug interactions is especially important.

Regional & Local Anaesthesia

Local anaesthetic solutions containing epinephrine can cause hypertensive crises in patients receiving TCAs and MAOIs.
  • Valproate causes platelet dysfunction, but there are no reports of adverse outcomes after neuraxial block.

Consent

  • Consent requires that the person understands the information, retains it, uses it to make an informed decision, and can communicate that decision.
  • Where possible, efforts to reverse temporary incapacity must be made before any medical intervention.
  • Mental illness may impair decision-making — but this should not be assumed (even if treated under the Mental Health Act 1983).
  • The Mental Capacity Act 2005 allows patients who may predictably become incompetent to express a predetermined wish via an Advanced Decision or Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA).
  • Where incapacity is irreversible and there is no Advanced Decision or LPA, it is the anaesthetist's responsibility (as part of the multidisciplinary team) to treat the patient in their 'best interests'.
