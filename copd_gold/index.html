1 · Definition, Causes & Diagnostic Criteria

Definition

COPD is a heterogeneous lung condition characterised by chronic respiratory symptoms (dyspnoea, cough, sputum production and/or exacerbations) due to abnormalities of the airways (bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and/or alveoli (emphysema) that cause persistent, often progressive, airflow obstruction.

Causes & risk factors

COPD results from gene–environment interactions over the lifetime (GETomics) that can damage the lungs and/or alter normal development and ageing.

  • Tobacco smoking — including in utero and passive exposure; vaping/e-cigarettes; cannabis.
  • Biomass & pollution — household cooking/heating smoke, ambient air pollution, wildfire smoke, occupational dusts/vapours/fumes/gases.
  • Host factors — abnormal lung development, prematurity, low birthweight, accelerated lung ageing, childhood respiratory infections, asthma.
  • Genetics — alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) and other variants of smaller effect.

Diagnostic criteria

Diagnosis In the appropriate clinical context, the presence of non-fully reversible airflow obstruction (post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.7) measured by spirometry confirms the diagnosis of COPD. Pre-bronchodilator spirometry can be used to exclude COPD but not to confirm it.

Clinical indicators prompting spirometry (Figure 2.1)

  • Dyspnoea that is progressive over time, worse with exercise, persistent.
  • Recurrent wheeze.
  • Chronic cough — may be intermittent and non-productive.
  • Recurrent lower respiratory tract infections.
  • History of risk factors — tobacco, biomass smoke, occupational exposures, host factors.

These indicators are not diagnostic in themselves; multiple indicators increase probability, but spirometry is always required.

2 · Spirometry — Practical Technique & Interpretation

Preparation

  • Spirometers should produce a hard copy or digital display of the expiratory curve to detect technical errors, or give an automatic prompt identifying an unsatisfactory test and the reason.
  • The supervisor needs training in optimal technique and quality performance.
  • Maximal patient effort is required to avoid underestimation of values and errors in diagnosis and management.

Performance

  • Follow national and/or international recommendations.
  • Expiratory volume/time traces should be smooth and free from irregularities.
  • Pause between inspiration and expiration < 1 second.
  • Record long enough to reach a volume plateau — may take > 15 seconds in severe disease.
  • Both FVC and FEV₁ should be the largest value from any of three technically satisfactory curves; values should vary by no more than 5% or 150 mL (whichever greater).
  • FEV₁/FVC ratio is taken from the acceptable curve with the largest sum of FVC + FEV₁.

Bronchodilation

  • Possible protocols: 400 µg short-acting β₂-agonist, 160 µg short-acting anticholinergic, or both combined.
  • Measure FEV₁ 10–15 min after a SABA, or 30–45 min after a SAMA or combination.
  • Patients already on bronchodilator treatment who need spirometry for monitoring do not need to stop their regular treatment.

Evaluation

  • Compare with reference values based on age, height and sex.
  • Post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.7 confirms non-fully reversible airflow obstruction.

4 · Initial Assessment, Follow-up & Additional Investigations

Goals of the initial assessment

  • Determine the severity of airflow obstruction.
  • Assess the impact of current symptoms on the patient.
  • Assess the risk of future events — exacerbations, hospital admissions, death.
  • Use all of the above to guide therapy.

Monitoring & follow-up

  • Routine follow-up of lung function, symptoms and exacerbations is essential to determine when to modify management and to identify complications and/or comorbidities.
  • Virtual and hybrid virtual/in-person care models may offer improved access, outcomes and affordability, but use should be based on evidence.

Additional investigations

Consider in patients with a marked discordance between airflow obstruction and perceived symptoms:

  • Lung volumes
  • Diffusion capacity (DLCO)
  • Exercise testing
  • Lung imaging (chest X-ray, HRCT)

Comorbidities to actively seek

Cardiovascular disease, skeletal muscle dysfunction, metabolic syndrome, osteoporosis, depression, anxiety and lung cancer. These occur frequently, influence health status, hospitalisation and mortality independently of airflow obstruction severity, and must be treated appropriately when present.

Why COPD is under-diagnosed (Figure 2.8)

  • Patient-related: under-recognition/under-reporting of symptoms; adaptation of activities to minimise breathlessness; mild disease.
  • Healthcare system-related: poor access to diagnostic testing (spirometry); disparities in access even in high-income countries; lack of access to quality healthcare in many LMICs; lack of human resources and training programmes.
  • Healthcare provider-related: poor understanding of diagnostic criteria; inadequate training in spirometry use and interpretation; inadequate investigation and referral of patients with respiratory symptoms.
5 · Pharmacological Maintenance Treatment

Principles

  • Initial pharmacological treatment should be individualised and guided by severity of symptoms, risk of exacerbations, side-effects, comorbidities, drug availability and cost, patient preference and ability to use the delivery device.
  • Review after a suitable interval — shorter in more severe disease, longer in less severe disease — and reassess attainment of treatment goals and barriers to success.
  • Inhaler technique and adherence must be assessed regularly.

Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)

  • Regular ICS increases the risk of pneumonia, especially in severe disease. A
  • An ICS combined with a LABA is more effective than either component alone — but a LABA + ICS combination is not encouraged in COPD. If there is an indication for ICS, LABA + LAMA + ICS is superior to LABA + ICS and is the preferred choice.

  • If the patient has features of asthma, treatment should always contain an ICS.
  • Independent of ICS use, a blood eosinophil count < 2% increases pneumonia risk. C

Bronchodilator key statements

  • Inhaled bronchodilators are central to symptom management and are commonly given regularly to prevent or reduce symptoms. A
  • Inhaled bronchodilators are recommended over oral bronchodilators. A
  • Regular and as-needed SABA or SAMA improves FEV₁ and symptoms. A
  • SABA + SAMA combinations are superior to either alone. A
  • LABAs and LAMAs are preferred over short-acting agents except in patients with only occasional dyspnoea, and for immediate relief of symptoms in patients already on long-acting bronchodilators. A
  • LABAs and LAMAs significantly improve lung function, dyspnoea and health status and reduce exacerbation rates. A
  • LAMAs have a greater effect on exacerbation reduction than LABAs A and decrease hospitalisations B.
  • When initiating long-acting bronchodilators, the preferred choice is LABA + LAMA. A
  • Combination LABA + LAMA reduces exacerbations compared with monotherapy. B
  • Single-inhaler therapy may be more convenient and effective than multiple inhalers.
  • Theophylline exerts a small bronchodilator effect in stable COPD A with modest symptomatic benefit B.
6 · Inhaler Devices — Choosing & Checking Technique
Golden rule When a treatment is given by the inhaled route, the importance of education and training in inhaler technique cannot be over-emphasised. Assess technique and adherence before concluding that the current therapy is insufficient.
  • The choice of device must be individually tailored — depends on access, cost, prescriber and, most importantly, the patient's ability and preference.
  • Provide instructions and demonstrate the proper technique when prescribing; re-check at every visit.
  • Consider drug availability in the device, the patient's beliefs, satisfaction with current and previous devices, and preferences.
  • Minimise the number of different device types for each patient.
  • Do not switch device type without clinical justification or without proper information, education and medical follow-up.
  • Shared decision-making is the most appropriate strategy for device choice.
  • Take into account cognition, dexterity and strength.

Assessing ability to use each device type

Physicians should prescribe only devices they — and the rest of the caring team — know how to use.

7 · Non-Pharmacological Treatment, Oxygen & Long-Term NIV

Risk reduction, lifestyle & patient education

  • All individuals who smoke should be strongly encouraged and supported to quit. Nicotine replacement and pharmacotherapy reliably increase long-term abstinence. Legislative smoking bans and counselling improve quit rates.
  • There is no evidence to support the effectiveness and safety of e-cigarettes as a smoking cessation aid at present.
  • People with COPD should receive all recommended vaccinations according to local guidelines.

Pulmonary rehabilitation

Exercise training combined with disease-specific education improves exercise capacity, symptoms and quality of life across all GOLD grades. It is complementary to pharmacological treatment and should form part of comprehensive management.

8 · Exacerbations — Definition & Severity (Rome Proposal)

Definition

An exacerbation of COPD is an acute event with symptoms worsening over a few days (up to 14 days), characterised by increased dyspnoea and/or cough and sputum that may be accompanied by tachypnoea and/or tachycardia. Exacerbations are often associated with increased local and systemic inflammation caused by airway infection, pollution or other insults to the lungs.

Causes & mimics

  • Most frequently caused by infections (viral, bacterial) or environmental pollutants.
  • Other conditions can mimic or worsen exacerbation-like symptoms: pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure, pneumothorax.
  • In many patients the exact cause remains unknown.

Initial assessment of a suspected exacerbation (Figure 4.1)

  1. Complete a thorough clinical assessment for evidence of COPD and potential respiratory and non-respiratory conditions — symptoms, and signs of pneumonia, cardiovascular conditions and pulmonary embolism.
  2. Assess:
    • Symptoms, severity of dyspnoea (VAS), presence of cough.
    • Signs — tachypnoea, tachycardia, sputum volume and colour, respiratory distress (accessory muscle use).
  3. Perform additional investigations as needed and available — pulse oximetry, laboratory (blood eosinophils, CRP, viral/bacterial studies), arterial blood gases.
  4. Evaluate for underlying conditions — viral or bacterial infection, environmental or cardiovascular events that mimic exacerbation.
  5. Consider the appropriate place of care.

10 · NIV & Invasive Mechanical Ventilation — Indications

Indications for non-invasive mechanical ventilation (Figure 4.8)

At least one of the following:

  • Respiratory acidosis — PaCO₂ ≥ 6.0 kPa (45 mmHg) and arterial pH ≤ 7.35
  • Severe dyspnoea with clinical signs suggestive of respiratory muscle fatigue and/or increased work of breathing — use of respiratory accessory muscles, paradoxical abdominal motion, retraction of the intercostal spaces
  • Persistent hypoxaemia despite supplemental oxygen therapy

Indications for invasive mechanical ventilation (Figure 4.9)

  • Persistent life-threatening hypoxaemia despite HFNT or NIV
  • Unable to tolerate HFNT and/or NIV
  • Status post respiratory or cardiac arrest
  • Diminished consciousness, psychomotor agitation inadequately controlled by sedation
  • Massive aspiration or persistent vomiting
  • Persistent inability to remove respiratory secretions
  • Severe haemodynamic instability without response to fluids and vasoactive drugs
  • Severe ventricular or supraventricular arrhythmias
Also consider (Figure 4.7) At least one of the following may indicate the need for ventilatory support: persistent hypoxaemia; unable to tolerate NIV; contraindication to NIV; weaning the patient off supplemental oxygen following NIV; preventing reintubation in patients requiring intubation and positive-pressure ventilation; treatment of patients with stable COPD at risk of exacerbations. Local resources must be considered.
11 · Post-Exacerbation Review & Follow-up

Before discharge — 8-point checklist (Figure 4.10)

  1. Full review of all clinical and laboratory data.
  2. Check maintenance therapy — patients with elevated blood eosinophils should be discharged on LABA + LAMA + ICS.
  3. Reassess inhaler technique.
  4. Ensure understanding of withdrawal of acute medications (steroids and/or antibiotics).
  5. Assess need for continuing supplemental oxygen.
  6. Provide a management plan.
  7. Follow up comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease.
  8. Ensure follow-up arrangements: early follow-up < 4 weeks, and late follow-up > 12 weeks as indicated.

1–4 weeks follow-up

  • Evaluate ability to cope in their usual environment
  • Review understanding of treatment regimen
  • Reassess inhaler technique
  • Reassess need for long-term oxygen
  • Document capacity to do physical activity; consider eligibility for pulmonary rehabilitation
  • Document symptoms (CAAT™ or mMRC)
  • Determine status of comorbidities

12–16 weeks follow-up

  • Evaluate ability to cope in their usual environment
  • Review understanding of treatment regimen
  • Reassess inhaler technique
  • Reassess need for long-term oxygen
  • Document capacity for physical activity and activities of daily living
  • Measure spirometry (FEV₁)
  • Document symptoms (CAAT™ or mMRC)
  • Determine status of comorbidities

12 · Comorbidities & Multimorbidity

Key points

  • Patients with COPD often have other chronic conditions that increase the risk of poor outcomes.
  • Multimorbidity is often underdiagnosed and undertreated and should be actively sought in each patient.
  • The presence of comorbidities should not alter COPD treatment; comorbid disease should be treated per usual standards despite the presence of COPD.
  • Cardiovascular diseases — particularly hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure and atrial fibrillation — are common. The risk of a major cardiovascular event is significantly increased during and up to one year after a moderate or severe exacerbation.
  • Lung cancer is a major cause of death. Annual low-dose CT is recommended for patients with a smoking history, similar to general population recommendations. Screening in COPD not associated with tobacco smoking is not recommended (lack of evidence).
  • Bronchiectasis is frequently present and, when associated with infections, impacts disease progression, exacerbations and risk of death.
  • Depression/anxiety are frequent, under-diagnosed and under-treated, and are associated with poor health status and increased risk of death.
  • Low BMI < 21 kg/m² is associated with increased risk of death. Obesity (> 30 kg/m²) is associated with metabolic syndrome and OSA — if OSA is present, CPAP treatment decreases risk of death.
  • GERD is associated with an increased risk of exacerbations and poor health status.
  • MOLT (multiple organ loss of tissue) — osteoporosis, sarcopenia, anaemia and emphysema — is associated with poor outcomes. Adequate nutrition, pulmonary rehabilitation and management of MOLT can improve outcomes.
  • When COPD is part of a multimorbidity care plan, a goal should be simplicity of treatment, minimising polypharmacy.

  • Smoking history > 30 pack-years
  • Presence of emphysema on CT scan
  • Presence of airflow limitation (FEV₁/FVC < 0.7)
  • BMI < 25 kg/m²
  • Family history of lung cancer

15 · Palliative & End-of-Life Care
  • All clinicians managing patients with COPD should be aware of the effectiveness of palliative approaches to symptom control and use these in practice. D
  • End-of-life care should include discussions with patients and families about views on resuscitation, advance directives and place-of-death preferences. D
  • Opiates, neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), oxygen and fans blowing air onto the face can relieve breathlessness. C
  • Nutritional supplementation should be considered in malnourished patients — may improve respiratory muscle strength and overall health status. B
  • Fatigue can be improved by self-management education, pulmonary rehabilitation, nutritional support and mind–body interventions. B
  • All patients with advanced COPD should be considered for end-of-life and palliative care support to optimise symptom control and allow patients and families to make informed choices about future management.
16 · ICU & Perioperative Practice Points (derived from the guide)
Important The following is a theatre/critical-care orientated distillation of the GOLD 2026 Pocket Guide content in this document. It does not add new recommendations and does not replace local protocols or clinical judgement.

Before the case — assessment

  • Confirm the diagnosis and grade: post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.7; GOLD 1–4 by FEV₁ % predicted.
  • Assign the ABE group — group E (≥ 1 moderate/severe exacerbation in the past year) carries the highest risk of future events.
  • Quantify symptoms: mMRC (0–4) and CAAT™ (/40). A CAAT ≥ 10 or mMRC ≥ 2 = high symptom burden.
  • Screen for comorbidities — cardiovascular disease (especially heart failure and atrial fibrillation), OSA, obesity/low BMI, diabetes, anxiety/depression, osteoporosis, lung cancer, bronchiectasis, GERD, anaemia.
  • Check recent exacerbation and hospitalisation history — a recent exacerbation is a marker of high risk and recovery may take 4–6 weeks.
  • Review current inhalers and adherence; check device technique; note last eosinophil count and whether ICS is indicated.
  • Confirm vaccination status and smoking status.

Perioperative & ventilatory considerations

  • Inhaled bronchodilators are central to symptom management — LABA + LAMA is the preferred long-acting combination; do not stop maintenance bronchodilators for procedures or for spirometry when monitoring.
  • If there are features of asthma, treatment should always contain an ICS — and a LABA + ICS combination alone is not encouraged in COPD; use triple therapy if ICS is indicated.
  • Optimise before elective surgery: ensure adequate bronchodilation, treat active infection, address reversible comorbidity, consider pulmonary rehabilitation referral.
  • Oxygen: titrate to SaO₂ ≥ 90% (this is the target used when prescribing long-term oxygen in the guideline).
  • Anticipate hypercapnia — blood gas measurement is required to grade exacerbation severity and to decide on ventilatory support.
  • Consider HFNT first in acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure; use NIV for hypercapnic respiratory failure (PaCO₂ ≥ 45 mmHg with pH ≤ 7.35) or non-response to HFNT, unless contraindicated.

In the ICU — do not miss

  • Exacerbation mimics: pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure, pneumothorax, arrhythmia, myocardial infarction — investigate actively (chest imaging, ECG, troponin, NT-proBNP, D-dimer, lung ultrasound).
  • Steroids and antibiotics are both 5-day courses; antibiotics are indicated with purulent sputum, prior positive sputum culture, or need for mechanical ventilation.
  • Methylxanthines are not recommended in exacerbations (side-effect profile).
  • VTE prophylaxis with subcutaneous heparin/LMW heparin; monitor fluid balance.
  • Long-term oxygen is not indicated for moderate desaturation — reserve for PaO₂ ≤ 55 mmHg (or < 60 mmHg with pulmonary hypertension, heart failure or haematocrit > 55%).
  • Long-term NIV may benefit patients with PaCO₂ > 53 mmHg after a recent hospitalisation.
  • Consider lung-protective strategies and early pulmonary rehabilitation referral once stable.

At discharge from ICU / hospital

  • Full review of clinical and laboratory data.
  • Check maintenance therapy — LABA + LAMA + ICS if blood eosinophils are elevated.
  • Reassess inhaler technique and ensure understanding of stopping acute steroids/antibiotics.
  • Assess ongoing oxygen requirement; provide a written management plan.
  • Arrange early follow-up < 4 weeks and late follow-up > 12 weeks (with spirometry).

Appendix C · Emerging Technologies & Telehealth (Chapter 6)
  • Artificial intelligence can help in the diagnosis, assessment, clinical management and prediction of prognosis of COPD.
  • AI carries risks and limitations that need careful consideration before deployment in clinical practice.
  • Telehealth includes virtual, hybrid virtual and in-person care models; it may offer improved healthcare access, outcomes and affordability.
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation and self-management may be delivered virtually; evidence on effectiveness of virtual versus in-person delivery is still emerging.
