Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is a supportive therapy for fluid balance and metabolic derangement in critically ill patients, with or without acute kidney injury (AKI).

Aims

  • Relieve hypervolaemia and maintain fluid balance
  • Remove excess urea & creatinine
  • Correct and maintain metabolic and electrolyte balance
  • Remove toxins

Indications to commence

  • Fluid overload
  • Electrolyte imbalance
  • Acute kidney injury (AKI)
  • Inborn errors of metabolism
  • Sepsis

Convection (ultrafiltration)

Large volumes of fluid cross the semi-permeable membrane down a hydrostatic pressure gradient, dragging solute with it (solvent drag). Replacement fluid (pre-blood pump and/or post-dilution) increases flow across the membrane. Best for middle and large molecules.

Diffusion

Solute moves down a concentration gradient. Achieved by running dialysate counter-current to blood on the other side of the membrane, maintaining the gradient along the whole filter. Best for small molecules (urea, creatinine, ammonia).

Adsorption

Some molecules adhere to the artificial membrane — thought to be how inflammatory markers are removed. High levels can clog the filter and reduce effectiveness.

Prismaflex filter pores pass molecules up to 35,000 Daltons — small ions and molecules freely, plus some larger molecules (myoglobin, insulin, interleukins).

CVVH vs CVVHDF

ModePrinciplesClearance
CVVHUltrafiltration / convection only (replacement fluid)Middle & large molecules
CVVHDFConvection + diffusion (replacement fluid and dialysate)Optimises clearance across all molecule sizes; better acid–base, waste and electrolyte control
  • Always select CVVHDF when setting up the Prismaflex.
  • With heparin you may switch between CVVH and CVVHDF during treatment according to clinical condition.
  • With citrate, CVVHDF is always the therapy of choice — dialysate off-loads citrate into the effluent bag (limits citrate accumulation) and reduces high blood bicarbonate.
  • If therapy is too efficient, move the patient down a weight bracket (see §9).

Replacement ≈ 50% of body weight (1 kg = 1 litre) is usually adequate for solute and electrolyte removal. Low blood flow, high haematocrit and high plasma protein limit filtration.

Citrate is the anticoagulation of choice on PCCU. It is regional: it anticoagulates the circuit and filter without anticoagulating the patient, so the bleeding risk is much lower than with heparin. It also increases circuit life and reduces treatment interruptions.

How it works

  • Citrate binds ionised calcium (iCa) and magnesium. iCa is essential for activation of the coagulation cascade — binding it prevents clotting in the extracorporeal circuit.
  • Metabolised in the liver to bicarbonate (1 mmol citrate → 3 mmol bicarbonate).
  • Delivered pre-filter on the pre-blood pump (PBP) scale, so both circuit and filter are anticoagulated.
  • Dose is expressed in mmol/L blood. If blood flow is adjusted, the Prismaflex automatically adjusts the PBP citrate rate to hold the set dose.
  • Most citrate is cleared by the filter into the effluent; the remainder reaching the patient is the patient citrate load.

Citrate also binds magnesium

Expect a fall in serum magnesium — check daily while on the filter. Correct if < 0.7 mmol/L (per unit policy; discuss with consultant and pharmacist before replacing).

Precautions / contraindications — discuss with consultant

  • Severe liver disease (the calcium–citrate complex is metabolised by the liver)
  • Severe hepatic failure with INR > 3.5
  • Paracetamol hepatitis with gross elevation of AST/INR and raised lactate
  • Ethylene glycol poisoning
  • Clinical bleeding risk considered too great for any CRRT anticoagulation
  • Previous citrate accumulation, if organ dysfunction persists
  • Additional citrate load, e.g. ongoing massive transfusion

If citrate is contraindicated, see Appendix A (heparin) and Appendix B (epoprostenol).

Citrate binds ionised calcium and some of that complex is lost in the effluent. To avoid systemic ionised hypocalcaemia, a separate calcium infusion is required, adjusted to keep patient iCa 1.0 – 1.3 mmol/L.

Route: the calcium infusion is delivered by the Prismaflex syringe pump directly to the patient via a central line — not into the circuit. DO NOT infuse calcium gluconate into the return lumen unless instructed by the consultant or Paediatric Renal Critical Care Nurse. If central access is problematic, discuss options with the consultant and pharmacist.

  • Additives (phosphate, glucose) are only added to bags used with heparin or epoprostenol anticoagulation — see Appendix C.

Priming

  • Plasmalyte 148 is the priming fluid of choice (use 0.9% sodium chloride if potassium is high).
  • With citrate, a heparin prime is no longer necessary — one prime with Plasmalyte 148 is all that is required.
  • 4.5% HAS prime may be considered if required — discuss with consultant.
  • Blood priming should not be practised. If the patient needs blood, transfuse before or during commencement of therapy.
  1. Baseline bloods: ABG, FBC, U&E, LFT, magnesium, phosphate, total calcium, clotting.
  2. Check iCa and magnesium are within normal limits.
  3. Set calcium compensation from pre-treatment iCa:
    • iCa > 1.3 → start at 90%
    • iCa 1.0–1.3 → start at 100%
    • iCa < 1.0 → start at 110% (consider a correction first)
  4. Magnesium < 0.7 → correct (unit policy; discuss with consultant & pharmacist).
  5. If a systemic calcium infusion is already running, continue it until filtration is established and iCa is at target, then stop the non-Prismaflex infusion.
  6. No ACT needed with citrate — effectiveness is monitored by filter iCa.
  7. Complete the CVVH/CVVHDF daily prescription and record chart; every change is checked by two nurses and signed.

  • Calculated hourly from actual fluid balance, desired balance, and the patient's condition and stability.
  • Depends on input (nutrition, IV fluids, drugs) and output (urine, gastric, drains).
  • The Prismaflex continues at the set hourly rate until changed by staff — the rate must be reviewed every hour.
  • Beware of removing fluid too quickly.

How to calculate the programmed rate

Net fluid removal rate = desired net removal (e.g. 0 mL/h = neutral balance; 10 mL/h = −10 mL/h to the patient).
Programmed rate = net fluid removal rate + total hourly input (IV fluids, infusions, drugs).
Example: 45 mL/h of IV fluids and infusions + 10 mL/h net removal = program 55 mL/h.
  • Do not remove fluid given as a bolus to support blood pressure or replace intravascular loss.
  • Blood products given for volume should be added to the programmed fluid removal rate.
  • A minus number on the machine means it has gained that amount of fluid — common at commencement; it should readjust after a few minutes.
EXTREME CAUTION when setting the fluid loss rate. The Prismaflex will try to remove whatever is set — it cannot assess the effect on the patient and will continue removing fluid even when the patient is hypovolaemic. Extra caution in low body weight children. No paediatric CRRT equipment is accurate mL for mL.

Clinical monitoring

  • Examine haemodynamic status as soon as therapy is commenced; continuous cardiovascular and respiratory monitoring.
  • Observations hourly or half-hourly depending on stability.
  • Continuous temperature measurement (centrally if able).
  • Arterial blood pressure monitoring is ideal (and required for regular patient iCa sampling).
  • CVP, pulse pressure variation and cardiac index if available.
  • Weigh daily (bed scales if possible).
  • Assess fluid status using all of: fluid balance, clinical examination (HR, ABP, CVP), biochemistry, haematocrit, weight.

Monitoring iCa — post-filter and patient

  • Take a blood sample from the blue port on the circuit (post-filter) and another from the patient's arterial line — at the same time.
  • Check within the first hour of commencing, then again 1 hour later even if the first results are normal.
  • If both sets remain in range, monitor 4-hourly.
  • After any change to citrate dose or calcium compensation, recheck both after 1 hour.

If the citrate dose is reduced, the pre-blood pump and total effluent will reduce. If total effluent falls below 30 mL/kg/h, gradually increase the replacement flow rate until a dose of 30 mL/kg/h is achieved.
Increasing calcium compensation requirements may indicate citrate accumulation — check the total calcium / iCa ratio.

Total calcium : iCa ratio (calcium T:I ratio)

Take total calcium with the morning bloods the morning after treatment has commenced; divide total calcium by patient iCa. Repeat daily once stable. Inform the renal critical care nurse if ratio > 2.5.

Calcium T:I ratioAction
< 2.5No change needed — check daily
> 2.5Aim for post-filter iCa 0.35–0.40 mmol/L by decreasing the citrate dose in 0.5 mmol/L increments until the range is achieved; recheck the ratio 30 min after the change. If the ratio is still > 2.5 after a 1 mmol/L decrease in citrate dose → see Citrate accumulation (§12).

Acid–base management — aim for pH 7.35–7.45

Metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.35, BE < −4 mmol/L, HCO₃ < 22 mmol/L)

  • Most AKI patients have some acidosis at commencement; it should improve over the first few hours.
  • If BE/HCO₃ fail to improve towards normal, consider:
    • Decreasing the dialysate rate by 50% from baseline (more citrate reaches the patient)
    • Increasing the blood flow rate (increases citrate delivery)
    • Systemic sodium bicarbonate
  • These have different effects on clearance, citrate toxicity risk and cardiovascular status — discuss with consultant and renal critical care nurse.

Metabolic alkalosis (pH > 7.45, BE > +4 mmol/L, HCO₃ > 25 mmol/L)

  • Occurs from citrate metabolism to bicarbonate (1 mmol citrate → 3 mmol bicarbonate) plus bicarbonate in the dialysate. May indicate citrate accumulation — check the calcium T:I ratio.
  • Consider doubling baseline dialysate flow (increases citrate clearance) or decreasing blood flow (minimum 20 mL/min) — discuss with consultant and renal critical care nurse.

Other considerations during therapy

  • Drug handling: filtration removes non-protein-bound, water-soluble drugs; adding dialysate increases diffusive removal. Check dose changes with a pharmacist. IVIG does not pass through the haemofilter.
  • Temperature control: set the sleeve heater 1–2 °C above the desired patient temperature on the return line. Beware of the heater masking pyrexia. Leave part of the line exposed near the catheter so air remains visible. Additional warming may be needed.
  • Asepsis: aseptic technique when connecting/disconnecting vascular access.

Emergency disconnection

Required if lines have clotted, technical failure, or evacuation of the unit. Consider wash-back. If time allows, disconnect and flush/lock the CVVH catheter with heparin or at least sodium chloride 0.9%.

Cardiac arrest

  • Stop treatment; aspirate, flush and lock the line as time allows.
  • Wash-back of circuit blood may be an option if deemed necessary for the patient.
  • Continuing filtration may be appropriate in some circumstances (e.g. life-threatening hyperkalaemia).
  • Haemofiltration catheters are central venous access and can be used instead of standard central lines in an emergency.

Air embolism

  • Attempt to aspirate air using the central line.
  • Place the patient on their left side, head down.
  • Summon emergency assistance.

Citrate accumulation

Occurs when the body fails to metabolise citrate effectively, or the citrate infusion rate exceeds the clearance capacity of the liver and haemofilter. Result: rising total calcium with falling patient iCa and a calcium T:I ratio > 2.5.

If the ratio remains high despite the steps in §11:

  1. Reduce the citrate dose to 1.5 mmol/L
  2. Repeat the ratio after 30 minutes
  3. If still > 2.5, double the baseline dialysate flow (increases citrate clearance)
  4. Decrease blood pump speed (minimum 20 mL/min) — reduces total citrate administered
  5. If improvement is seen, restart citrate at 70% of the previous dose
  6. Monitor circuit iCa hourly to ensure adequate anticoagulation
If the calcium T:I ratio remains high despite these actions, do not restart citrate — discuss converting to an alternative anticoagulation with the consultant.

Used only when citrate is contraindicated (e.g. citrate accumulation).

Properties

  • Inhibits coagulation by preventing conversion of prothrombin to thrombin and fibrinogen to fibrin; acts almost immediately IV; clotting time returns to normal 2–6 hours after stopping.
  • Binds extensively to plasma proteins; activated in the liver, excreted in urine.
  • Reversed by protamine sulphate — dose depends on the heparin rate (see BNFC); base the protamine dose on the cumulative heparin dose over the previous 2 hours.

Administration

  • Given into the dedicated heparin infusion line on the access side of the circuit.
  • Initial bolus (if appropriate) as blood reaches the pre-filter port, then a continuous infusion.
  • Made up in a 50 mL syringe.

Monitoring

  • Anticoagulation is monitored by ACT (Actalyke MINI).
  • A pre-filtration, non-heparinised clotting time must be taken at initial catheter insertion — individuals respond differently to the same dose. Initial ACT should be 100–130 seconds.
  • Check ACT 1–2 hourly thereafter; keep within the limits set on the prescription chart.
  • Maximum concentration 100 units/kg/40 mL; maximum rate 10 mL/h (25 units/kg/h). If ACTs remain low at this maximum rate, review and amend the target range and document it.
  • ACT low → risk of thrombosis/clotting (circuit and patient). ACT too high → risk of bleeding.
  • Monitor laboratory clotting closely; if deranged, the ACT target may need review and reduction.

Contraindications

Active bleeding · imminent or recent surgery · coagulopathies (e.g. thrombocytopenia, especially septic/oncology) · heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) · DIC · liver disease · haemophilia · diabetes · pericarditis.

CRRT may be carried out in these circumstances with minimal/no heparin or an alternative such as epoprostenol (Appendix B).

Use if the patient has contraindications to both citrate and heparin; may also be combined with heparin if heparin alone is inadequate. Epoprostenol anticoagulates the patient (and therefore the blood travelling through the circuit).

Properties

  • Prostaglandin and potent inhibitor of platelet aggregation — dose related; half-life ~6 minutes.
  • ACTs are not affected — do not use ACT to titrate the epoprostenol rate (overall coagulation status may still be checked regularly).
  • Potentiates heparin by ~50%, possibly by reducing release of heparin-neutralising factor.
  • If blood pressure is supported with inotropes, titrate these upwards before reducing the epoprostenol dose.
  • Never give a bolus (systemic effects).

Contraindications / cautions

  • Treat any hypovolaemia before commencing the infusion.
  • Avoid sudden withdrawal — risk of rebound pulmonary hypertension.
  • Contraindicated in severe left ventricular dysfunction and pulmonary veno-occlusive disease.
  • Inhibition of platelet aggregation can cause unwanted bleeding — monitor coagulation carefully, especially with other anticoagulants.
  • Vasodilator effect may be augmented by, or augment, other vasodilators.
  • Caution with abnormal coagulation.
