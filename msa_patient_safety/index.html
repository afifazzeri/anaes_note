1Principles of Anaesthesia Care
  • Who is an “anaesthesiologist”? A registered medical practitioner who is either a qualified specialist anaesthesiologist, or a medical officer/trainee who administers an anaesthetic. 1.1
  • Who may give anaesthesia: all anaesthetics must be given by a registered medical practitioner with recognised certified training in anaesthesia and resuscitation, or by medical officers under adequate supervision of a qualified specialist anaesthesiologist. The specialist is responsible for overall anaesthetic / monitored anaesthesia care. 1.2
  • One patient at a time: the anaesthesiologist cannot provide direct care for more than one patient receiving anaesthesia or sedation, and must be present from induction until safe transfer to recovery or ICU. 1.3
  • Temporary delegation: may delegate monitoring temporarily to an appropriately qualified person judged competent. May leave only if the patient is stable, no potentially adverse event is likely, and return at short notice is possible. A skilled assistant is no substitute for the anaesthesiologist. 1.4
  • Pre-anaesthetic consultation by a registered medical practitioner with appropriate anaesthesia training; informed consent obtained per Malaysian Medical Council guidelines. 1.5, 1.10, 1.11
  • Documentation: adequate, legible minimal standard monitoring records — part of the patient's medical records. 1.6
  • Skilled assistance must be available at all times during anaesthesia. 1.7
  • Equipment: ensure all equipment functions properly before each anaesthetic; the health facility is responsible for maintenance and servicing. 1.8
  • Transfer & positioning: adequate manpower; anaesthesiologist has main responsibility for airway, head and neck. Use devices allowing horizontal transfer without lifting, to avoid staff injury. 1.9
  • Fatigue: identify fatigue in yourself and other team members. 1.12
  • Exceptional circumstances: if called to assist a life-saving procedure nearby, use individual judgement, leave a trained assistant who knows how to recall you, and return to your primary patient as soon as possible. 1.13
Purpose of pre-anaesthetic consultation: medical assessment before surgery/procedure; identify illnesses and anaesthesia risks to reduce anaesthesia- and surgery-related morbidity and mortality.
2The Anaesthetic Machine / Anaesthetic Workstation
  • Provision, maintenance, calibration and renewal of anaesthetic machines are the institution's responsibility; anaesthesiologists should be involved in procurement and maintenance policies, and must be adequately trained in use and checking. 2.1
Pre-use check — before EVERY anaesthetic

The anaesthesiologist must CHECK the machine before the start of EACH anaesthetic, in addition to the automatic manufacturer's check:

  1. Gas supply pipelines
  2. Anaesthetic machine
  3. Airway devices, breathing systems and circuits
  4. Ventilators
  5. Vaporisers
  6. Suction device
  7. Monitoring and ancillary equipment
  8. Scavenging system

2.2

  • Back-up: an alternative device for oxygenation and ventilation (e.g. self-inflating bag) with a separate oxygen source must be readily available. 2.3
Mandatory machine safety features
  1. Minimum oxygen ratio device (O₂/N₂O proportioning system)
  2. Oxygen failure safety (“fail-safe”) device
  3. Oxygen supply pressure failure alarm
  4. Vaporiser interlock device
  5. Pin Index Safety System
  6. Non-interchangeable, gas-specific connectors on gas pipeline inlets

Existing machines lacking these features are to be replaced. 2.4, 2.5

3Intraoperative Monitoring of the Patient

Ensure proper functioning of equipment and monitor oxygenation, ventilation, circulation and depth of anaesthesia from induction through emergence until transfer to recovery/PACU. Clinical observations must be supplemented by monitoring equipment wherever possible. 3.1, 3.2

3.3.1 Oxygenation
  • Colour of mucous membranes and operative site.
  • Pulse oximeter is mandatory — must have a variable pulse tone and a low alarm limit audible to the anaesthesiologist or anaesthesia care personnel.
  • Oxygen concentration of the anaesthetic gas mixture must be continuously monitored during general anaesthesia.
3.3.2 Circulation
  • Observe pulse, heart rate and blood pressure.
  • BP and HR measured and recorded regularly, at a frequency appropriate to the patient's clinical condition.
  • ECG continuously displayed throughout the anaesthetic. A normal ECG may be present despite grossly inadequate circulation or oxygenation; it may give early warning of arrhythmias and myocardial ischaemia.
3.3.3 Ventilation

Adequacy of ventilation monitored at all times by observing:

  • Excursion of the chest wall
  • Movement of the reservoir bag
  • Auscultation of breath sounds (precordial or oesophageal stethoscope)
  • A tidal volume monitor
3.4 Capnography — mandatory in general anaesthesia
  1. Quantitative assessment of ventilation
  2. Detector of adverse clinical events such as air embolism or pulmonary embolism
  3. Indicator of correct placement of a tracheal tube or laryngeal mask airway
  4. Indicator of the presence or absence of circulation
3.5–3.8 Pressure, volume, temperature, neuromuscular function, depth
  • Airway pressure monitoring: should be used during controlled ventilation and is recommended during spontaneous ventilation (breath-by-breath information on chest–lung mechanics). 3.5
  • Temperature: means to measure body temperature readily available; must be monitored when change in temperature is intended, anticipated or suspected; monitor in neonatal and paediatric patients and where an active warming device (forced air, radiant heater) is used. 3.6
  • Neuromuscular function: a peripheral nerve stimulator should be available when muscle relaxants are used. 3.7
  • Depth of anaesthesia:
    • Anaesthetic gas concentration monitoring with a minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) indicator is mandatory when inhalation anaesthetic gases are used.
    • Depth monitoring (BIS, Auditory Evoked Potential, Entropy) is indicated in patients at high risk of awareness, and when TIVA is used — especially with neuromuscular blockade.
    3.8
3.9 Specialised monitoring

May be required: complicated operations or specialised procedures; special techniques such as induced hypotension or one-lung ventilation; patients with coexisting medical disease.

Includes:

  • Invasive arterial and central venous pressure monitoring
  • Cardiac output monitor
  • Neurological function monitoring
  • Transoesophageal echocardiography
  • Point-of-care blood investigations (e.g. ACT, ABG, glucose, TEG)
3.10 Caveats
  • Brief interruptions of continuous monitoring may be unavoidable — document in the anaesthetic record.
  • In rare/unusual circumstances some methods may be clinically impractical — make an appropriate notation in the anaesthetic record.
  • Monitoring encourages quality patient care, but even when appropriately used it may fail to detect untoward clinical developments and cannot guarantee any specific outcome.
4Recovery from Anaesthesia
  • Recovery should be in a designated area, appropriately staffed and equipped. Minimum staffing ratio appropriate for the number of operational beds. Staff must be trained for the role; junior staff placed under direct supervision of experienced nurses, preferably with post-basic anaesthesia training. The most experienced nurse should be available for patients needing closer observation. 4.1
  • All patients in recovery must be appropriately monitored according to their condition (see Section 3). 4.3
  • Proper handover for transfer of care: OR staff/doctor → recovery staff; recovery staff → ward staff. Handover must include monitoring requirements and lines of management for expected and unexpected complications. Document on the anaesthesia form or case notes. 4.4, 4.5, 4.7
  • Discharge: use of a recovery scoring system is encouraged. Medical assistance must be immediately available for emergencies. The patient must be reviewed by an anaesthesiologist before discharge from recovery. 4.6, 4.8, 4.9

  • Theatre → recovery: under supervision of the anaesthesiologist; supplemental oxygen if necessary; bed/trolley with supportive side rails and a mechanism for head-down positioning.
  • Recovery → ward / ICU: a checklist should document that the patient is fit for safe discharge from recovery; the patient should be received by a qualified nurse during handover, with documentation.
5Patient Monitoring in the Non-Operating Room Anaesthesia (NORA) Setting
  • NORA procedures continue to increase in type and complexity. Patients present at extremes of age and in graver health, more often as emergencies, sometimes at advanced stages of disease previously considered unsuitable for intervention; complex/multiple procedures may be prolonged. 5.1
  • Anaesthesia outside the operating room carries greater risk to the patient and environmental risk to staff — physical separation from the patient (e.g. radiological suites), hazardous environments (e.g. radiotherapy), or inability to use certain monitors (e.g. MRI rooms). 5.2
  • No difference in monitoring standard. Patients receiving NORA are entitled to the equivalent standard of monitoring during anaesthesia and recovery as in the operating room. Basic monitoring standards apply; advanced monitoring (e.g. invasive haemodynamic monitoring) must be available for complex procedures and unstable patients. Specialised devices (depth of anaesthesia, respiratory monitoring) can improve safety. 5.3
  • Physical separation mandates reliable, appropriately positioned, procedure-specific monitoring equipment. Where there is uncertainty about the extent of intervention, place advanced monitors pre-procedure rather than in response to catastrophe. 5.4
  • Two critical components: the equipment measuring/displaying data, and the provider who reads, interprets and acts on it. Vigilance and the special skillset of the anaesthesiologist are extremely important in NORA. 5.5
  • Because proximity is limited, sight/sound/touch assessment (airway, oxygenation, ventilation, circulation, temperature) and equipment checks are compromised. Strategically placed video cameras can help detect changes in ventilatory pattern, movement, and monitor function. 5.6
  • An appropriate location with appropriate monitoring facilities for post-anaesthetic recovery must be available for NORA patients, with equivalent standards to the operating room. 5.7
  • Skilled, dedicated assistance for the anaesthesiologist is essential during the procedure and in recovery. 5.8
6Regional Anaesthesia
  • Patients undergoing regional anaesthesia must receive the equivalent standard of care and monitoring as general anaesthesia throughout the perioperative period. 6.1
  • Pre-operative assessment: in addition to routine assessment, enquire into possible contraindications to regional anaesthesia. Patient refusal is an absolute contraindication. 6.2
  • Ultrasound guidance, with or without nerve stimulator, is encouraged for safe conduct of regional blocks when necessary. 6.3
  • All staff must recognise the symptoms and signs of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST). The recovery room should have 20% Intralipid available at all times, administered at the recommended dose until clinical improvement is noted (see Section 9). 6.4

Note: the source document specifies 20% Intralipid in §6.4, while the emergency drug list in §9.1 lists “Intralipid 10%”. Follow your local protocol and the latest ASRA/AAGBI guidance on lipid emulsion therapy.

7Monitored Anaesthesia Care and Monitored Sedation

Objectives of sedation: anxiolysis, a degree of amnesia, and maintenance of patient co-operation so that uncomfortable diagnostic and minor surgical procedures may be performed.

Monitored Sedation vs MAC: in monitored sedation, sedatives/analgesics are given to allay anxiety and manage pain, and the proceduralist may deliver these drugs personally while supervising delivery. In Monitored Anaesthesia Care (MAC), an anaesthetic doctor focuses exclusively on the patient for airway, oxygenation, haemodynamic or physiological derangements. The MAC provider must be prepared and qualified to convert MAC to general anaesthesia when required.
  • Any patient given sedation for a procedure should be assessed and informed consent obtained. 7.1
  • The person administering sedation must have adequate knowledge of the pharmacology of the drugs used and be able to detect and manage complications. For MAC, the person giving the drugs must monitor the patient and must not assume the additional role of operator. 7.2
  • There should be a written record of the time and dosages of drugs used, plus monitored vital signs, as part of the patient's records. 7.4
  • A protocol for handover to recovery staff, and from recovery staff to ward staff or discharge, including instructions on monitoring and management of expected and unexpected complications. 7.5
  • Extra medical staff should be immediately available in an emergency. 7.6
  • The patient should be reviewed by a doctor before discharge from the recovery room. 7.7
7.3 Location, staffing and equipment

The procedure should be performed in a location suitable in size and environment, staffed and equipped to deal with any cardiopulmonary emergency. Available facilities should include:

  1. Operating table or trolley that can be tilted to Trendelenburg position
  2. Adequate lighting and suction equipment
  3. Supply of oxygen and oxygen delivery devices
  4. Equipment to monitor the patient — basic monitoring: NIBP, pulse oximeter, heart rate, ECG. Respiratory monitors such as capnography or chest wall impedance are highly recommended, especially when loss of response to verbal contact is expected. Alarms enabled and set to appropriate audible values.
  5. Equipment for airway management and ventilation
  6. Appropriate drugs for cardiopulmonary resuscitation and equipment for drug administration
MRI suite: MRI-safe or MRI-conditional equipment must be used. These areas are considered high-risk — access to alarms and patients may be poor. 7.8
8Pre-Anaesthetic Consultation
  • Should preferably be performed by the anaesthesiologist who will manage the patient. If not possible, there must be means to convey the findings to the administering anaesthesiologist. 8.1
  • Timing: at an appropriate time before surgery to allow adequate assessment, review of investigations and optimisation — especially in significant co-morbidity, major surgery, or specific anaesthetic concerns. May be done in the anaesthetic clinic, ward or operating theatre. 8.2
  • Early consultation may not be possible (emergency surgery, labour ward, ICU) — but a relevant assessment must not be omitted. 8.3
  • Day-care or day-of-admission surgery: consultation preferably before admission (e.g. anaesthetic clinic); otherwise allow adequate time for assessment before surgery. 8.4
  • Consultation rooms/areas must have sufficient equipment, hand-washing/disinfection facilities, and enough space for private consultation and clinical evaluation. 8.5
  • Written summary available and part of the medical records; questionnaires, screening exams and telemedicine may be used. Privacy and confidentiality must be respected at all times. 8.11–8.13
8.6 What the consultation should include
  1. Relevant present and past medical history
  2. Review of current and past medication, including herbal medication
  3. Allergies
  4. Clinical examination of the patient
  5. Review of laboratory, radiological and other investigations; request further investigations if necessary
  6. Therapeutic measures ordered and carried out to optimise the patient, if necessary
  7. Fasting time
  8. Discussion of the anaesthetic plan, technique and pain management with the patient and/or guardian

Also: informed consent covering the anaesthetic technique, risk of anaesthesia and other risks relevant to the patient's condition and type of surgery 8.7; medication management — prescription, adjustment and discontinuance 8.8; the optimisation plan relayed to the primary team 8.9; consultation with other disciplines where appropriate 8.10.

9Resuscitation Facilities

Adequate resuscitation facilities must be available wherever anaesthesia, regional anaesthesia or monitored care is provided, and in corresponding recovery areas and areas conducting clinic procedures under local anaesthesia. They must be able to deal with cardiac arrest, arrhythmias, local anaesthetic toxicity, hypovolaemia/bleeding, bronchospasm, anaphylaxis, shock states and malignant hyperthermia.

  • All resuscitation equipment must undergo scheduled checks and maintenance.
  • Sizes appropriate to the whole range of patients cared for in that area, including all paediatric age groups, must be available.
  • Drug storage should be designed to avoid administration errors — separate high-alert medications and minimise confusion from look-alike/sound-alike (LASA) medications.

10Quality Assurance
  1. A continual evaluation of anaesthesia practices should be initiated. 10.1
  2. Regular discussion of appropriate topics and cases with multidisciplinary professional colleagues should take place. 10.2
  3. Protocols and standard operating procedures should be developed to ensure that deficiencies in individual and collective practice are identified and rectified. 10.3
11Safe Surgery Saves Lives

The WHO introduced the Surgical Safety Checklist in 2008 to improve surgical safety across the pre-, intra- and post-operative stages. In 2009 the Malaysian Ministry of Health pledged to improve surgical safety through two main strategies: improving inter-team communication, and using the Surgical Safety Checklist to improve standards of care. In 2013, the Safe Surgery Saves Lives (SSSL) programme was included in the Malaysian Patient Safety Goals.

The Ministry of Health Peri-Operative Checklist consists of: Pre-operative checklist, Operating team checklist, Swab and instrument count form, and Pre-discharge check.

11.1 Pre-operative checklist
  • Performed by two nurses — one from the ward, one from the operating theatre.
  • Performed at the receiving bay of the OT.
  • Includes checking the patient's profile, a pre-transfer check, and information on the surgeon, surgery and time.
11.2 Operating team checklist

Performed by the circulating nurse in the operating room, covering the entire events from start to end of surgery.

  • SIGN-IN — by the anaesthesiologist and coordinator nurse, prior to induction of anaesthesia. All components in this list must be checked.
  • TIME-OUT — surgeon, anaesthesiologist and scrub nurse must be present; prior to surgical incision. Each surgical team must perform their time out before starting their respective surgery.
  • SIGN-OUT — surgeon summarises the operative procedure and findings, and verifies specimens and tests to be done; anaesthesiologist highlights the post-operative anaesthetic plan. Doctors may want to update families from time to time.
11.3–11.5 Swab count, incident reporting, pre-discharge check
  • Swab and instrument count: performed by the scrub nurse and circulating nurse, who must not be interrupted during this procedure.
  • Incident / instrument malfunction form: must be filled up if there were any untoward incidences during the surgery or procedure.
  • Pre-discharge check: performed by the ward nurse and recovery room nurse as the patient leaves the OT.
