›2 Monitoring, Precautions & the Safety Checklist

Recommendations

  • All patients undergoing bronchoscopy should have heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturation recorded repeatedly — before, during and after the procedure. Grade D
  • All bronchoscopy units should undertake periodic audit of performance — efficacy, complications and patient satisfaction. √
  • All Trusts should have a safe sedation policy; all bronchoscopy unit staff (including trainees) must receive appropriate training. √

Flexible Bronchoscopy Safety Checklist

Adapted from the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist — not comprehensive; local additions encouraged.

  • Patient confirmed: identity, procedure, consent

  • Anticoagulant / antiplatelet agent status confirmed (Y/N)

  • Essential imaging reviewed and displayed (bronchoscopist)

  • Equipment problems identified for action
  • Key recovery / management concerns handed over

›3 Hypoxaemia & Oxygenation

Recommendations

  • Patients should be monitored by continuous pulse oximetry during bronchoscopy. Grade C
  • Oxygen supplementation should be used when desaturation is significant — SpO₂ change > 4%, or SpO₂ < 90% and prolonged (> 1 min). Grade D
  • Fitness for bronchoscopy should incorporate baseline SaO₂, lung function, comorbidity, sedation and procedural sampling, with appropriate monitoring and pre-procedure optimisation. Grade D

Evidence highlights

  • Desaturation typically begins with sedation and worsens on passage through the vocal cords. (Evidence level 2++)
  • Desaturation > 4% and SpO₂ < 90% occur twice as often sitting as supine.
  • Desaturation to < 90% by sampling technique: BAL 89% · wash 44% · brush 15% · biopsy 10%.
  • Suctioning can exacerbate hypoxaemia.
  • Supplemental O₂ via nasal or pharyngeal catheter at ≥ 2 L/min reduces severity. (Evidence level 2++)
  • Hypoxaemia may persist for at least 2 h post-procedure; in one third of patients saturations remain below baseline at 3 h.
  • Incidence of cardiac arrhythmia is not affected by oxygen supplementation.

›4 Cardiac Risk, Arrhythmia & Myocardial Infarction

Recommendations

  • Continuous ECG monitoring should be used when there is a high clinical risk of arrhythmia. Grade D
  • When arrhythmia risk is high, optimise oxygen saturations, pulse rate and blood pressure; give appropriate aftercare monitoring and instructions. Grade D

Haemodynamic effects

  • Heart rate rises ~40% above baseline; blood pressure ~30% above baseline; cardiac index ~17–32% above baseline.
  • The rate–pressure product can approach or exceed the threshold associated with silent myocardial ischaemia, particularly in hypertensive patients.
  • ECG changes occur in ~15% (ST-T change 4%, transient RBBB 3%) and correlate with older age and smoking pack-years, but not with lung function or oxygen saturation changes.
  • Cardiac strain reported in 21% of patients over 60 years.

Arrhythmia

  • Sinus tachycardia 55–58%; sinus bradycardia 5–8%; premature ventricular contraction ~8%; atrial premature contraction 3–5%.
  • Major arrhythmias increase from 4% pre-procedure to ~40% during/after. Ventricular arrhythmias occur mainly on passage through the vocal cords and correlate with minimum oxygen saturation.
  • Asymptomatic ST-T changes in ~6%, typically correlating with maximum heart rate.

Myocardial infarction

  • Acute MI is a contraindication to bronchoscopy within 4–6 weeks.
  • Bronchoscopy within 30 days of acute MI carries a 5% mortality, limited to patients with active ischaemia at the time of the procedure.
  • In the absence of active ischaemia, with good clinical justification, bronchoscopy can be performed.

FB should ideally be delayed for 4 weeks after MI. Grade D
Good practice Resuscitation equipment immediately available; IV access before sedation and maintained until discharge. √
›5 Bleeding Risk & Anticoagulation

Recommendations

  • Perform coagulation studies, platelet count and haemoglobin concentration when there are clinical risk factors for abnormal coagulation. Grade D
  • Bronchoscopy with lavage can be performed with platelet counts > 20,000 per µL. Liaise with the local haematology team regarding platelet transfusion before bronchoscopy if EBB or TBLB is planned. Grade D
  • Discontinue clopidogrel 7 days prior to consideration of EBB and TBLB. Low-dose aspirin alone can be continued. Grade C
  • Anticoagulants should be managed according to published guidelines (see algorithm below). √
  • The risk of biopsy must be weighed against potential benefit and appropriate informed consent obtained. √

Clinical risk factors for abnormal coagulation (Box 2)

  • Anticoagulant therapy
  • Evidence of liver disease
  • History, family history or physical evidence of bleeding tendency
  • Active bleeding or pre-procedure transfusion
Important Over two-thirds of patients who bleed have normal coagulation and no clinical risk factors. Routine coagulation, platelet or haemoglobin testing does not reliably predict bleeding risk.

Bleeding classification (Table 5)

GradeDefinition / intervention required
No bleedingTraces of blood, no need for continuous suctioning; stops spontaneously
MildContinued suctioning of blood from the airways; stops spontaneously
ModerateIntubation of the biopsied segment with the bronchoscope into the wedge position; use of adrenaline or cold saline to stop bleeding
SeverePlacement of bronchus blocker or catheter, fibrin sealant; resuscitation, blood transfusion, ICU admission or death

Bleeding risk by procedure

  • ~90% of bleeding stops spontaneously or needs only local vasoconstrictor therapy (adrenaline/cocaine).
  • TBLB causes a twofold increase in mild–moderate and a threefold increase in severe bleeding vs EBB — but 92% of TBLB bleeds are < 20 mL.
  • Clopidogrel with TBLB: excessive (> 100 mL) bleeding in 89% (clopidogrel alone) vs 3.4%; clopidogrel + aspirin 100% vs 3.4%.
  • Renal failure (dialysis or not) with biopsy/TBNA: overall complication rate ~8% (4% major, 4% minor).
  • Lung transplant recipients may be predisposed to greater blood loss at biopsy.

Management of bleeding at bronchoscopy (Appendix 6)

  1. Ensure adequate oxygenation and IV access for fluid resuscitation. Monitor vital signs regularly.
  2. Retract the bronchoscope proximally to maintain vision; apply suction to remove free blood and preserve airway patency. Consider lying the patient onto the side of the bleeding. Do not suction to remove clot.
  3. Apply local vasoconstrictor: 5–10 mL 1:10,000 epinephrine or 5–10 mL 4 °C saline (saline may be repeated).
  4. If bleeding continues, wedge the bronchoscope into the bleeding segmental bronchus and hold for 10–15 minutes.
  5. If still uncontrolled, use a balloon catheter to apply pressure and isolate the segment.
  6. Check platelet count, PT and PTT; recheck drug history.
  7. Seek senior/expert assistance; consider referral to critical care.

Before biopsy: consider whether the biopsy is necessary, whether the patient has physiological reserve and a secure airway, and whether prophylactic local vasoconstrictor, APC or rigid bronchoscopic biopsy is more appropriate.

›6 Pneumothorax

Recommendations

  • A chest radiograph should be obtained if a patient is symptomatic or there is clinical suspicion of possible pneumothorax after TBLB. Grade D
  • Fluoroscopic screening may improve diagnostic yield of TBLB in focal but not diffuse lung disease. Grade D
  • Patients should be advised of the potential for delayed complications following TBLB and given written information regarding likely symptoms and action required. Grade D

Key facts

  • Pneumothorax represents ~10% of all bronchoscopy complications but rarely occurs without TBLB or therapeutic bronchoscopy.
  • Rarely total; often delayed (~40%), and 40–70% of cases require intercostal tube drainage.
  • Frequency is related to age and the number of TBLBs.
  • Routine chest X-ray after TBLB rarely provides useful information in the absence of symptoms and may not be required. In the absence of routine imaging, monitor for symptoms for 2 hours.
  • Rate of pneumothorax after TBLB does not differ significantly with or without fluoroscopy, but fluoroscopy may improve diagnostic yield in focal disease (focal 4.3% vs diffuse 9%).
ICU relevance Post-bronchoscopy pneumothorax can occur in mechanically ventilated patients even when no biopsy has been taken. Monitor after the procedure accordingly. Grade D
›7 Fever & Antibiotic Prophylaxis

Recommendations

  • Patients should receive written information regarding post-bronchoscopy fever (PBF) and appropriate management advice. Grade C
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis is not warranted before bronchoscopy for the prevention of endocarditis, fever or pneumonia. Grade B

Key facts

  • PBF typically occurs ~8 h (range 4–24 h) after BAL (13%), with fever > 38 °C, neutrophilic leucocytosis, raised CRP/fibrinogen and proinflammatory cytokines, and no bacteraemia.
  • Fever is usually < 40 °C and lasts on average 14 h; rarely accompanied by a chest X-ray infiltrate.
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis does not prevent PBF, pneumonia or the cytokine response. (Evidence level 1++)
  • True bacteraemia occurs in 6–8%; NICE (2008) concluded antibacterial prophylaxis is not recommended for prevention of endocarditis during upper/lower respiratory tract procedures including bronchoscopy.
›8 Specific Medical Conditions

Asthma

  • Patients' asthma control should be optimised prior to bronchoscopy, especially when BAL is likely. Grade C
  • Nebulised bronchodilators should be considered before bronchoscopy. √

FEV₁ falls on average 10–26% in asthma (9–17% in healthy volunteers), but most comparative studies show no difference between groups. Up to 10% develop respiratory symptoms post-procedure; BAL causes more symptoms than bronchoscopy alone. Complication rates 3.5–12%. Severe asthma is more likely to require post-procedure oral corticosteroids.

COPD

  • COPD treatment should be optimised prior to bronchoscopy when possible. Grade D
  • Bronchoscopists should be cautious when sedating patients with COPD. Grade D

Severe COPD (FEV₁ < 50% predicted or < 1 L with FEV₁/FVC < 69%): 5% complication rate vs 0.6% controls (pneumonia, respiratory failure, desaturation). In hypercapnic patients, desaturation occurred in 30%, wheeze in 55%, and the procedure was terminated early in 20%. Nebulised salbutamol before bronchoscopy does not alter post-procedure complication rates.

Ischaemic heart disease

  • Liaise with cardiologists in high-risk cardiac patients and if FB is indicated within 4–6 weeks after MI. Grade D
  • FB should ideally be delayed for 4 weeks after MI. Grade D

Haemoptysis

  • Consider bronchoscopy after a normal CT if the patient is high risk for lung carcinoma or if haemoptysis continues. Grade D

CT should always be considered before bronchoscopy. In one series, an endobronchial diagnosis was found in only 0.5% of individuals when CT was normal.

Older patients

  • Age alone should not be a contraindication for bronchoscopy. Grade D
  • Older patients may require reduced doses of benzodiazepines/opioids for sedation. Grade D

Complication rates for pneumothorax and transient hypotension may increase with age. Patients over 80 may have higher complication and mortality rates and are more likely to be mechanically ventilated after bronchoscopy.

Immunosuppressed patients

  • When a diagnosis is not likely to be obtained through non-invasive measures, bronchoscopy with BAL can be considered to provide diagnostic information. Grade C
  • TBLB is helpful in lung transplant recipients when rejection is a possibility. Grade C

BAL complication rate 0–49% depending on definitions; main complications are desaturation, fall in FEV₁ and haemorrhage. TBLB carries ~30% complication rate (mainly pneumothorax). Lung transplant recipients may be at higher risk of bleeding.

›9 Sedation, Premedication & Topical Anaesthesia

Premedication

  • Anticholinergics (glycopyrrolate or atropine) should not routinely be used — lack of clinical benefit and possible increased risk of haemodynamic changes. Grade A
  • Premedication for bronchoscopy is not routinely indicated. Grade C

Three RCTs (> 1300 patients) failed to show consistent benefit from anticholinergics; they were associated with tachycardia and hypertension. Premedication with benzodiazepines or opioid-like drugs should be discouraged if the same class is to be given IV during the procedure.

Sedation — principles

  • IV sedation should be offered to patients undergoing bronchoscopy, provided there are no contraindications. Grade B
  • Some patients tolerate unsedated bronchoscopy well — patient preference should be sought. Grade B
  • Sedative drugs should be titrated to the desired depth, given significant inter-patient variability. Grade B
  • Desired depth of sedation: verbal contact possible at all times ("conscious" sedation). Grade D
  • Document an assessment of sedation depth as part of the procedural report. √
Midazolam safety — NPSA Only low-strength midazolam (1 mg/mL) should be available within bronchoscopy suites. High-strength (2 mg/mL or 5 mg/mL) should be restricted to general anaesthesia, intensive care and other areas where its use has been formally risk assessed. Grade D
No more than 5 mg should be initially drawn up for patients < 70 years (2 mg for patients > 70 years). Grade D

Opioids

  • Combination opioid + midazolam sedation should be considered to improve bronchoscopic tolerance. Grade B
  • Use short-acting agents (fentanyl or alfentanil) to minimise post-procedural sedation. Grade D
  • When combination sedatives are used, give opioids first and allow time for maximal effect before any other agent. Grade D

Propofol

  • Similar efficacy to midazolam, but should only be used by practitioners formally trained in its administration (e.g. anaesthetists) — narrow therapeutic window beyond which general anaesthesia is achieved. Grade B
  • Associated with shorter recovery time than midazolam and a similar adverse event profile; no reversal agent available.

Topical anaesthesia

  • Lidocaine should be used for topical anaesthesia during bronchoscopy, unless contraindicated. Grade A
  • Nasal topical anaesthesia is most effectively provided using 2% lidocaine gel. Grade A
  • Both cricothyroid and spray-as-you-go techniques are effective for delivering lidocaine to the vocal cords and trachea. Grade B
  • Nebulisation is not recommended for delivering lidocaine to the airways. Grade B
  • 1% lidocaine solution should be used for spray-as-you-go administration. Grade A
  • Use the lowest dose sufficient to prevent excessive coughing and provide comfort. Grade D
  • Remain vigilant for objective and subjective symptoms of lidocaine toxicity, given significant inter-patient variability in absorption and metabolism. Grade B
  • Monitor and document the total lidocaine dose delivered at all sites. √
Lidocaine toxicity thresholds Subjective symptoms (e.g. dizziness, euphoria) reported at ≥ 9.6 mg/kg. Toxic plasma concentration conventionally > 5 micrograms/mL. A low cough score is achievable with a total dose of < 160 mg.

Sedation in specific groups

  • Respiratory failure: risk of oversedation and respiratory depression may be increased with combined sedation — use caution. A small rise in PaCO₂ occurs with midazolam ± alfentanil.
  • Older patients: likely to need lower doses and may have prolonged after-effects (prolonged amnesia, extended impaired coordination). No more than 2 mg midazolam initially drawn up for patients > 70 years (5 mg if < 70).
  • Comorbidities: dose modification likely needed in hepatic impairment, heart failure and renal impairment.
  • Concomitant medications: benzodiazepines, fentanyl and alfentanil are metabolised by CYP 3A4 — risk of prolonged sedation with antifungals, antiretrovirals, calcium channel blockers and macrolide antibiotics.
  • Substance misusers: may need higher doses of sedatives.

›10 Bronchoscopy in the ICU & Ventilated Patient
Core principle Patients in the ICU should be considered at high risk from complications when undergoing bronchoscopy. All potential risk factors (ventilator parameters, clotting dysfunction) should be corrected as far as possible before undertaking the procedure. Grade D
The risks and benefits should be carefully considered in mechanically ventilated patients. √

Bronchoscope selection in the ICU

  • The external diameter of the bronchoscope used in ICU should be carefully selected according to the external diameter of the scope, the size of the airway support device (ETT or laryngeal mask) and the type of airway device used. Grade D
  • In non-intubated patients the bronchoscope occupies only 10–15% of the tracheal cross-sectional area. A 5.7 mm bronchoscope occupies 40% of a 9 mm ETT and 66% of a 7 mm tracheal tube — failure to recognise this may cause inadequate ventilation, or impaction/damage of the scope.
  • Lubrication is essential. Tracheostomy tubes are prone to damage the bronchoscope, especially during withdrawal.

Indications in the ICU

Lobar collapse / atelectasis

  • Prophylactic bronchoscopy and lavage should not be used to prevent post-lobectomy atelectasis in ventilated patients. Grade A
  • Bronchoscopy may be considered in specific circumstances for the relief of atelectasis in intubated and ventilated patients. Grade D

A well-conducted RCT showed no benefit of bronchoscopy vs physiotherapy and suction for prevention of post-lobectomy atelectasis (blood gases, spirometry, need for further bronchoscopy, radiology, ICU and hospital stay). Four case series (47 patients) report good immediate outcomes for reversing established lobar atelectasis refractory to conventional treatment.

Haemoptysis / haemorrhage

  • Bronchoscopy may be considered in ventilated patients with haemoptysis if CT imaging has been performed and is unhelpful, or is not possible. Grade D

In 80 cases of haemoptysis in ventilated patients, a bleeding site was identified by bronchoscopy in 89% vs 80% by HRCT; the two together identified the site in 96%. The final cause was identified by bronchoscopy in only 2 patients vs 49 by HRCT. CT is therefore likely first-line. Consider local resources and other options (rigid bronchoscopy, surgery, interventional radiology).

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)

  • Directed non-invasive diagnostic strategies (e.g. blind catheter aspiration) should be used first line in preference to bronchoscopy in ventilated patients with suspected VAP. Grade A
  • When such non-invasive techniques fail to identify a responsible organism, bronchoscopy should be considered for the diagnosis of VAP. Grade D

A 2008 systematic review repeated in 2012 (5 RCTs, 1367 patients) showed no mortality difference (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.75–1.11) between invasive (bronchoscopic) and non-invasive sampling, and no difference in days ventilated, ICU stay or antibiotic change. Grade A / Evidence level 1−

Transbronchial biopsy & TBNA in ventilated patients

  • TBLB under fluoroscopy (13 ventilated patients with progressive infiltrates): diagnostic in 46%, but significant complications in 31% (2 pneumothoraces requiring drainage, 1 haemorrhage, 1 arrhythmia). Use cautiously — poor yield, significant potential harm.
  • TBNA (8 ventilated patients with abnormal lymph nodes on CT): no complications; sensitivity 83%, specificity 100%; management changed in 63%. May be useful in highly selected patients.

ARDS

  • Bronchoscopy and BAL can distinguish ARDS from acute lung injury using cell profiles where catheter lavage cannot — but the diagnostic methods used are research tools, and clinical implications are unknown.

Precautions & contraindications in ICU

  • Critically ill patients often have hypoxia, electrolyte disturbances, clotting abnormalities and arrhythmias. Elevated PT, increased APTT, reduced fibrinogen or thrombocytopenia make biopsy hazardous.
  • Critically ill patients may be more susceptible to the toxic effects of local anaesthetics; in the ventilated patient, IV sedation or anaesthesia is probably the most appropriate alternative.
  • Severe thrombocytopenia (platelets < 50) undergoing bronchoscopy: 2 procedure-associated deaths in 37 patients (5.4%), one from major airway bleeding; minor tracheobronchial bleeds needing no treatment in 32%. Feasible if corrected, but with significant potential harm.
  • In highly selected ARDS patients, bronchoscopy and BAL appeared relatively safe in the short term (1 h) with no clinically significant change in oxygenation; one pneumothorax occurred without TBLB.

Monitoring

  • Continuous multimodal physiological monitoring should be undertaken during and after bronchoscopy in the ICU setting. Grade C
  • Patients should be monitored post-procedure for complications, including pneumothorax, even when a biopsy has not been taken. Grade D

Use ECG, continuous intra-arterial or intermittent cuff blood pressure, pulse oximetry and end-tidal CO₂. Set appropriate alarm limits and ask attending staff to monitor physiological variables during the procedure. Adverse events require immediate withdrawal of the bronchoscope and resuscitation of the patient.

Bronchoscopy with BAL in ventilated patients is associated with worsened oxygenation (80–86% reduction in PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio from baseline regardless of BAL volume), changes in lung compliance, rises in mean arterial pressure, mean pulmonary artery pressure and CO₂. Hypoxia and tachycardia are common but clinically insignificant in most patients; in one ARDS subgroup a > 30% drop in PaO₂ occurred in 35%.

Ventilator settings

  • Pre-oxygenate by increasing inspired oxygen to 100%; give 100% oxygen during bronchoscopy and in the immediate recovery period.
  • Adjust the ventilator to a mandatory setting — triggered modes such as pressure support or assist control will not reliably maintain ventilation during bronchoscopy.
  • Increase the ventilator pressure limit to ensure adequate tidal volumes, and increase the rate if necessary.
  • Use a special swivel connector with a perforated diaphragm to allow continued ventilation and maintenance of PEEP/CPAP — particularly important in hypoxic patients (e.g. ARDS).
  • Jet ventilation and high-frequency positive pressure ventilation have limited supportive evidence; further comparative studies are needed.
Recommendations — ventilatory support CPAP plus oxygen support may be considered in patients with hypoxia undergoing bronchoscopy to prevent desaturation and post-procedure requirement for mechanical ventilation. Grade B
When patients require non-invasive ventilation prior to bronchoscopy, the procedure should be conducted in an environment where intubation and ventilatory support are readily accessible. Grade D
Bronchoscopy should be undertaken cautiously in patients with documented or suspected raised ICP. Grade D
Care must be exercised to ensure adequate ventilation and oxygenation is maintained during bronchoscopy in intubated patients. √

A small but well-conducted RCT suggests CPAP plus oxygen is superior to oxygen alone during bronchoscopy in hypoxic patients. In patients requiring NIV before bronchoscopy (n = 40), 10% required mechanical ventilation within 8 h, 45% within 48 h, and 55% in total (median time to intubation 27 h).

Intracranial pressure

  • Monitor ICP in patients with head injury if sudden rises due to CO₂ retention are to be avoided. Endotracheal CO₂ monitoring may help detect falls in minute ventilation caused by the bronchoscope within the ETT.
  • Four studies (1 RCT, 3 others) consistently demonstrate that bronchoscopy raises ICP.
  • Hyperventilation via the ventilator during bronchoscopy showed no difference in peak ICP but may accelerate return to baseline after bronchoscopy in closed head injury — clinical significance unknown.
  • Three small non-randomised studies suggest that while ICP is significantly raised, cerebral perfusion pressure is probably maintained.

Sedation & analgesia in ICU

  • Adequate sedation and analgesia should be provided. The risks of the procedure should be carefully balanced against potential benefit in ventilated patients. Grade D
  • Clinicians administering sedation/anaesthesia/analgesia should be acquainted with the use of these agents; the anaesthetist/intensivist is usually best placed to fulfil this role. Grade D

Synthetic narcotics such as alfentanil or fentanyl suppress cough and provide profound analgesia. Sedation can be induced with incremental doses of a benzodiazepine or propofol. Some patients need only light sedation; bronchoscopy can then be performed with supplemental topical lignocaine injected through the bronchoscope — but exercise care where renal failure, liver dysfunction and congestive heart failure may cause lignocaine accumulation. In one series (n = 104), falls in oxygenation were independently associated with ARDS and a clinician's observation of the patient "fighting the ventilator", providing weak indirect evidence that adequate sedation may help prevent hypoxia during bronchoscopy. (Evidence level 3)

›11 Sampling & Diagnostic Accuracy

Lung cancer

  • A diagnostic level of 85% should be attainable when definite endobronchial tumour is visible. Grade B
  • At least five biopsy samples should be taken when endobronchial tumour is visible — to maximise diagnostic yield, biopsy volume, and allow tumour phenotyping and genotyping. Grade D
  • Brushings and washings can increase the diagnostic yield when endobronchial tumour is visible. Grade D
  • A chest CT scan should be performed prior to diagnostic bronchoscopy in patients with suspected lung cancer. Grade D

Pooled sensitivity of bronchoscopic techniques ~88%; biopsy of visible lesions alone 74%; washings 48%; brushings 59%. Biopsy alone 82%, rising to 87% when combined with brushings and washings. At least 5 samples are needed for a 90% probability of positive malignant biopsy. In peripheral lesions biopsied by TBLB, only 45% yield with one biopsy — up to 10 may be required.

Interstitial lung disease & sarcoidosis

  • In suspected sarcoidosis, EBBs should be considered to increase diagnostic yield. Grade C
  • TBLB is recommended for the diagnosis of stage II–IV sarcoidosis. Grade C
  • In patients with diffuse ILD, five to six TBLBs should be taken from the same lung. Grade D
  • Fluoroscopy should be considered for TBLB in patients with localised or focal parenchymal lung disease. Grade D

Diagnostic yield rises with the number of specimens: 1–3 biopsies ~35%, 6–10 biopsies ~69%. TBLB sensitivity in sarcoidosis: 55% stage I, 80% stage II, 82% stage III. In typical HRCT features of IPF, BAL and TBLB are not required for diagnosis.

Bronchoscopists should keep a record of their personal diagnostic accuracy for FB. √

Infection — immunocompromised host

  • In patients with pulmonary infiltrates who are immunocompromised and in whom TB is considered unlikely, BAL alone is usually sufficient. In areas or populations with high TB prevalence, TBLB may be considered in addition. Grade C
  • BAL or bronchial washings should be sent for AFB microscopy and mycobacterial culture in immunocompromised patients with pneumonia. Grade C
  • Post-bronchoscopy sputum could be collected in immunocompromised patients with suspected TB. Grade D
  • TBLB and EBB for invasive aspergillosis may be avoided if BAL galactomannan testing is available — high sensitivity and specificity, and inherent biopsy risks. Grade C
  • In suspected invasive aspergillosis, BAL should be sent for hyphal microscopy and fungal culture; consider BAL galactomannan to further improve yield. Grade C

BAL sensitivity for P. jiroveci 90–98% (falls to ~64% with prior empiric antibiotics) — regarded as the gold standard. BAL galactomannan for invasive aspergillosis: sensitivity 94%, specificity 79%. Avoid TBLB in suspected P. jiroveci pneumonia (pneumothorax risk 2.5–3.7%).

Infection — immunocompetent host

  • Bronchoscopy may be considered in patients with non-resolving or slowly resolving pneumonia, especially current or ex-smokers older than 50 years. Grade C
  • If bronchoscopy is performed for community-acquired pneumonia, BAL specimens should be sent for legionella PCR and atypical pathogens. Grade C
  • Bronchoscopy may be considered if TB is suspected when sputum smear is negative. Grade C
  • In suspected TB, BAL, bronchial aspirates and post-bronchoscopy sputum appear complementary and should all be analysed. Grade C
  • In areas of high or intermediate TB prevalence, patients undergoing bronchoscopy for another indication should have samples sent routinely for TB culture. Grade C

Procedure techniques (Appendix 10)

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL)

Guide the site by available radiology; advance the bronchoscope to the desired site.
Wedge the bronchoscope so the lumen of the bronchus is occluded.
Instil 60–180 mL of normal saline into the segment.
Apply low-pressure suction to retrieve the sample — enough to retrieve without causing airway collapse.
Use tubing with several containers in series attached to the suction pump; some centres use a syringe from the instillation port.

Endobronchial biopsy (EBB)

Advance the bronchoscope until the endobronchial lesion is visualised and secure it in position.
Advance the forceps closed through the working channel.
Open the forceps once visualised at the distal end.
Keeping the bronchoscope still, advance the open forceps towards the abnormality.
Close the forceps, trapping as much tissue as possible.
Retract and remove from the working channel — a "tugging" sensation may be felt.
Remove the sample and repeat 5–6 times.

Endobronchial brush

Insert the brush through the working channel in its retracted position within the protective sheath.
Push the brush out (open) once seen at the distal end.
Advance towards the lesion and move back and forth several times.
Retract into the sheath (closed) and remove from the working channel.
Repeat the procedure.

Transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB)

Advance the bronchoscope as far as possible into the area to be biopsied.
Insert the forceps into the working channel and advance as far as possible.
Retract the forceps 1 cm to avoid biopsying the pleura.
Ask the patient to take a slow deep breath in; open the forceps during inspiration.
Ask the patient to breathe out slowly; advance and close the forceps during expiration.
Remove the forceps and retrieve the sample.
Repeat a further 5–6 times.
Warning Do not take the biopsy if the patient experiences pain when the forceps is pulled back or removed — the pleura may have been caught. Open the forceps and remove without biopsy.

Patient co-operation is necessary for TBLB; patients should be able to follow commands and light sedation is therefore advised.

›12 Decontamination, Staff Safety & Staffing

Key recommendations

  • All personnel involved in cleaning and decontaminating bronchoscopes must receive specific training in infection control and decontamination processes. Grade D
  • Decontamination and disinfection should be carried out at the beginning and end of each list and after each patient use. If drying cabinets/storage chambers are unavailable, decontaminate no more than 3 h before the procedure. Grade D
  • Thorough cleaning, brushing and flushing of all accessible channels with enzymatic or low-foaming detergent remains the most important initial stage. Grade D
  • Single-use suction valves should replace reusable valves wherever possible. Grade D
  • On grounds of staff safety, manual disinfection is no longer recommended; bronchoscopes should be processed in automated endoscope reprocessors (AERs). Grade D
  • Aldehyde-based disinfectants are no longer recommended. Grade C
  • Use sterile or filtered water for the final rinse — tap water is not recommended. Grade D
  • Drying cabinets/storage chambers are recommended for storing clean bronchoscopes; valves must not be attached during storage. Grade D
  • Keep a record of each bronchoscope and reusable accessory used on each patient, plus each step of the decontamination cycle and personnel involved. Grade D
  • There is currently no known decontamination method that prevents transmission of vCJD — record keeping and identification of high-risk cases are advised. √

Staff safety

  • Open troughs of disinfectant are not recommended. Grade D
  • Staff handling disinfectants should always wear full PPE in line with COSHH risk assessment. Grade D
  • Record medical histories including pre-existing asthma, skin and mucosal sensitivities. Grade D
  • Pre-employment baseline spirometry should be measured and recorded; annual spirometry for all personnel directly exposed to disinfectants. Grade D
  • Confirm immunisation against hepatitis B and TB before employment; offer vaccination if necessary. Grade D
  • Do not use hypodermic needles or other sharps to remove tissue samples from biopsy forceps — blunt-ended needles or sterile plastic toothpicks are preferable. Grade D
  • Reusable spiked forceps are not recommended. Grade D
  • In suspected TB, perform bronchoscopy in an appropriately engineered and ventilated area with adequate protection, including masks. √

Nursing skill mix & staffing

  • A minimum of two qualified nurses are required during bronchoscopy: one assistant nurse, and another dedicated to monitoring the patient's response to medication and the procedure. Grade D
  • A qualified nurse is required to recover a patient after bronchoscopy. Grade D
  • Advanced procedures may require additional staff. Grade D
›13 Patient Information, Fasting, Consent & Discharge

Recommendations

  • Verbal and written patient information explaining indications, what to expect during the procedure, and potential complications should be provided to improve tolerance. Grade C
  • Patients should be offered sedation during FB to improve tolerance. Grade B
  • It is sufficient for patients to have no food by mouth for 4 h and to allow clear fluids up to 2 h before bronchoscopy. Grade D
  • Patients who had sedation should be advised not to drive, sign legally binding documents or operate machinery for 24 h after the procedure. √

Consent

  • Practitioners undertaking FB should be familiar with, and adhere to, national and local guidance for obtaining informed consent. √
  • Consent is a process, not a one-off event. Patients need time to read and understand information before FB and should be given the opportunity to ask questions. Patients can change their minds and withdraw consent at any time.
  • The practitioner undertaking the bronchoscopy (or responsible for the trainee) is responsible for ensuring valid consent was obtained. If delegated, the person must be suitably trained and qualified, have sufficient knowledge of the procedure and risks, and agree to act in accordance with national and local guidance.

Aftercare messages for patients

  • Do not eat or drink until the numbness in the mouth/throat has worn off (usually ~2 h).
  • Mild sore throat, hoarseness and cough are common after the procedure.
  • Small amounts of blood may be coughed up — inform the nurse if you cough up more than a tablespoon of blood, have chest pain or difficulty breathing.
  • Arrange for someone to take you home; someone may need to stay with you for a couple of hours if you live alone.
