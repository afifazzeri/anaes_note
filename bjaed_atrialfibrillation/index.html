Perioperative Atrial Fibrillation

Quick reference for anaesthetists & intensivists · prevention, risk stratification, and management

1 · At a glance — key numbers
Peak onset ~48 h post-op 70% occur in first 4 days Non-cardiac surgery: 3–8% (≥45 y) Non-cardiac thoracic: up to 30% Cardiac surgery: up to 80% Isolated CABG: 15–40% Isolated valve: 37–50% Mitral valve: ~50% CABG + valve: up to 70%
Bottom line: AF is the most common serious perioperative arrhythmia. It increases morbidity, mortality, ICU and hospital length of stay, readmission and total healthcare cost. Most POAF is self-limiting (mean 11–12 h; 80% revert spontaneously within 24 h) — so rate control first in the stable patient, and synchronised DC cardioversion if unstable.

Definition & timing

  • POAF = AF occurring after any surgery, typically within 7 days, peaking around 48 h.
  • Up to 70% of cases occur in the initial 4 days.
  • Late-onset POAF (up to 30 days) occurs in 4% of cardiac surgery patients after hospital discharge.
  • Two peak-risk windows after cardiac surgery: immediately after surgery and at 48 h.
  • AF is also the most common arrhythmia encountered in the ICU.

Why it matters

  • Thromboembolic events including stroke.
  • Perioperative cardiac complications: MI, congestive heart failure.
  • Longer hospital and ICU stay, higher readmission rate, higher total healthcare costs.
2 · Pathogenesis

The pathophysiology of POAF after non-cardiac surgery is not fully understood and is likely multifactorial. Increased sympathetic nervous system activation from surgical stress or pain, with catecholamine release, is a significant mechanism. After cardiac surgery, inflammatory changes in cardiac tissue also correlate with POAF (raised CRP, white cell count and interleukins).

ATRIAL FIBRILLATION

Hypoxaemia acts through interrelated mechanisms: direct cellular ischaemia altering atrial conduction velocity and refractoriness with increased anisotropy; and pulmonary vasoconstriction (also from hypercarbia) raising right heart pressures and dilating the right atrium, altering conduction through atrial tissue and dispersing electrical activity.

Other contributory factors: direct surgical irritation or injury, hyper- or hypovolaemia, intraoperative hypotension, anaemia, hypercarbia and metabolic imbalances.

4 · Identification & risk prediction

More accurate prediction of individual risk is paramount to prevention and guided treatment, because of the increased morbidity and mortality associated with POAF. There is no consensus on an accepted risk model to predict POAF.

Score / toolNotes
CHA₂DS₂-VAScDesigned for thromboembolic risk in AF; prospectively and retrospectively validated for predicting AF after cardiac surgery.
POAF scoreIn a retrospective single-centre analysis of several predictive models, the POAF score performed best.
HATCHOne of the established predictive models for POAF.
COM-AFCombines highly predictive variables from the POAF, CHA₂DS₂-VASc and HATCH models; found to have the best discrimination ability compared with the individual models.
Machine learning (ML)Promising. Classical logistic regression struggles with multicollinearity and large numbers of variables; ML models can analyse different correlations and incorporate multiple demographic and clinical variables. A 2023 review of >10 separate models concluded ML can aid prediction of POAF after cardiac surgery and may offer an advantage over conventional risk scores.
High-risk settings to flag preoperatively: advanced age, mitral valve surgery, combined CABG + valve surgery, open abdominal surgery in the elderly, non-cardiac thoracic surgery, CPB, and any patient on chronic beta-blockers or ACEIs.
5 · Prevention & risk reduction
Foundation: maintain homeostasis — euvolaemia and electrolyte balance at all times. Correct any electrolyte abnormality promptly, regardless of perioperative phase. Potassium and magnesium supplementation are especially critical.
Fig 2 (simplified) · Prevention / risk-reduction strategies by phase

Preoperative

  • Continue beta-blocker therapy if already prescribed
  • High-dose statin therapy 7 days before cardiac surgery
  • Optimisation of chronic illnesses / patient risk factors
  • Correct electrolytes; optimise volume status

Intraoperative

  • Avoid / treat known risk factors and triggers (hypoxaemia, hypercarbia, hypotension)
  • Vasopressin has lower AF incidence than noradrenaline as pressor of choice
  • Dexmedetomidine use
  • Steroid use
  • Regional anaesthesia
  • Atrial pacing after cardiac surgery
  • Posterior pericardiotomy

Postoperative

  • Initiation of beta-blocker therapy after cardiac surgery unless contraindicated
  • Amiodarone or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers as second-line agents
  • Prompt correction of electrolytes; avoid unnecessary catecholamines

7 · Management of established POAF
First principle: identify and manage the likely mechanism(s) causing AF. Rhythm or rate control attempts are less likely to be effective if modifiable risk factors (hypoxaemia, hypercarbia, hypotension, volume status, electrolytes, pain, catecholamines) have not been addressed.

Initial management

Scroll for full detail.

Clinical scenarioAction
Unstable Haemodynamic instability, symptoms, or both → synchronised DC cardioversion.
  • Consider pharmacological cardioversion if the patient is haemodynamically stable but cannot obtain adequate rate control, or is intolerant of rate-control medication.
  • Recommend consultation with an electrophysiologist.
  • Options: amiodarone, procainamide, flecainide, propafenone, vernakalant (Europe only — not FDA approved; studied specifically in POAF after cardiac surgery; ESC class IIb).
Stable & asymptomatic Typically self-limiting → pharmacological rate control.
  • Beta-blockers (standard first choice, particularly with concomitant ischaemic heart disease).
  • Alternatives: amiodarone, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (e.g. diltiazem), digoxin — tailored to patient characteristics and comorbidities.

Rate control targets

  • Optimal target is unclear; recent guidelines suggest <100 beats min⁻¹ in stable or chronic AF (based on AFFIRM and RACE II).
  • <80 beats min⁻¹ may be beneficial in symptomatic patients or those with reduced left ventricular systolic function.
  • Specifically after cardiac surgery: target <100 beats min⁻¹ in asymptomatic patients.

Rate vs rhythm control

  • In the general population with asymptomatic, haemodynamically stable AF, conversion to sinus rhythm was historically the treatment of choice — but increasing evidence shows no clinical benefit over rate control.
  • The AFFIRM trial (2002, >4000 patients with non-valvular AF and increased stroke/death risk) showed no difference in survival, complications or duration of hospital stay between rate and rhythm control (using beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers or digoxin).
  • POAF is commonly self-limited (mean 11–12 h), with minimal acute haemodynamic alteration; 80% convert back to sinus rhythm spontaneously within the first 24 h of onset. This supports initial rate control to allow possible spontaneous cardioversion.

Long-term management

StrategyDetail
Electrical or pharmacological cardioversionFor persistent AF; drug choice tailored to specific comorbidities, route and time course.
Ablation therapy
  • Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) — surgical (e.g. MAZE), catheter-based, or hybrid approach.
  • Beneficial for rhythm control in patients with known AF in the perioperative period; surgical/catheter/hybrid ablation provides a long-term treatment option for symptomatic, drug-refractory AF.
  • More effective than pharmacological rhythm control at maintaining long-term sinus rhythm, and confers a mortality benefit in patients with concomitant CHF (CABANA trial).
AnticoagulationFor primary risk prevention of thromboembolic sequelae — see section 8.
8 · Anticoagulation & thromboembolic risk
  • Anticoagulation has been a mainstay for prevention of thromboembolic events associated with AF, especially as an initial measure in the acute setting.
  • The decision to start anticoagulation, and drug choice (based on pharmacokinetics and reversibility), is challenging in POAF: it requires balancing therapy in the hypercoagulable postoperative state against a high bleeding risk if postoperative surgical haemostasis is inadequate.
  • Most guidelines recommend starting anticoagulation in POAF persisting more than 48 h.
  • Assess the balance of risks and benefits using:
    • CHA₂DS₂-VASc — thrombotic risk
    • HAS-BLED — bleeding risk
  • Left atrial appendage exclusion — via surgical ligation (can be performed with other cardiac surgery in patients with known AF before surgery) or minimally invasive catheter-based occlusion — is an option to reduce thromboembolic risk in patients at high risk or known to be intolerant of anticoagulation.

10 · Clinical bottom line
  • Perioperative AF is one of the most common arrhythmias, with far-reaching effects on individual morbidity and mortality and on the healthcare system.
  • Appropriate risk identification followed by institution of prevention strategies is imperative — and in the perioperative setting should involve a multidisciplinary approach (anaesthetists, surgeons, cardiac electrophysiologists), especially in high-risk patients.
  • Machine learning risk prediction models are promising tools for increasing accuracy of POAF prediction.
  • If POAF develops, acute management typically involves rate control, unless the arrhythmia leads to haemodynamic instability or significant symptoms.
  • In haemodynamically significant AF, perform early synchronised DC cardioversion; pharmacologic antiarrhythmic therapy can be instituted in consultation with cardiology colleagues.
  • Long-term management of persistent AF may also require anticoagulation and ablation therapy.
