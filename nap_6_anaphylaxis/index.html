Perioperative Anaphylaxis: For Anaesthetists & Intensivists

1. Epidemiology & Trigger Agents

Incidence: ~1 in 10,000 anaesthetics. (Estimated 1:11,752 in NAP6).

Mortality: 3.8% of Grade 3-5 cases (1 in 26.6 cases).

Key Shift: Antibiotics have overtaken NMBAs as the most common cause. Teicoplanin is associated with the highest per-administration risk, often used in patients with a reported (but often false) penicillin allergy.
2. Clinical Features & Recognition

Presentation (First clinical feature)

  • Hypotension (46%): Universal during the episode. Often the only sign in severe cases.
  • Bronchospasm / High Airway Pressure (18%): More common in asthma, obesity, and with Suxamethonium.
  • Tachycardia (9.8%)
  • Desaturation / Cyanosis (4.7%)
  • Bradycardia (3%)
  • Reduced/Absent Capnography (2.3%)
Diagnostic Pitfalls: Rash is a rare presenting feature, especially in severe cases, and may only appear after resuscitation. Isolated hypotension or isolated bronchospasm can occur. Always suspect anaphylaxis in unexplained, severe hypotension during anaesthesia.

Onset Times

  • NMBAs & Antibiotics: Rapid (usually within 5-10 minutes).
  • Chlorhexidine & Patent Blue Dye: Can be delayed (up to 60 minutes or more).
3. Immediate Management (The "Must-Dos")
  1. Stop the Trigger: Discontinue suspected agents. Remove chlorhexidine-coated CVCs if inserted recently.
  2. Adrenaline: Give immediately. IV route preferred (titrate 50mcg boluses). If IV access is lost, use IM/IO.
  3. Fluids: Rapid IV crystalloid challenge of 20 mL/kg. Repeat as necessary. Avoid colloids.
  4. Airway & Oxygen: 100% oxygen. Airway swelling/difficulty is rare, but intubate if indicated.
  5. CPR: If systolic BP < 50 mmHg, start CPR simultaneously with adrenaline and fluids, even if not in cardiac arrest.
  6. Second-line Drugs:
    • Refractory hypotension: Consider IV Vasopressin 2 Units.
    • Beta-blocker patients: Consider IV Glucagon 1mg.
    • Steroids: Give Hydrocortisone 200mg.
    • Antihistamines: Chlorphenamine 10mg (no clear evidence of benefit/harm, may help rash/urticaria).
Sugammadex: NAP6 could not prove it halts rocuronium anaphylaxis once underway, but it may be considered.

5. Special Populations (Obstetrics & Paediatrics)

Obstetric Patients

  • Incidence: 3.4 per 100,000 anaesthetics.
  • Often occurs in awake patients (neuraxial). Patient reporting "feeling unwell" is a key early sign.
  • Do not withhold Adrenaline due to fears of placental perfusion. Maternal hypotension is the main threat to the fetus.
  • Phenylephrine is often used first due to availability, but Adrenaline is the definitive treatment.

Paediatric Patients

  • Incidence: 2.7 per 100,000 (much lower than adults).
  • Most common presentation: Bronchospasm/High Airway Pressure (64%).
  • No cardiac arrests or deaths in NAP6 paediatric cohort.
  • Atracurium was the most common culprit NMBA.
6. Investigation & Referral

Mast Cell Tryptase (MCT) Sampling

  • Sample 1: As soon as patient is stable (ideally < 30 mins).
  • Sample 2: 1-2 hours post-event.
  • Sample 3 (Baseline): At least 24 hours post-event.
  • Dynamic rise: Peak tryptase > (1.2 x baseline + 2 mcg/L) indicates mediator release.

Referral

  • All patients with suspected anaphylaxis must be referred to a specialist allergy clinic by the consultant anaesthetist in charge.
  • Provide a full list of all drugs given in the 60 minutes prior.
  • Always test for Chlorhexidine and Latex.
7. Departmental & Systemic Preparedness

What every department needs:

  • A named Departmental Lead for Anaphylaxis.
  • Anaesthesia Anaphylaxis Treatment Packs available in all theatres (containing algorithms, pre-filled adrenaline syringes, hydrocortisone, and info on glucagon/vasopressin location).
  • Anaesthesia Anaphylaxis Investigation Packs (tryptase tubes, referral forms, patient letters).
  • Guidelines immediately available (AAGBI or RCUK).
  • Reporting: Must report to local trust systems and MHRA Yellow Card scheme (which is poorly done - only ~24%).
