Part A · Clinical Essentials, Anatomy & Diagnostic Principles

1. Critical Care Pearls & High-Yield Thresholds

Core Points for Intensivists & Anaesthesiologists

  • Necrotising Soft Tissue Infections (NSTI) and Gas Gangrene represent extreme clinical surgical emergencies requiring immediate physiological stabilization, high-dose broad-spectrum antimicrobials, and urgent, radical surgical debridement.
  • Acute Burn Fluid Resuscitation: TBSA > 15% in adults and > 10% in paediatrics warrant Parkland fluid resuscitation (4 mL × TBSA% × kg), delivering the first half within 8 hours of injury.
  • Procedural Pain Control: Wound-related procedures generate severe acute pain. Multimodal analgesia titrated prior to dressing changes or bedside debridement prevents haemodynamic instability and chronic neuropathic transitions.
  • ICU Pressure Injuries (PI): Immobility, vasoactive infusions, and medical devices (endotracheal tubes, NIV masks, central lines) place critical care patients at high risk for Medical Device-Related Pressure Injuries (MDRPI) and Deep Tissue Pressure Injuries (DTPI).
  • Immunonutrition Warning: Arginine supplementation is contraindicated in critically ill patients presenting with active sepsis.

2. Applied Skin Anatomy & Healing Dynamics

Skin Layers & Structural Architecture

The skin represents the body's largest organ, with a thickness ranging between 0.5 mm and 4.0 mm, serving critical regulatory, barrier, sensory, and immunological functions.

  • Epidermis (0.1–1.5 mm): Avascular epithelial layer nourished by diffusion from the underlying dermis. Comprises five distinct strata: stratum germinativum (deepest germinal layer containing basal cells, melanocytes, and Merkel touch receptors), stratum spinosum (keratinocytes, Langerhans immune cells, blister formation plane), stratum granulosum (lipid keratohyalin barrier preventing transepidermal fluid loss), stratum lucidum (transparent layer on palms and soles), and stratum corneum (anucleated cornified layer).
  • Dermis: Provides tensile durability, elasticity, and structural strength. Composed of papillary and reticular connective tissue housing fibroblasts, mast cells, nerve endings, and extensive vascular plexuses (deep reticular plexus giving rise to ascending arterioles and upper subpapillary loops). Dermal perfusion is essential for tissue survival and re-epithelialisation.
  • Subcutaneous Tissue (Subcutis / Hypodermis): Composed of areolar and adipose tissue serving as mechanical cushioning, thermal insulation, and nutritional reserve.

Wound Healing vs Scarring

Superficial wounds confined to the epidermis heal via epithelial regeneration without scar formation. Deeper wounds breaching the full thickness of the dermis heal through granulation tissue formation, wound contraction, and collagen deposition, leaving a permanent fibrous scar. Specialized epidermal appendages (hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous units) do not regenerate if the full dermal depth is destroyed.

Phases of Wound Healing

3. Wound Assessment: TIME & Harikrishna Classification

The T.I.M.E. Principles of Wound Bed Preparation

A systematic framework implemented to identify and treat local pathophysiological barriers to healing:

  • T — Tissue Viability: Differentiate non-viable devitalised tissue (black necrotic eschar, yellow/grey fibrinous slough) from viable tissue (pink epithelialisation margins, healthy red vascular granulation). Mandates sharp, autolytic, or mechanical debridement.
  • I — Infection / Inflammation: Clinical detection of bioburden: expanding erythema, local calor, new onset pain, purulent discharge, wound breakdown, or foul malodour.
  • M — Moisture Imbalance: Assess volume and viscosity of exudate. Desiccation stalls cell migration; macerating high-volume exudate contains destructive matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that degrade extracellular matrix. Balance with moisture-retentive or absorbent dressings.
  • E — Epidermal Margin: Assess wound edges for advancing epithelial borders versus non-advancing, undermined, hyperkeratotic, or rolled margins (epibole).

4. Wound Infection Bacteriology & Diagnostic Sampling

Pathways of Wound Infection

  • Contamination: Transient presence of non-replicating microorganisms that do not cause clinical damage.
  • Colonisation: Multiplying microorganisms adhering to tissue without triggering an overt host immune response.
  • Local Infection: Microbial multiplication breaching local defenses, invading viable tissue, and stimulating host inflammatory response and tissue injury.
  • Systemic Infection / Sepsis: Microorganisms invading the bloodstream and lymphatic vessels, provoking systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), hemodynamic instability, and organ failure.

Microbiological Sampling Protocols

Laboratory Sampling Caveat

Superficial surface swabs have the lowest clinical yield and often merely capture superficial colonizing bacteria. Always obtain tissue biopsies or deep needle aspirates under strict aseptic technique prior to initiating systemic antimicrobial therapy. Never place microbiological culture specimens in formalin.

Table 3 — Microbiological sampling modalities and laboratory containers
Specimen type Indication Collection method & container Transport & handling requirements
Tissue biopsy Deep invasive infection, chronic non-healing ulcers, NSTI, osteomyelitis. Excise a specimen from deep base of debrided wound bed; place in sterile screw-capped container with 2–3 drops of sterile saline. Deliver within 30 min. For anaerobes, utilize Thioglycollate medium or Robertson's Cooked Meat Medium (RCMM). Do not refrigerate.
Pus aspirate / Body fluid Fluctuant abscesses, sinus tracts, deep collections. Aspirate directly using a sterile syringe and needle. Do not collect from drainage collection bags. Deliver immediately in sterile capped syringe or sterile container.
Wound swab Superficial open wounds where tissue cannot be biopsied. Cleanse surface with saline, irrigate away debris. Rotate swab in a zigzag pattern across viable wound tissue. Place into Amies transport medium. Store at room temperature; send to laboratory immediately. Prohibited: Cary-Blair or boric acid containers.

Part B · Critical Care Emergencies & Acute Wounds

5. Life-Threatening Infections: NSTI & Gas Gangrene

Necrotising Soft Tissue Infection (NSTI) Subtypes

NSTI is a rapidly destructive, lethal soft tissue and fascial infection carrying extreme morbidity and mortality. Early clinical presentations often mimic simple cellulitis.

Table 4 — NSTI subclassification and microbiological aetiologies
Subtype Microbiological aetiology Clinical profile & features
Type I Polymicrobial: Mixed aerobes and anaerobes (Bacteroides, Peptostreptococcus, E. coli, Enterobacter). Most common subtype. Frequently seen in immunocompromised, diabetics, post-abdominal/perineal surgery (Fournier's gangrene).
Type II Monomicrobial: Group A beta-haemolytic Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes) ± S. aureus. Known as "flesh-eating bacteria". Can occur in young, healthy individuals after minor blunt or penetrating trauma; high toxic shock incidence.
Type III Marine exposure: Vibrio vulnificus. Acquired through seawater exposure, marine puncture wounds, or raw seafood ingestion; fulminant progression in cirrhotic/hepatic patients.
Type IV Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Aggressive necrosis; necrotising pneumonia or severe skin/soft tissue cavitation.

Gas Gangrene (Clostridial Myonecrosis)

  • Fulminant anaerobic infection caused primarily by Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium septicum, or Clostridium novyi.
  • Non-clostridial gas producers include E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
  • Characterized by intense muscular necrosis, profound systemic toxaemia, hemolytic anemia, and rapid vascular collapse.

Diagnostic "Red Flags" & Bedside Evaluation

  • Pain out of proportion: Severe agonizing pain disproportionate to visible cutaneous physical findings is the earliest hallmark sign.
  • Rapid margin progression: Erythematous borders advancing by centimeters per hour despite initial antibiotic administration.
  • Cutaneous evolution: Skin shifts from tense, shining edema to dusky violaceous discoloration, culminating in hemorrhagic bullae containing thin, foul-smelling, brownish fluid ("dishwater pus").
  • Subcutaneous crepitus: Palpable gas tracking along fascial planes.
  • Bedside Finger Probe Test: Under local anesthesia, make a 2 cm incision down to the deep fascia. Positive test features: absence of bleeding, foul "dishwater pus", and complete lack of tissue resistance when sweeping an index finger along the fascial plane.
  • Radiological imaging: Plain radiographs may show soft tissue gas tracking along fascial compartments. Caution: Imaging must never delay emergency surgical intervention!

Intensive Care & Surgical Resuscitation Protocol

1. Immediate Resuscitation

  • High-flow O₂ / early intubation
  • Invasive arterial line & CVC
  • Balanced crystalloid fluid loading
  • Correct metabolic acidosis & coagulopathy

2. Empirical IV Antibiotics

  • Broad-spectrum coverage
  • Carbapenem / Piperacillin-tazobactam
  • + Vancomycin / Linezolid (MRSA)
  • + Clindamycin (anti-toxin)

3. Radical Surgical Debridement

  • Zero delay to operating theatre
  • Extensive wide fascial excision
  • Debride until healthy, bleeding tissue
  • Mandatory 24-hr re-look exploration
6. Acute Burn Injury & Fluid Resuscitation

Classification of Burn Depth

Table 5 — Burn wound classification by depth of tissue destruction
Degree Depth Clinical appearance Sensation Healing potential
1st Degree Superficial (Epidermis only) Erythematous, dry, no blisters; painful. Intact, hyperaesthetic. Spontaneous healing in < 2 weeks without scar.
2nd Degree (Partial) Superficial to Deep Dermis Pink/red, blistered, moist, marked exudate, oedematous. Exquisitely painful. 2–3 weeks; deep partial may require tangential excision and STSG.
3rd Degree (Full) Entire Epidermis & Dermis into Subcutis White, waxy, leathery, or charred brown/black; dry. Insensate to light touch. Requires surgical excision and skin grafting.
4th Degree Underlying Muscle, Tendon & Bone Charred, black, exposed structural framework. Complete anaesthesia; rhabdomyolysis risk. Extensive flap reconstruction or amputation.

Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) & Parkland Resuscitation

  • Lund and Browder Chart: The gold-standard assessment tool for calculating TBSA. Adjusts head and leg proportions according to paediatric age groups.
  • TBSA Calculation Rules: Only 2nd-degree burns and deeper are included in TBSA; exclude areas of 1st-degree erythema.
  • Resuscitation Threshold: TBSA > 15% in adults, TBSA > 10% in children.

Parkland Resuscitation Formula

Total 24-hour Crystalloid (Hartmann's Solution) = 4 mL × TBSA (%) × Body Weight (kg)

  • First 8 hours: Infuse 50% (half) of the total calculated volume, timed from the exact moment of burn injury (not time of arrival).
  • Subsequent 16 hours: Infuse the remaining 50%.
  • Paediatric requirement: Add maintenance IV fluid (Dextrose 5% in 0.45% Saline) to prevent hypoglycaemia.
  • Titration endpoints: Titrate rate to maintain urine output of 0.5–1.0 mL/kg/h in adults and 1.0–1.5 mL/kg/h in children.

Specialized Burn Referral Criteria (American Burn Association / MOH)

  1. Full-thickness burns > 5% TBSA in any age group.
  2. Partial-thickness burns > 10% TBSA in paediatrics or adults > 50 years.
  3. Partial-thickness burns > 15% TBSA in any group.
  4. Burns involving special areas: face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, or major joints.
  5. High-voltage electrical burns and lightning injury.
  6. Chemical burns with potential cosmetic/functional disability.
  7. Inhalation burn injuries (airway compromise, carbon monoxide/cyanide toxicity).
  8. Pre-existing medical comorbidities complicating recovery.
  9. Concomitant major trauma.

Topical Silver Sulfadiazine (SSD) Safety

Screen for G6PD deficiency prior to applying Silver Sulfadiazine (SSD) cream due to the risk of acute drug-induced haemolysis.

7. Traumatic Wounds, Haemorrhage & Bite Injuries

Haemorrhage Control Priorities

  • Stepwise arterial bleeding control: Direct continuous manual pressure with sterile gauze → Pressure bandage → Arterial tourniquet (time documented, maximum safe ischemic window 90–120 min) → Direct vascular clamping → Topical haemostatic agents (absorbable gelatin, oxidized cellulose, fibrin sealants).
  • Debridement: Gold standard intervention for traumatic wounds. Remove non-viable tissue, particulate debris, and foreign material with copious low-pressure irrigation.

Animal and Human Bite Management

  • Characterized by puncture, crush, and shear forces with deep inoculation of polymicrobial oral flora (Pasteurella multocida, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, Capnocytophaga, anaerobes).
  • Primary Closure Contraindication: Bite wounds must never undergo primary closure due to exceedingly high rates of closed-space abscess and necrotizing infection. Leave open for delayed primary closure or secondary healing.
  • Administer antimicrobial therapy according to National Antimicrobial Guidelines, verify tetanus vaccination, and administer rabies post-exposure prophylaxis where indicated.

Part C · Critical Care Complications & Chronic Wounds

8. ICU Pressure Injuries & Device-Related Damage (MDRPI)

Definition & ICU Etiology

A Pressure Injury (PI) is localized damage to the skin and underlying soft tissues resulting from sustained pressure or pressure combined with shear, typically over bony prominences or related to medical devices. Critically ill patients are at extreme risk due to impaired sensory perception, vasopressor-induced peripheral vasoconstriction, edema, and prolonged immobilization.

Pressure Injury Staging System

Table 6 — Staging of Pressure Injuries and targeted clinical management
Stage Tissue characteristics & anatomical depth Critical clinical actions
Stage 1 Intact skin with localized non-blanchable erythema. Excludes purple/maroon hues. Relieve pressure; 2-hourly turning; apply barrier films/emollients; monitor skin integrity.
Stage 2 Partial-thickness skin loss with exposed pink, moist dermis. Intact or ruptured serum-filled blister. No adipose or deep tissue visible. Protect with hydrocolloid or thin foam; avoid shear forces.
Stage 3 Full-thickness skin loss. Adipose tissue visible; granulation tissue and rolled edges (epibole) frequently present. Bone, tendon, and muscle are not exposed. Debride slough; manage cavity with alginate/gelling fiber; control exudate with foam dressings.
Stage 4 Full-thickness skin and tissue loss with exposed or directly palpable fascia, muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage, or bone. Undermining/tunneling common. Surgical debridement; rule out osteomyelitis; specialized wound fillers; NPWT; plastic surgical reconstructive consultation.
Unstageable Full-thickness tissue loss where true depth is obscured by slough (yellow/tan) or eschar (black/brown). Stable, dry, intact eschar on ischemic heels should not be debrided without revascularization. In other sites, debride to establish stage.
DTPI Deep Tissue Pressure Injury: Persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration; blood-filled blister. Severe underlying tissue necrosis; immediate off-loading; avoid aggressive friction; observe for rapid breakdown into Stage 3/4.
Mucosal PI Pressure injury located on mucous membranes associated with medical devices (e.g. endotracheal tube, nasogastric tube, urinary catheter). Cannot be staged using standard skin criteria. Reposition device; loosen securement tapes; inspect oral/nasal mucosa daily.

Medical Device-Related Pressure Injuries (MDRPI) in Critical Care

  • Endotracheal Tubes & Bite Blocks: Cause severe labial, lingual, and oral mucosal necrosis. Reposition tube from side to side at scheduled 12-hour intervals.
  • Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV / BiPAP masks): High-pressure necrosis over the nasal bridge. Prophylactically apply custom silicone gel sheets or hydrocolloid pads over the nasal dorsum prior to strap tightening.
  • Tracheostomy Flanges & Ties: Inspect peristomal and posterior cervical skin; ensure two fingers can slide underneath ties.
  • Rigid Cervical Collars: Rapid occipital and mandibular breakdown in trauma patients; remove or pad as soon as cervical spine clearance is achieved.

The aSSKINg Prevention Bundle

  • A — Assess risk: Evaluate every ICU admission using standardized risk assessment tools.
  • S — Skin assessment: Inspect all bony prominences (sacrum, heels, trochanters, occiput) at least once per shift.
  • S — Support surfaces: Specialized dynamic alternating pressure air mattresses for high-risk patients.
  • K — Keep moving: Scheduled 2-hourly turning and lateral repositioning (30° tilt).
  • I — Incontinence management: Cleanse promptly; apply dimethicone or zinc oxide barrier film to prevent moisture-associated skin damage (MASD).
  • N — Nutrition & hydration: Ensure adequate caloric and protein delivery via early enteral feeding.
  • G — Giving information: Multidisciplinary team communication.

Stemmer's Sign in Lymphoedema

Inability to pinch and lift a fold of skin at the base of the second toe indicates significant chronic subcutaneous lymphatic fibrosis (Positive Stemmer's sign).

10. Atypical & Malignant Fungating Wounds

Pyoderma Gangrenosum (PG) & The PARACELSUS Score

A non-infectious, destructive neutrophilic dermatosis associated with systemic autoimmunity (inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, hematological malignancies). Surgical debridement is strictly contraindicated due to pathergy (rapid ulcer expansion in response to surgical trauma).

Calciphylaxis (Calcific Uremic Arteriolopathy)

  • Microvascular calcification and thrombosis of subcutaneous arterioles resulting in ischemic necrosis and septal panniculitis.
  • Almost exclusively encountered in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on maintenance haemodialysis; carries 40% to 50% mortality.
  • Presentation: Intensely painful, violaceous mottled purpura advancing to central black leathery necrosis over adiposity-rich areas (thighs, abdomen, breasts).
  • Management: Sodium thiosulfate infusions, discontinuation of calcium-based phosphate binders, cinacalcet for secondary hyperparathyroidism, low-pressure wound care.

Malignant Fungating Wounds & Marjolin Ulcers

  • Marjolin Ulcer: Aggressive squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) developing within chronic burn scars, venous ulcers, or osteomyelitis sinus tracts, often with a latency period > 10 years (37% mortality). Perform deep wedge or punch biopsies from multiple margins.
  • Palliative Management Principles for Fungating Tumours:
    • Haemorrhage Control: Friable tumour vessels bleed easily; apply calcium alginate dressings or silver nitrate cautery; avoid aggressive sharp debridement.
    • Odour Control: Severe anaerobic colonisation generates pungent malodour; apply topical crushed metronidazole powder/gel or activated charcoal dressings.
    • Exudate Management: High-capacity silicone foams changed frequently.

11. Procedural Analgesia, Sedation & Naloxone Protocol

Categorization of Wound Pain

  • Background Pain: Persistent discomfort at rest when the wound is untouched; stems from tissue ischemia, local inflammation, and nerve injury. Requires scheduled round-the-clock analgesia.
  • Incident / Breakthrough Pain: Intermittent pain triggered by routine movement, coughing, or position shifts. Requires PRN rescue doses.
  • Procedural / Operative Pain: Acute, intense pain evoked by wound cleansing, peeling adhesive dressings, wound packing, or bedside mechanical debridement. Mandates pre-emptive analgesia administered prior to starting the procedure.

Emergency Naloxone Rescue Algorithm

Opioid-Induced Respiratory Depression Management

Diagnostic Criteria: Respiratory Rate < 8 breaths/min AND Sedation Score ≥ 2, OR Sedation Score = 3 (Unarousable) ± pinpoint pupils.

  1. Stop all sedative and opioid infusions immediately.
  2. Call for help; administer high-flow oxygen via bag-valve-mask.
  3. Dilute 1 ampoule of Naloxone (0.4 mg / 1 mL) with 4 mL sterile saline to achieve 0.08 mg/mL.
  4. Administer 0.1 mg (1–1.25 mL) IV every 1 to 2 minutes titrated until patient arouses and RR > 10 breaths/min. (Paediatrics: 0.01 mg/kg IV up to max 0.4 mg).
  5. Mandatory ICU Observation: The elimination half-life of Naloxone is short (45–60 minutes), whereas morphine persists for 3–4 hours. Monitor in ICU/HDU for at least 4 hours; be prepared to initiate a continuous Naloxone infusion if resedation occurs.
12. Critical Care Nutrition & Metabolic Support

Energy & Protein Requirements

  • Severe wounds, major burns, and surgical trauma trigger hypermetabolic, hypercatabolic states that rapidly deplete lean skeletal muscle mass and compromise wound tensile strength.
  • Caloric Intake Targets:
    • Normal baseline: 30–35 kcal/kg/day.
    • Underweight / Major trauma: 35–45 kcal/kg/day.
    • Major thermal burns: 40 kcal/kg/day or calculate via the Toronto Formula.
    • Obese patients (BMI > 30): Calculate baseline via the Mifflin-St. Jeor equation using ideal body weight.
  • Protein Targets:
    • Chronic wounds: 1.25–1.5 g/kg/day.
    • Severely catabolic polytrauma, multiple wounds, or Stage 3–4 Pressure Injuries: 1.5–2.2 g/kg/day.

13. Reconstructive Ladder & Principles of Closure

The Modern Reconstructive Ladder

Definitive wound closure proceeds from the simplest, safest technique to increasingly complex surgical transfers. Modern reconstructive surgery integrates intermediate technological rungs (NPWT, dermal scaffolds) to minimize donor site morbidity.

1. Primary / Secondary

  • Direct linear closure
  • Delayed primary closure
  • Secondary intention

2. Advanced Scaffolds

  • Negative Pressure (NPWT)
  • Dermal matrices (Integra)
  • Tissue expansion

3. Autologous Grafts

  • Split-thickness (STSG)
  • Full-thickness (FTSG)
  • Punch / Pinch grafts

4. Flap Reconstruction

  • Local advancement flaps
  • Regional pedicled flaps
  • Free microvascular flaps

14. Wound Cleansing Solutions & Biofilm Efficacy

Cleansing removes exudate, bacterial bioburden, and remnants of previous dressings without damaging newly formed healthy granulation tissue.

Sterile Normal Saline 0.9% Isotonic crystalloid Zero None (mechanical rinsing only). Standard wound irrigation; physiological baseline.
Polyhexamethylene Biguanide (PHMB) Surfactant antimicrobial Low to none High: Surfactant lowers surface tension, penetrating and dissolving Gram-negative biofilms with 15-min soak. Heavily colonized and critically infected wounds; no known acquired bacterial resistance.

Modern dressings maintain a moist physiological healing environment, absorb excess fluid, deliver antimicrobials, and promote cellular migration.

16. Wound Debridement Modalities

Debridement eliminates necrotic barriers, senescent fibroblasts, and microbial biofilms to convert a chronic, non-healing wound into an active, vascularised bed.

Table 15 — Summary of wound debridement modalities
Method Mechanism of action Clinical advantages Contraindications & hazards
Surgical / Sharp Scalpel, scissors, or curette excision of non-viable tissue down to healthy bleeding margins. Rapid conversion; essential for advancing sepsis, NSTI, and thick eschars. Bleeding risk; accidental nerve/vessel injury; requires adequate analgesia/anaesthesia.
Autolytic Endogenous macrophage and neutrophil proteases dissolve slough under moisture-retentive dressings (Hydrogels). Painless, non-invasive, highly selective for necrotic tissue. Slow progression; contraindicated in acute life-threatening infection or neutropenia.
Enzymatic Topically applied exogenous enzymes (Collagenase, Fibrinolysin with DNase) break down fibrinous bonds. Faster than autolysis; well-tolerated in outpatient/ward settings. Requires prescription agents; local periwound irritation.
Mechanical Physical force (Wet-to-dry gauze peeling, pulsed lavage, whirlpool). Removes thick debris. Non-selective: tears away new capillaries and epithelial buds; painful.
Biological (Larval) Sterile medical maggots (Lucilia sericata) secrete digestive proteases and ingest bacteria. Disinfects MRSA biofilms; spares living tissue; stimulates micro-angiogenesis. Psychological distress/aversion; avoid adjacent to large blood vessels.
Hydrosurgical High-pressure saline jet cutting tool with simultaneous vacuum evacuation (Versajet). Ultra-precise tangential debridement in operating theatre; reduces operative time. Equipment cost; risk of deep tissue aerosolisation; requires GA/regional block.
17. Advanced Therapies: NPWT, NPWTi-d & HBOT

Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT) Principles

Continuous or intermittent subatmospheric pressure applied across a sealed wound bed generates powerful mechanical and biological responses:

  • Macrostrain: Physical mechanical retraction drawing wound margins together in 3 dimensions; collapses dead space.
  • Microstrain: Microscopic cellular deformation at the foam-tissue interface triggering cellular mitosis, angiogenesis, and rapid granulation tissue proliferation.
  • Interstitial Edema Reduction: Removal of excess interstitial fluid and systemic inflammatory mediators (TNF-alpha, MMPs), restoring microvascular capillary perfusion.

NPWT Variations: NPWTi-d & Incisional iNPWT

  • NPWT with Instillation and Dwell (NPWTi-d): Automatically delivers topical antiseptic or saline solutions onto the wound bed, allows a programmed dwell time (typically 3–10 minutes) to loosen slough and bioburden, then re-applies continuous subatmospheric suction. Highly effective for infected or contaminated cavitary wounds.
  • Closed Incisional NPWT (iNPWT): Applied over primarily sutured surgical incisions under tension; splints incision edges, off-loads lateral shear stresses, and dramatically reduces surgical site infections (SSI) and seroma/haematoma formation.
Table 16 — Standard subatmospheric pressure protocols for NPWT
Clinical indication Recommended subatmospheric pressure Suction mode Dressing change interval
Pressure Injuries & Acute Wounds -125 mmHg Continuous for 48 hours, then switch to intermittent (5 min ON / 2 min OFF). Every 48–72 hours.
Mesh Skin Graft Fixation -50 to -75 mmHg Continuous pressure. Leave undisturbed for 4–5 days.
Chronic Vascular / Diabetic Ulcers -50 to -75 mmHg Continuous pressure. Every 3–5 days.
Compromised Flaps -125 mmHg Continuous pressure until flap adhesion achieved. Every 48 hours.

Absolute NPWT Contraindications

  1. Direct placement over exposed vital structures: Direct foam contact with exposed major arteries, veins, vascular anastomoses, or intestinal viscera can cause erosion, severe fistulisation, and exsanguinating hemorrhage.
  2. Malignant tissue in the wound bed: Increases local tumor proliferation through angiogenesis pathways.
  3. Untreated osteomyelitis or gross non-debrided necrotic tissue: Must undergo sharp surgical debridement before NPWT placement.

Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBOT) in Critical Wound Management

  • Administration of 100% medical oxygen at pressures > 1.4 atmospheres absolute (ATA), typically 2.0 to 2.8 ATA for 90 minutes per daily session (15–30 sessions).
  • Mechanism: Multiplies dissolved plasma oxygen by up to 20-fold independently of haemoglobin; raises hypoxic wound tissue PO₂ above the 40 mmHg critical threshold required for neutrophil oxidative burst and bacterial killing; stimulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and mobilizes bone marrow stem cells.
  • Approved Indications: Refractory diabetic foot ulcers, acute necrotising fasciitis, gas gangrene, acute crush injuries/compartment syndrome, threatened compromised flaps/grafts, and osteoradionecrosis.
Table 17 — HBOT contraindications and clinical complications
ClassificationClinical conditionsCritical action / precaution
Absolute Contraindication Untreated Pneumothorax Mandatory tube thoracostomy prior to hyperbaric chamber pressurisation to avoid fatal tension pneumothorax.
Relative Contraindications COPD with bullae, severe asthma, active upper respiratory tract infection, uncontrolled congestive heart failure, claustrophobia, uncertified pacemakers. Consult hyperbaric physician; ensure echocardiography and chest radiography review.
Complications Middle ear and sinus barotrauma, transient hyperoxic myopia, acute hypoglycemia in diabetics, central nervous system oxygen toxicity (grand mal seizures). Monitor blood glucose prior to dive; instruct on middle ear autoinflation; slow decompression protocols.

1 Clean, healthy granulating < 25% No Dry / Minimal All conventional or inert dressings; hydrogel, transparent film. Avoid silver/charcoal. No Ready for secondary wound closure or heal by secondary intention.
