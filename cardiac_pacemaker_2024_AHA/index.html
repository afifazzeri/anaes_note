★Start here: the four questions

Before any procedure, answer these four questions. They determine everything that follows.

1 · What device?

Pacemaker, ICD, CRT, leadless, subcutaneous/extravascular ICD, or loop recorder — and which manufacturer?

2 · Where is it?

Right/left infraclavicular, abdominal, or midaxillary. Is the surgical site or grounding pad near it?

3 · Pacing dependent?

Check interrogation within 3 months and a 12-lead ECG. Dependency can increase under sedation.

4 · Above the umbilicus?

Procedures above the umbilicus carry the highest risk of electromagnetic interference (EMI).

The single most important safety step Re-enable defibrillator tachyarrhythmia detection and therapies before the patient leaves a monitored setting. Deaths have been reported after failure to reactivate ICD therapies following elective procedures.

The periprocedural cycle

CIED care centre (procedure planning, remote monitoring) → Preprocedure (optimise settings, prepare, test magnet) → Intraprocedure (monitoring, emergency readiness) → Postprocedure (restore settings) → back to the CIED care centre for follow-up.

Involvement of the cardiology clinic that primarily manages the device improves both patient safety and continuity of care. Planning begins as soon as the decision to operate is made — not on the morning of surgery.

1Preprocedural screening & interrogation

Patient screening checklist

  • Presence and location of the CIED — right/left infraclavicular, abdominal, or midaxillary region.
  • Device type — transvenous or leadless pacemaker; transvenous, subcutaneous or extravascular defibrillator; cardiac resynchronisation device; or implantable cardiac monitor.
  • Indication and arrhythmic history — sick sinus syndrome, AV block, primary/secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death, VT/VF, sinus node dysfunction, tachy-brady syndrome, SVT, AF, or cause of cardiomyopathy.
  • Physical examination — jugular venous pressure, pulmonary auscultation, lower-extremity oedema, and condition of the CIED pocket if applicable.
  • Other sources of EMI — nerve stimulators, cochlear implants, programmable hydrocephalus shunts, left ventricular assist devices, cardiac contractility modulation devices.
  • New arrhythmia events recorded by the device.
  • Extracardiac conditions affecting intraprocedural safety — acute or chronic kidney dysfunction, hepatic failure, acute/subacute neurological impairment, pulmonary insufficiency, and impediments to vascular access.
Finding the device details Sources include the patient's device card, recent interrogation or remote transmission, the electronic health record, and the chest x-ray. Chest x-ray also distinguishes an ICD from a pacemaker: the radiopaque coil on the lead is the hallmark of a defibrillator.

What to interrogate

An interrogation within 3 months of the planned procedure may be reviewed to assess recent device activity. Specific elements:

  • Cardiac rhythm status — underlying rhythm, baseline heart rate, and dependency on pacing support.
  • Battery life, thresholds, impedances, sensing parameters, and history of prior arrhythmias.
  • Whether the device is MRI conditional, and at what magnet strength.
  • Testing and confirmation of magnet response.

Additional screening

Active or ongoing heart failure symptoms — orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea — warrant assessment for atrial or ventricular arrhythmias and optimisation of cardiac status before the procedure. A high burden of atrial or ventricular arrhythmias, or recent ICD therapy (antitachycardia pacing or shocks), may warrant further medical evaluation unless the operation is urgent or emergent.

2Procedure & surgical site risk

Above vs. below the umbilicus

SiteEMI riskPractical implication
Above the umbilicus Higher Consider inhibiting ICD therapies; consider asynchronous pacing if pacemaker dependent; test magnet response preoperatively.
Below the umbilicus Lower Unlikely to cause EMI provided the return/grounding pad is also below the umbilicus.
CIED generator in the abdomen Variable Consult the electrophysiology/CIED team about cautery; reprogramming may be needed because of proximity to the generator.

Cautery

  • Unipolar cautery is the principal source of EMI. Oversensing of EMI may cause failure of pacing output or false detection of arrhythmias.
  • True bipolar cautery — frequently used by ocular and plastic surgeons — is safe and has no adverse effects, unless applied directly to a CIED.
  • Place the grounding/return pad far away from the CIED — ideally on the contralateral lower limb — to keep the current path away from the device.
Risk mitigation — the standard bundle
  • Keep the current path away from the CIED; return electrode on the contralateral lower limb.
  • Use bipolar electrosurgery whenever possible.
  • Minimise monopolar bursts to ≤ 5 seconds.
  • Avoid whole-body return electrodes.
  • Remember: a low battery increases the risk of electrical current intrusion (protection circuitry depends on battery current).
3Pacemaker dependency

Pacing dependency may change according to the programmed pacemaker mode, depth of anaesthesia, changes in autonomic activity, and administration of other pharmacological agents. It may increase under sedation.

Assessing dependency

  • Review interrogation within 3 months — the burden of atrial, ventricular, or dual-chamber pacing helps determine dependency.
  • A 12-lead ECG can determine whether intrinsic conduction (underlying rhythm) or pacing is present.
  • Patients who are not pacing dependent may still have an underlying rhythm inadequate for the haemodynamic demands of the procedure — careful observation may be used if sinus node dysfunction with a stable rhythm is present.

If the patient is pacing dependent

  • Programme an asynchronous pacing mode — AOO, VOO or DOO — according to hospital preoperative workflow, or
  • Apply a magnet continuously, assuming a stable position can be maintained and the generator is not within the sterile field.
Important Magnet application causes pacing at a proprietary, manufacturer- and battery-specific rate, with variability between leadless and transvenous devices. Not all pacemakers respond. If magnet application is planned, test the response preoperatively in a monitored environment.
4Defibrillator (ICD / CRT-D) management
  • For procedures above the umbilicus, defibrillators may be inhibited preoperatively to avoid inappropriate shocks due to EMI.
  • Continuous application of a magnet inhibits arrhythmia detection and therapy but does not affect pacing function. Oversensing of EMI may therefore still inhibit pacing during magnet application.
  • Removal of the magnet restores arrhythmia detection to its original settings.
  • If a stable magnet position is difficult to maintain — due to patient positioning or the extent of the sterile field — reprogramming may be necessary.
  • Some magnet responses are programmable; check the setting and magnet response routinely before the proposed procedure.
Critical workflow requirement There must be a designated workflow to ensure ICD therapies are re-enabled before the patient is discharged from a monitored setting.
Manufacturer nuance — Biotronik After 8 hours of continuous magnet application, tachycardia detection and therapy are automatically re-enabled. Unlike all other manufacturers, there is no audible tone when the magnet is applied.

Magnet checklist

  1. Appropriate patient position.
  2. Magnet not in sterile field.
  3. Test magnet mode activation preoperatively.
  4. If leadless pacemaker — may not have a magnet mode.
  5. If ICD — the magnet does not affect the pacing mode (reprogramming is required to change pacing).
6Special devices: leadless, S-ICD, EV-ICD, ILR, CSP

Leadless pacemakers

  • Entirely contained within the heart — may not be seen or palpated on examination. A careful history is required to identify them.
  • Two systems are commercially available in the US; identify the company and specific type, because behaviour (especially magnet response) differs.
  • Usually interrogated before the planned procedure for pacing burden, battery life, ventricular sensing amplitude, capture threshold, and device impedance. Repeat assessment after the procedure.
  • Ventricular leadless devices provide ventricular pacing with or without rate response (VOO, VVI, VVIR). Devices with accelerometers (Medtronic Micra AV) may also provide AV synchrony via atrial mechanical sensing (VDD, VDI).
  • Unlike transvenous devices, leadless pacemakers can be programmed "off".
Key anaesthetic consideration Magnet response may be absent or difficult to activate because of device position or body habitus. The Medtronic Micra VR and AV have no magnet response. If the patient is pacemaker dependent and the procedure involves EMI above the umbilicus, programme the device to asynchronous ventricular pacing (VOO) to avoid sensing of noise.

When the Abbott AVEIR magnet mode is activated, it paces asynchronously (VOO) at 100 bpm for 5 seconds, followed by a fixed rate determined by battery voltage. When the magnet is removed, the device returns to its previous settings. The Abbott AVEIR DR (dual-chamber leadless) paces in DOO with magnet application; a single atrial leadless device converts to AOO.

Rate response differs by manufacturer: Micra series increase rate via an accelerometer; Abbott series increase rate according to the temperature of blood returning to the heart.

Subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD) and extravascular ICD (EV-ICD)

  • Implanted outside the vascular system: pulse generator in the extrathoracic space along the left midaxillary line, with a single lead tunnelled to the midline.
  • S-ICD shock coil lies superficial to the sternum; EV-ICD shock coil lies deep to the sternum.
  • Both use widely spaced electrode recordings, making them more susceptible to EMI than transvenous bipolar sensing configurations.
  • Neither provides long-term bradycardia pacing. Both provide temporary "post-shock pacing" after defibrillation. The EV-ICD additionally provides antitachycardia pacing and temporary pause-prevention pacing at 40 bpm for pauses > 5 s (programmable up to 15 s).
  • Avoid including the pulse generator and defibrillator electrode in the electrocautery current path to the grounding pad.
  • To inhibit EMI-induced shocks or pacing: place a magnet over the pulse generator, or programme the defibrillator "off".
  • For the S-ICD, best magnet response has been shown with direct placement of a ring-shaped magnet.

Loop recorders / implantable cardiac monitors

  • Implanted subcutaneously in the left upper chest; may be palpated and identified on chest x-ray.
  • If the insertion site lies within the planned surgical incision, removal may be considered.
  • Electrocautery, defibrillation, radiofrequency ablation, lithotripsy, and nerve stimulators can cause inappropriate episode storage or inhibit episode storage.
  • Preprocedural data retrieval may be needed to avoid loss of symptomatic preprocedural events. Postprocedural data may contain artefacts — accurate reporting of procedural date/time is essential.

Conduction system pacing

Leads implanted within the conduction system usually produce a narrower ventricular-paced QRS than traditional RV or biventricular pacing. His bundle pacing sites may carry a higher risk of elevated pacing thresholds or microdislodgement compared with RV apical or left bundle branch pacing sites.

Lead dislodgement & vascular instrumentation

  • Surgical instruments or guide wires can dislodge leads or leadless pacemakers, causing loss of pacing or inappropriate shocks.
  • Avoid central line access on the ipsilateral side of a CIED to reduce the risk of vascular stenosis and infection.
  • Recently implanted leads and leadless devices are at higher risk of dislodgement.
  • With an ICD — especially dual-coil leads — upper-body central venous catheter placement risks inducing ventricular arrhythmias or oversensing from guide wire interference. High-voltage shorting from guide wire contact with coils can cause ICD circuitry issues.
7EMI sources & their effects

General effects of EMI

  • Oversensing of electrosurgical energy.
  • Initiation of noise-reversion mode (asynchronous pacing).
  • Initiation of electrical reset mode.
  • Permanent damage to, or failure of, the CIED pulse generator.
  • Damage to the lead–myocardial interface causing an increase in pacing thresholds.

Most common problem during surgery:

  • Pacemakers: oversensing of EMI and device inhibition, which may lead to inappropriately high heart rates due to tracking.
  • ICD: false detection of tachyarrhythmia and inappropriate therapy.

Device reset occurs infrequently with electrosurgery. Pulse generator damage from electrosurgery can occur but is uncommon.

Unipolar sensing & pacing

CIED sensing must occur between two electrical references. Most commonly this is between closely spaced electrodes in the heart (bipolar configuration). Some transvenous devices may be programmed to sense between an electrode in the heart and the device generator (unipolar configuration). Transvenous devices with unipolar sensing are more susceptible to EMI, with greater potential for oversensing.

8Intraprocedural management
  • Continuous telemetry monitoring for all patients with CIEDs for the duration of the procedure.
  • Because of possible electrical interference with telemetry recordings, use plethysmography or arterial blood pressure monitoring.
  • Consider storing telemetry strips for expert review after the procedure if concerns arise about device malfunction or arrhythmia detection.
  • Cardiac pacing spikes seen on telemetry are added by an algorithm and may not be identified or displayed if pacing output is low — this is often not a reliable means of monitoring pacing output.
If a magnet is used intraprocedurally
  • The magnet must be continuously applied directly to the CIED without contaminating the sterile field.
  • The device returns to its prior settings as soon as the magnet is no longer detected.
  • While ICD therapies are deactivated, external defibrillation pads must be in place in case of ventricular arrhythmias or need for pacing.

Plans should be made for rapid detection and treatment of ventricular arrhythmias whenever ICD therapies are temporarily deactivated or pacing settings altered — including immediate availability of an external defibrillator and external transcutaneous pacing device.

If programming changes are performed, they should be completed under the direct supervision of a physician or health care professional experienced in periprocedural CIED management. Industry-employed allied professionals may assist with logistics and technical aspects, but should not be primarily responsible for perioperative recommendations.

9Postprocedural care
Non-negotiable Ensure the device is returned to its prior settings after the procedure. Patient deaths have been reported after failure to reactivate ICD therapies following elective procedures.
  • Once the magnet is removed, the CIED returns to its previous settings.
  • If reprogramming is required to reinstate tachyarrhythmia detection and therapies, continuous monitoring until the device therapy is re-enabled is useful.
  • Place all reprogramming documentation in the electronic health record.
  • In the event of major intraprocedural events — cardiac arrest, ICD therapy delivery, external defibrillation, or suspected device reset — interrogate the device postprocedurally.
  • In the absence of any major procedural events, the patient can return to routine device follow-up.
  • Communicate with the CIED team about any device changes or reprogramming to ensure safe continuity of care.
  • For radiofrequency ablation, check sensing, impedance and threshold testing after the EP study; loop recorders are usually not reprogrammed, but note that the patient underwent a cardiac procedure that may produce recorded arrhythmias, artefact, or noise.
10Emergency use of magnets

In the preprocedural period there may be an increased risk of scenarios leading to unwanted ICD therapy aside from EMI:

  • Supraventricular tachycardia, sinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter.
  • Oversensing secondary to lead fracture.
  • T-wave oversensing.
  • Presence of mechanical circulatory support devices.

In the absence of haemodynamic instability and malignant ventricular arrhythmia, a magnet can be used to cease unwanted tachytherapies from an ICD as a bridge to further treatment and device reprogramming.

A magnet can be applied to a pacemaker to temporarily increase the heart rate through asynchronous pacing, if the magnet response rate exceeds the current intrinsic heart rate. However, asynchronous ventricular pacing may be detrimental in some clinical cases.

Caveats
  • Magnet mode may be disabled in some devices — test the response before the procedure.
  • Not all devices produce an audible tone to confirm activation.
  • Magnet behaviour may become unpredictable when battery voltage falls below end-of-life values.
  • Be aware of the magnet mode timeout — some devices revert to original programming after a set period.
11MRI in patients with a CIED
  • Improvements in CIED structure, electronics, and programmed algorithms, plus changes in MRI protocol design over the past decade, have created a relatively safe environment for performing MRI in patients with CIEDs.
  • Institution-specific protocols improve safety of MRI studies and MRI-based procedures.
  • Most — but not all — contemporary devices are considered MRI conditional: they carry FDA labelling for performance under MRI conditions provided certain prespecified conditions are met, including periprocedural monitoring and programming when indicated.
  • MRI-conditional labelling must apply to the entire system. Abandoned leads, or any part that is not MRI conditional, render the whole system non-conditional.
✓Considerations for clinical practice

Pacemaker-dependent patients

Electrocautery can cause EMI, leading to oversensing of non-physiological signals and inhibition of pacing — particularly when surgery is performed above the umbilicus. (Patients with CIED generators in the abdomen may require reprogramming because of proximity to the generator.)

To prevent this, the pacemaker may be programmed to an asynchronous mode (DOO, AOO, or VOO), or a magnet can be placed over the device during the procedure — assuming a stable magnet position can be maintained and the pulse generator is not within the sterile surgical field.

Note: application of a magnet to an ICD has no effect on the pacing function.

Patients with ICDs

To prevent inappropriate therapies due to EMI, tachyarrhythmia detection should be programmed "off". If a programmer is not available, a magnet may be placed over the pulse generator during the procedure.

It is essential to re-enable detection of tachyarrhythmias before discharge from a monitored setting.

Magnet response in leadless pacemakers

Unlike transvenous pacemakers, the Micra leadless pacemaker does not have a programmed response to a magnet. Therefore, if a patient is pacemaker dependent and a procedure will involve EMI above the umbilicus, the device may be programmed to VOO asynchronous pacing before the use of electrocautery.

Although some leadless pacemakers do have an asynchronous magnet response, the magnet mode may be limited by patient body habitus — testing before entering the operating room may be considered. If a stable position cannot be maintained, reprogramme to an asynchronous mode before electrocautery.

Emergency magnet use

In addition to magnet mode programming, magnets may be useful in certain emergency scenarios, including avoiding inappropriate shocks due to haemodynamically stable rhythms, EMI, or ICD lead fracture — or increasing the heart rate to the pacemaker magnet response rate before a device programmer is available.

Communication with the CIED team

Before the procedure, notifying the device team about the upcoming procedure may facilitate generation of a periprocedural plan for the CIED. Postprocedural communication about any device changes or reprogramming ensures patient safety and safe continuity of care.

Ongoing research & future directions Research on optimal workflows for perioperative CIED management is limited. Remote programming may be an option in the future but is not currently available with most devices. Team-based care is essential: an integrated, multidisciplinary model including cardiology, anaesthesia, surgery and electrophysiology — with nurses, advanced practice clinicians and physicians — with the patient at the centre of the framework.
