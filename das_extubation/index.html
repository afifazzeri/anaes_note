Tracheal extubation is a high-risk phase of anaesthesia. It is not simply the reversal of intubation: conditions at extubation are often less favourable, and there is a transition from a controlled to an uncontrolled situation.

~1 in 3Major anaesthesia-related airway complications in NAP4 occurred during emergence or recovery.
UnchangedASA closed-claims for injury at extubation/recovery did not fall after difficult-airway guidelines (unlike induction claims).
Higher riskDeath or brain injury was more common in extubation/recovery claims than in induction claims; obese and OSA patients over-represented.

Problems related to airway reflexes

  • Exaggerated reflexes — breath-holding, coughing, bucking; hypertension, tachycardia, raised venous, intra-ocular and intracranial pressures.
  • Laryngospasm — protective exaggeration of glottic closure; triggered by blood, secretions, surgical debris, light anaesthesia, airway/upper-abdominal stimulation. Propofol-based TIVA may reduce incidence.
  • Consequences if unrelieved — post-obstructive (negative-pressure) pulmonary oedema and hypoxic cardiac arrest. Lower-airway equivalent = bronchospasm.
  • Reduced / obtunded reflexes — pharyngeal tone loss and obstruction (particularly obesity and OSA), aspiration and airway soiling, late obstruction after opioids in OSA. Residual neuromuscular blockade (TOF 0.7–0.9) impairs pharyngeal function and hypoxic ventilatory response.
  • Dysfunctional reflexes — e.g. paradoxical vocal cord motion.

Physiological compromise in other systems

  • Hypertension, tachycardia and myocardial ischaemia.
  • Raised venous pressure (haematoma, suture disruption), raised intra-ocular and intracranial pressure.

Human factors

The environment at extubation is less favourable than at intubation: equipment, monitoring and assistance may be inadequate. Distraction, time pressure, operator fatigue, poor communication and lack of skilled help all compound patient factors.

Mechanical / anatomical causes of obstruction

  • Glottic oedema (glottis is the narrowest adult airway; posterior glottis supports the tube) — from oversized or poorly positioned tubes, over-inflated cuffs.
  • Supraglottic swelling → posterior displacement of epiglottis → inspiratory obstruction.
  • Subglottic/tracheal oedema, haematoma, haemorrhage, distorted anatomy, surgical oedema.
  • Biting on the tube → occlusion → negative-pressure pulmonary oedema.

Two questions aid decision-making and determine whether extubation is low-risk or at-risk:

  1. Are there airway risk factors? — Was the airway normal/uncomplicated at induction? Has the airway changed?
  2. Are there general risk factors?

Low-risk extubation

  • Routine / uncomplicated
  • Airway normal or uncomplicated at induction and unchanged at end of surgery
  • No general risk factors
  • Fasted

At-risk extubation

  • Ability to oxygenate/ventilate uncertain after tube removal
  • Reintubation potentially difficult
  • And/or general risk factors present

Airway risk factors

  • Pre-existing airway difficulty — difficult at induction (anticipated or unanticipated) and possibly worse intra-operatively; includes obesity, OSA and aspiration risk.
  • Peri-operative airway deterioration — normal at induction but now difficult: distorted anatomy, haemorrhage, haematoma, oedema from surgery, trauma or non-surgical causes.
  • Restricted airway access — straightforward at induction but limited at end of surgery (shared airway, halo fixation, mandibular wiring, implants, cervical spine fixation).
  • Lower airway — trauma, oedema, infection, secretions; consider CXR to exclude bronchial intubation, pneumothorax, surgical emphysema if intubation was difficult or oxygenation suboptimal.

General risk factors

  • Impaired respiratory function
  • Cardiovascular instability
  • Neurological / neuromuscular impairment
  • Hypo- or hyperthermia
  • Abnormal clotting, acid–base balance or electrolytes
  • Special surgical requirements (coughing/straining → haematoma, ↑ICP, ↑IOP, suture disruption)
Smooth emergence matters surgically. Coughing and straining raise venous pressure (haematoma, airway compression, suture disruption) and intra-ocular/intracranial pressure, and can provoke ischaemia in severe coronary disease.

Also consider

  • Gastric distension splints the diaphragm — decompress with oro/nasogastric tube if high-pressure facemask or supraglottic ventilation was needed.
  • If the rescue plan involves subglottic access, confirm the neck is accessible.

An outline extubation plan should be in place before induction of anaesthesia and reviewed throughout and immediately before extubation.

Planning checklist

  • Was the airway normal at induction? Has anything changed?
  • Any general risk factors that may complicate or preclude extubation?
  • Is there a clear rescue plan for ventilation and reintubation with minimum difficulty and delay?
  • Where should extubation happen (theatre, recovery, HDU/ICU)? Who needs to be present?

Preparation aims at final optimisation of airway, general and logistical factors so that extubation is performed under the best possible conditions. Preparation plus planning enables risk stratification and should always precede extubation.

Airway factors — reassess before extubation

  • Would bag–mask ventilation be achievable? Assess oedema, bleeding, clots, trauma, foreign bodies and distortion by direct or indirect laryngoscopy.
  • Beware: a tracheal tube in situ can give a falsely optimistic view of the larynx; oedema can progress rapidly.
  • Cuff-leak test — a large audible leak with the cuff deflated is reassuring; absence of a leak around an appropriately sized tube generally precludes safe extubation. Exercise caution even with a leak if airway oedema is suspected. Spirometry is sensitive but lacks specificity.

General factors

  • Full neuromuscular reversal — target train-of-four ratio ≥ 0.9 using a peripheral nerve stimulator (accelerometer more accurate than visual assessment). Sugammadex antagonises rocuronium more reliably than neostigmine.
  • Correct cardiovascular instability; ensure adequate fluid balance.
  • Optimise temperature, acid–base balance, electrolytes and coagulation.
  • Provide adequate analgesia.

Logistical factors

  • Extubation should be carried out in a controlled manner with the same standards of monitoring, equipment and assistance as at induction.
  • Safe extubation can take as long as safe intubation — allow for this in list scheduling.
  • Communication between anaesthetist, surgeon and theatre team is essential.
  • Additional resources may be required for the at-risk patient (HDU/ICU, ENT/surgical support, difficult-airway trolley).

Any technique must ensure minimum interruption of oxygen delivery to the lungs, avoid airway stimulation where possible, and have a back-up plan for ventilation and reintubation.

  • Pre-oxygenation — raise FiO₂ to 1.0 (or as close as possible; aim FeO₂ > 0.9) to maximise pulmonary oxygen stores and cover the apnoeic period. Although FiO₂ 1.0 increases atelectasis, maximising stores is the priority at extubation.
  • Patient position — no universal evidence. Head-up (reverse Trendelenburg) or semi-recumbent is increasingly used and gives mechanical advantage in obesity. Left-lateral, head-down is traditional for the non-fasted patient.
  • Suction — under direct vision (ideally with a laryngoscope) to avoid oropharyngeal trauma. Be vigilant for blood in the airway — the "coroner's clot" can cause obstruction and death. Consider endobronchial suction and gastric tube aspiration.
  • Alveolar recruitment — sustained PEEP and vital-capacity breaths may temporarily reverse atelectasis. Deflating the cuff and removing the tube at the peak of a sustained inflation produces a passive exhalation and may expel secretions and reduce laryngospasm/breath-holding.
  • Bite block — prevents tube occlusion if the patient bites. If biting occurs, deflate the cuff so gas can flow around the device and negative pressure cannot build up. If rolled gauze is used, tape/tie it to the tube.
  • Awake vs deep — awake extubation is generally safer (airway tone, reflexes and respiratory drive return). Deep extubation reduces coughing, bucking and haemodynamic response but increases upper airway obstruction; it is an advanced technique reserved for easy airways with no aspiration risk.

Pharmacological adjuncts

  • Remifentanil infusion — drug of choice for cough suppression during emergence; allows a tube-tolerant, awake, obeying patient. Requires careful titration and close monitoring (see at-risk section).
  • Alfentanil, fentanyl, morphine — blunt cough and cardiovascular response.
  • Lidocaine — topical at intubation, into the cuff, or IV before extubation (some benefit).
  • Esmolol, nicardipine, magnesium, clonidine, ketamine, beta-blockers — attenuate cardiovascular response.
  • Doxapram for laryngospasm — limited evidence; causes cardiovascular stimulation.
  • Steroids — for inflammatory oedema (see post-extubation care).

No extubation is risk-free, but in routine extubation reintubation is expected to be manageable without difficulty if needed.

An at-risk extubation is one where risk stratification has identified general and/or airway risk factors suggesting the patient may not maintain their own airway after tube removal, and airway management may not be straightforward should reintubation be required.

Examples: emergency aortic aneurysm repair (full stomach, cardiovascular instability, acid–base derangement, temperature) — general factors. Head and neck surgery after awake fibreoptic intubation (previous radiotherapy) — airway factors.

Awake extubation

The technique is the same as for the low-risk patient and is suitable for most at-risk patients (e.g. aspiration risk, obesity, many difficult airways).

1 · Laryngeal mask exchange (Bailey manoeuvre)

Replaces the tracheal tube with an LMA to maintain a patent, unstimulated airway with stable physiology and protection from blood/secretions. Emergence profile is superior to awake or deep extubation. Useful where cardiovascular stimulation from the tube could disrupt surgical repair, and in smokers, asthmatics and irritable airways. Inappropriate if reintubation would be difficult or if regurgitation risk exists. Described originally with the Classic LMA; data for other supraglottic devices are lacking.

Table 4 — Sequence for LMA exchange in at-risk extubation
#Step
1Administer 100% oxygen
2Avoid airway stimulation: either deep anaesthesia or neuromuscular blockade is essential
3Perform laryngoscopy and suction under direct vision
4Insert a deflated LMA behind the tracheal tube
5Ensure LMA placement with the tip in its correct position
6Inflate the cuff of the LMA
7Deflate the tracheal tube cuff and remove the tube whilst maintaining positive pressure
8Continue oxygen delivery via the LMA
9Insert a bite block
10Sit the patient upright
11Allow undisturbed emergence from anaesthesia

Variants: remove the tracheal tube before LMA insertion after laryngoscopy and pharyngeal suction; or pass a flexible fibrescope through the LMA to confirm position and observe vocal cord motion (useful after thyroid/parathyroid surgery). For nasotracheal tubes, the LMA may be inserted from the side of the tube, or the tube removed first.

2 · Remifentanil extubation technique

Tracheal tube presence can trigger coughing, agitation and haemodynamic disturbance — undesirable in neurosurgery, maxillofacial/plastics and significant cardiac or cerebrovascular disease. Remifentanil infusion attenuates these responses, producing a tube-tolerant patient who is fully awake and obeys commands. Remove the hypnotic agent well in advance and titrate remifentanil to avoid coughing (too low) or delayed emergence/apnoea (too high).

3 · Airway exchange catheter (AEC) assisted extubation

AEC provides continuous airway access when reintubation is likely to be difficult. Inserted through the tracheal tube before extubation, it acts as a guide for reintubation and a route for oxygen insufflation. The only device commercially available in the UK for this purpose is the Cook Airway Exchange Catheter (11 and 14 FG; 83 cm; ID 2.3/3.0 mm).

Table 6 — Sequence for use of an airway exchange catheter
#Step

3Perform pharyngeal suction before removing the tracheal tube
4Remove the tracheal tube over the AEC while maintaining AEC position (do not advance it)
5Secure the AEC to the cheek or forehead with tape
6Record the depth at the teeth/lips/nose in the notes

Respiratory deterioration with an AEC in situ

  • Identify and treat the cause.
  • If due to upper airway obstruction: high-flow oxygen by facemask only (not via the AEC); standard airway manoeuvres/adjuncts; mask CPAP (move the AEC to the corner of the mouth for seal); nebulised adrenaline.
  • Heliox can temporise airway swelling.
  • Oxygen insufflation through the AEC only in extremis — risk of barotrauma. Confirm the tip is above the carina and that there is a route for exhaled gas. Flow should not exceed 1–2 L/min. Reintubation will usually be required.
AEC risks: barotrauma (including death), direct tracheal mucosal perforation, interstitial pulmonary emphysema and dislodgement (poor compliance, poor supervision, inadequate fixation, airway manipulation). High-pressure source (jet) ventilation via an AEC is a last resort — only with a leak permitting expiration, minimal effective inflation pressure, short inspiratory time, and a device with a pressure sensor that stops flow above a pause pressure of 10–20 cmH₂O.

Postpone extubation

Extubation is entirely elective. Delay may allow airway oedema to resolve and may be the best option to match skilled personnel with the period of greatest risk (e.g. avoid extubating a very difficult airway late in the evening). It is sensible if a return to theatre within 24 hours is likely.

If the patient is transferred to a critical care area, there must be a written emergency reintubation plan (as recommended by NAP4).

Elective surgical tracheostomy

Consider when airway patency may be compromised for a considerable period due to pre-existing problems, the nature of surgery (e.g. free-flap reconstruction) or the extent of tumour, swelling, oedema or bleeding. Discuss between anaesthetist and surgeon during planning/preparation.

Decision informed by:

  1. Extent of airway compromise at the end of surgery
  2. Likelihood of postoperative airway deterioration (usually swelling)
  3. Ability to rescue the airway
  4. Expected duration of significant airway compromise

Tracheostomy reduces glottic damage compared with long-term tracheal intubation, and allows rapid emergence without fear of unplanned extubation or failure to reintubate.

Prophylactic (rescue) subglottic cannula

Transtracheal cannulae do not provide a definitive airway, but if inserted at induction for an anticipated difficult airway they may be left in situ. Oxygen insufflation or high-pressure ventilation can be life-saving, but balance against barotrauma, displacement, obstruction/kinking, trauma, haemorrhage and infection. Requires the same level of postoperative care and monitoring as a tracheostomy. Can remain in place up to 72 hours.

Life-threatening complications after extubation are not restricted to the immediate postoperative period — anaesthetists have a continuing duty of care.

Transfer & staffing

  • Administer oxygen during transfer; consider portable monitoring if the recovery area is distant or the patient unstable.
  • Trained staff until airway reflexes have returned and the patient is physiologically stable. One recovery nurse per patient, never fewer than two personnel in recovery. An appropriately skilled anaesthetist must be immediately available.
  • Communication: clear verbal handover and written instructions for recovery and ward/HDU; in high-risk cases brief the on-call team and have a written airway management plan.
  • A calm atmosphere and reassurance help — anxiety increases work of breathing.

Observations and warning signs

  • Conscious level, respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, SpO₂, temperature and pain score.
  • Capnography (using a specially designed facemask) may aid early detection of airway obstruction.
  • Pulse oximetry is not a monitor of ventilation — never rely on it as the sole monitor.
  • Early problems: stridor, obstructed breathing pattern, agitation; surgical issues (drain losses, free-flap perfusion, airway bleeding, haematoma, airway swelling).
  • Late problems: mediastinitis and airway injury. Mediastinitis after airway perforation presents with severe sore throat, deep cervical or chest pain, dysphagia, painful swallowing, fever and crepitus — advise patients to seek help if these occur.
  • Airway trauma most commonly involves larynx (routine intubation), pharynx and oesophagus (difficult intubation); pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum/surgical emphysema present in only ~50%.
Never ignore a patient who is agitated or complains of difficulty breathing, even if objective signs are absent.

Equipment, location and safe transfer

  • A difficult airway trolley must be immediately available, plus relevant items such as clip removers and wire cutters.
  • Standard monitoring continued in recovery; capnography available.
  • All extubations supervised by an anaesthetist. At-risk extubation should occur in the operating theatre. Patients with airway concerns should stay in recovery or go to a critical care environment.
  • Transfer supervised by an anaesthetist. Transferring at-risk ICU patients to theatre for extubation may be appropriate to ensure equipment and expertise.

Respiratory care for airway compromise

  • Nurse upright; high-flow humidified oxygen; end-tidal CO₂ monitoring desirable.
  • Keep starved — laryngeal competence may be impaired despite full consciousness.
  • Avoid factors impeding venous drainage; encourage deep breaths and coughing to clear secretions.
  • In OSA, a nasopharyngeal airway may overcome upper airway obstruction; if the patient uses CPAP at home, have it available.

Steroids

Steroids reduce inflammatory airway oedema (surgical/anaesthetic/thermal/chemical) but have no effect on mechanical oedema from venous obstruction (e.g. neck haematoma). All steroids appear equally effective at adequate dose — equivalent to hydrocortisone 100 mg every 6 hours. Start as soon as possible in patients at high risk and continue for at least 12 hours. Single-dose steroids immediately before extubation are ineffective.

Stridor / upper airway obstruction

  • Nebulised adrenaline 1 mg may reduce airway oedema.
  • Heliox may help but limits FiO₂.

Analgesia

  • Good analgesia optimises respiratory function; avoid or cautiously titrate sedative analgesia; effective antiemesis is important.

Documentation & future management

  • Record clinical details and instructions on the anaesthetic chart.
  • Document difficulties in the "Alerts" section of the notes and in the local difficult intubation database.
  • Record details of airway management and future recommendations.
  • Send a letter to the GP and give a copy to the patient (DAS Airway Alert form), with a full explanation when they can retain it.
  • Warn about delayed symptoms of airway trauma and advise seeking medical help.
  • Advise patients with difficult airways to register with an accessible, dependable database such as MedicAlert.

Most commonly seen post-extubation in theatre or recovery, but can occur with a supraglottic airway in situ. Appropriate equipment, monitoring and personnel should be available throughout the theatre suite.

#Step
1Call for help
2Apply continuous positive airway pressure with 100% oxygen using a reservoir bag and facemask whilst ensuring the upper airway is patent. Avoid unnecessary upper airway stimulation
3Larson's manoeuvre: place the middle finger of each hand in the laryngospasm notch (between the posterior border of the mandible and the mastoid process) whilst displacing the mandible forward in a jaw thrust. Deep pressure may relieve laryngospasm

8Atropine may be required to treat bradycardia
9In extremis, consider a surgical airway

Key facts

  • Negative intrathoracic pressure from forceful inspiration against an obstructed airway → post-obstructive (non-cardiogenic) pulmonary oedema.
  • Commonest cause is laryngospasm (> 50%); also biting on a tracheal tube or LMA occluding the lumen.
  • Occurs after 0.1% of all general anaesthetics; more common in young muscular adults (M:F ≈ 4:1).
  • Presents with dyspnoea, agitation, cough, pink frothy sputum and low oxygen saturations; diffuse bilateral alveolar opacities on CXR.
  • Prompt diagnosis and management usually resolves clinically and radiologically within a few hours; delayed presentation up to 2.5 hours has been described. Death is rare and usually due to hypoxic brain injury at the time of obstruction.
  • Prevention: bite block during emergence; if biting occludes the tube, deflate the cuff to allow some gas flow and reduce negative intrathoracic pressure.
Table 8 — Management of post-obstructive pulmonary oedema
#Action
1Treat the cause: relieve the airway obstruction
2Administer 100% O₂ with full facial CPAP. CPAP reduces oedema formation by increasing mean intrathoracic pressure, minimises alveolar collapse, improves gas exchange and reduces work of breathing
3Nurse the patient sitting upright
4Fulminant oedema with critical hypoxaemia → tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation with PEEP. Less severe hypoxia responds to supplemental oxygen, non-invasive ventilation or CPAP
5Intravenous opioids may reduce subjective dyspnoea
6Chest radiography may exclude other complications of difficult airway management and other causes of hypoxia (gastric aspiration, pre-existing infection, pneumothorax, barotrauma, pulmonary collapse)
7Frank haemoptysis may necessitate direct laryngoscopy and/or flexible bronchoscopy
8Diuretics are often administered, but their efficacy is unproven
