Neuraxial Anaesthesia for the Parturient with Intracranial Pathology

1 · Key Points at a Glance
  • CPP = MAP − ICP. Normal CPP 60–100 mmHg in pregnant and non-pregnant adults. Normal ICP 7–15 mmHg.
  • Sustained ICP of 20–25 mmHg is generally treated; > 40 mmHg is life-threatening.
  • Neuraxial analgesia is the most common (77%) and most effective technique for labour pain in the USA, and is preferred for caesarean delivery (CD).
  • Pregnancy, neuraxial anaesthesia and general anaesthesia (GA) can all cause acute changes in ICP.
  • Pathologies raising ICP: preeclampsia 6–8% of pregnancies, head trauma 0.5–6%, ischaemic/haemorrhagic stroke 0.01–0.03%, intracranial venous thrombosis 1:50,000, tumours ~3.5 per million births; Chiari malformation ~0.8% radiological prevalence.
  • Evidence is limited to case reports and retrospective reviews → individualised, multidisciplinary, shared decision-making.
Bottom line: Neuraxial techniques are usually safe when there is no mass effect and no obstruction to CSF flow. They are relatively contraindicated when dural puncture could create a pressure gradient across an obstructed compartment.
2 · Intracranial Physiology — General Principles
  • Cerebral blood flow (CBF) depends on cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP): CPP = MAP − ICP.
  • Significant falls in CPP (↓MAP or ↑ICP) → cerebral hypoperfusion and ischaemia.
  • Monro–Kellie doctrine: the skull is a rigid, fixed volume (~1500 mL) containing brain tissue (80%), blood (10%) and CSF (10%). An increase in one component requires compensatory decrease in the others.
  • Hydrocephalus: increased CSF accumulation — obstructive (↑ICP) or non-obstructive (normal ICP). Causes include tumours, congenital malformations, infection, post-haemorrhagic obstruction of CSF resorption.
  • Choroid plexus produces ~20 mL CSF per hour; absorption at arachnoid granulations of cerebral veins. Raised venous pressure (venous thrombosis, jugular compression) reduces reabsorption.
  • Any blockage at the foramen magnum → obstructed CSF flow → ↑ICP and possible herniation.
Remember: The two questions that determine neuraxial safety are mass effect and CSF flow obstruction.
3 · Pregnancy & Labour Factors Affecting ICP
  • Resting CSF pressure in pregnancy is similar to non-pregnant adults.
  • Uterine contractions increase CSF pressure by ~30 mmHg (↑ central blood volume and MAP).
  • Pushing in the second stage transiently raises CSF pressure by ~53 mmHg — the largest increase.
  • Aortocaval compression from ~20 weeks (earlier in multiple pregnancy) ↓ preload, cardiac output and MAP → use 15–30° left uterine displacement.
  • Uteroplacental perfusion is not autoregulated; it depends on arterial pressure.
  • Vasopressors (phenylephrine, ephedrine, noradrenaline) are well studied for short-term use in CD without fetal harm. Target haemodynamics within 10–15% of baseline.
  • Progesterone increases cerebral CO₂ sensitivity → chronic respiratory alkalosis with metabolic compensation; normal PaCO₂ ~4 kPa (4.0–4.3 kPa).
  • Pain relief attenuates maternal hyperventilation (which causes uterine artery vasoconstriction) and blunts catecholamine-driven ICP surges.
  • But: systemic opioids and sedatives can cause hypoventilation and hypercarbia → cerebral vasodilation → ↑ICP.
Ventilation sweet spot: Avoid extreme hypocapnia (cerebral & uteroplacental vasoconstriction, left-shifted maternal Hb dissociation curve) and hypercapnia (↑ICP). Aim PaCO₂ 3.3–4.0 kPa.

5 · Neuraxial Procedures — Risks & Precautions
  • Neuraxial risks in intracranial pathology: acute and chronic CSF leak with changes in CPP and transcompartment pressure gradients, plus cerebral hypoperfusion from spinal/epidural hypotension.
  • Untreated spinal hypotension → maternal nausea/vomiting, fetal acidosis, lower Apgar scores, compromised CPP. Treat rapidly with IV fluids + prophylactic vasopressor infusion.
  • Unintended dural puncture (DP) during epidural placement occurs in 0.5–1.5%, even with experienced operators.
  • A 17-G epidural needle leaves a much larger dural hole than a 25-G spinal needle → larger, more acute CSF leak and greater pressure-gradient change.
  • Epidural fluid boluses may transiently ↑ICP. In non-pregnant patients with raised ICP, ICP rises within < 1 min; lasting ~4.5 min after a 10 mL bolus vs ~2.8 min after 5 mL.
  • For CD under spinal: give 1 L IV crystalloid plus a prophylactic vasopressor infusion; keep BP within 10% of baseline.
  • About one-third of parturients with intracranial pathology undergo CD; some require GA. Weigh risks and benefits of both, with shared decision-making.
Historical vs current view: Neuraxial procedures were traditionally considered contraindicated with raised ICP because of herniation risk after DP. Current literature suggests raised ICP without an obstructive component does not automatically preclude neuraxial anaesthesia — e.g. 224 patients with cerebral venous thrombosis (83% with raised ICP) had no difference in death or neurological worsening after lumbar puncture.
6 · General Anaesthesia — Considerations

Why GA is higher risk in pregnancy

  • Difficult intubation 1:49; failed intubation 1:808.
  • ↑ aspiration risk: raised intra-abdominal pressure from the gravid uterus, ↓ lower oesophageal sphincter tone, delayed gastric emptying (pain and opioids in labour).
  • ↓ functional residual capacity + ↑ oxygen consumption → rapid desaturation during apnoea at induction, even with optimal preoxygenation.
  • Potent inhaled anaesthetics cause uterine relaxation → atony and postpartum haemorrhage, and ↑CBF.
  • Accidental awareness under GA for CD: 1 in 212.

Modifications for raised ICP

  • Blunt the stress response to direct laryngoscopy with opioids or antihypertensives.
  • Plan a smooth emergence — avoid coughing and bucking at extubation.
  • Maintain PaCO₂ and MAP at target; avoid hypoxia and hypotension.
7 · Specific Conditions — Quick Reference Table
ConditionKey pointsNeuraxial / anaesthetic implication
Obstructive hydrocephalus / CSF flow obstruction Communicating vs non-communicating. Obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus with an intracranial mass = obstruction along the ventricular system. Aqueductal stenosis is the commonest non-tumoural obstructive type and may be undiagnosed until adulthood. Contraindication to dural puncture when due to a mass lesion (herniation risk). LP is generally well tolerated in isolated aqueductal stenosis.
Brain tumours Pregnancy hormones may accelerate growth of glioblastomas and meningiomas; relative immunosuppression, vascular growth, ↑blood volume and cardiac output contribute to tumour growth and surrounding oedema. Repeat MRI within 2 months of planned delivery. Labour epidurals are reported successful — but avoid DP where there is mass effect, ventricular compression and ↑ICP. Fatal herniation reported after unintentional DP. Tumour without mass effect → spinal/epidural acceptable.
Chiari malformation (CM) Type 1 commonest (0.8% radiological prevalence); symptoms rare (~1/1000): headache, neck pain, visual change, hearing difficulty, gait ataxia. May be associated with syringomyelia (CSF collection in cord) suggesting intermittent foramen magnum obstruction. Kyphosis (↑GA difficulty) and scoliosis (↑neuraxial difficulty). Higher CD and GA rates but similar morbidity vs controls. No reported fatal or devastating herniation after neuraxial procedures in CM. Usual care if no foramen magnum crowding and no CM symptoms; neurosurgery often advises CD if syringomyelia/significant symptoms — shared decision-making.
Spontaneous intracranial hypotension Spontaneous spinal CSF leak; incidence ~4 per 100,000 per year; may be exacerbated by pregnancy. Brain sagging may mimic CM. LP historically avoided to prevent worsening brain sagging — but CSF pressure is already low, so further ICP decrease from unintended DP is unlikely.
Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) ↑ICP without structural cause; likely insufficient CSF resorption. Commonest in women of childbearing age, often with obesity (1–2 per 100,000). Opening pressure ≥ 25 cmH₂O (18.4 mmHg). No contraindication to neuraxial procedures — no intracranial–spinal pressure gradient and no obstructive structure. LP is often therapeutic.
Arteriovenous malformation (AVM) Direct low-resistance, high-flow arteriovenous shunts. Rupture risk may ↑ with higher oestrogen and cardiac output; AVM sequelae account for 5–12% of maternal deaths. Neuraxial considered safe in stable AVM — avoids pain-related ICP surges and the induction/intubation response of GA.
Preeclampsia Multiorgan disease with CNS involvement: cerebral oedema and ↑ICP on imaging. Severe features show ↑ICP in 19–43% based on optic nerve sheath diameter. No reports of preeclampsia-related ↑ICP causing herniation after DP. Labour epidural and neuraxial anaesthesia remain preferred with close haemodynamic control. Autoregulation threshold is shifted → maintain a higher BP for maternal and fetal perfusion.
8 · Decision-Making & Risk Assessment (Figure 1 Simplified)

Before delivery

  • Anaesthesia consultation several weeks before delivery: obtain records, imaging and neurosurgical opinion.
  • Multidisciplinary team — obstetrics, neurosurgery, anaesthesia — to discuss mode of delivery and analgesia/anaesthesia options, including postpartum.
  • Review imaging for mass effect (Table 2 below), which suggests ↑ICP and herniation risk with DP. Repeat imaging within 2 months for tumours.
  • Neurosurgical consultation should cover: symptoms, pathophysiology, herniation risk with DP (24–27 G spinal vs 16–18 G epidural needle), risk of acute ICP surges, and mode of delivery (tolerance of second-stage Valsalva).

9 · Risk Modification, Safety & Monitoring

Reducing physiological risk

  • Reduce ICP before delivery — e.g. an external ventricular drain was used to lower and monitor ICP, allowing spinal anaesthesia and awake neurological monitoring for CD in a patient with a cerebellopontine angle mass.
  • Use atraumatic pencil-point spinal needles — smaller (≤ 25 G) needles leak less and for shorter periods.
  • In vitro CSF flow through human dura: 65% of 29 G Quincke holes had no flow within 5 min vs only 10% after 22 G dural puncture. A 26 G Quincke needle had a median CSF loss of 0.12 mL min⁻¹ (range 0–1.5).
  • Maintain CPP: strict BP monitoring, keep within 10–15% of baseline, prophylactic vasopressor infusion.
  • Neuraxial ultrasound for anticipated difficult placement increases first-pass success and may reduce complications. Use a senior/experienced operator to reduce unintentional or multiple DPs.

Monitoring

  • Labouring or postpartum patients with raised ICP: hourly neurological checks.
  • Consider 1:1 nursing or ICU admission based on symptoms and likelihood of deterioration.
Safety net: With increasing risk of a disease process that could produce acute herniation from acute CSF leak, neuraxial analgesia/anaesthesia becomes relatively contraindicated.
10 · Alternative (Non-Neuraxial) Pain Relief

Consider when neuraxial techniques are contraindicated, or when the patient elects to avoid them after shared decision-making.

Systemic opioids — remifentanil PCA

  • Provides better pain control than traditional non-neuraxial options for labour; commonly used in Europe.
  • Typically reserved for contraindications to neuraxial analgesia because of the higher risk of maternal respiratory depression.
  • Mitigate risk with 1:1 nursing, continuous pulse oximetry and continuous end-tidal CO₂.
  • Neonatal outcomes (Apgar, NICU admission, resuscitation) are comparable with other methods.
  • In intracranial pathology, remember that respiratory depression → hypercarbia → ↑ICP.

Nitrous oxide

  • Can increase CBF, cerebral metabolic rate and ICP.
  • No difference in outcomes vs controls during aneurysm clipping in non-pregnant patients.
  • Concerns should be part of interdisciplinary discussion and shared decision-making for labour analgesia or scheduled CD under GA.

Fascial plane blocks

  • Transversus abdominis plane (TAP) or quadratus lumborum (QL) blocks ± catheters after CD when intrathecal opioids cannot be given.
  • Use as part of multimodal analgesia; reserve systemic opioids for rescue only.
11 · Managing Potential Complications

Deterioration of mental status

  • Clinical emergency — activate the brain decompensation rapid response team where available.
  • Assess and treat causes (e.g. ↓MAP / ↓CPP). Ensure a neurosurgeon is aware when a patient with ↑ICP or a significant intracranial mass starts labouring or is scheduled for CD.
  • Acute standard actions: elevate the head (avoid positions impairing intracranial venous outflow), hyperventilate, give IV mannitol.
  • Balance positioning: avoid aortocaval compression (left uterine displacement) while optimising preload to maintain fetal perfusion.
  • Cautious hyperventilation to end-tidal CO₂ 3.3–4.0 kPa.
  • Mannitol in pregnancy has not been studied in humans but appears to cross the placenta in animals; IV mannitol 0.25–0.5 g kg⁻¹ has been reported without adverse effects. Hypertonic saline (not studied in pregnancy) may also be considered.

Unintentional dural puncture

  • If it occurs with non-obstructive intracranial pathology: monitor closely for acute neurological change.
  • Options: place an intrathecal catheter via the epidural needle, or withdraw and resite at the same or a different level (most choose the latter).
  • Keep the patient supine with left uterine displacement to minimise the intracranial–spinal canal pressure gradient.
  • Acute boluses of preservative-free normal saline into the epidural/intrathecal space can temporarily raise spinal canal pressure and reduce the gradient.
  • If discharged postpartum: follow up for at least one week with strict return-to-hospital instructions for any neurological deterioration.
  • Post-DP headache: treat symptomatically. Epidural blood patch is an individualised risk/benefit decision based on the intracranial pathology.
12 · Postpartum & Postoperative Care
  • Immediately after delivery the uterus contracts → ↑ central blood volume and cardiac output to its peripartum peak. This is the highest-risk period for intracranial haemorrhage.
  • The vasodilation of neuraxial anaesthesia moderates this immediate rise until the block wears off (typically 1–2 h).
  • IV diuretics after delivery can help mitigate the acute increase in circulating volume.
  • Treat postpartum/postoperative pain with multimodal analgesia; use systemic opioids only as 'rescue' (risk of hypoventilation → ↑ICP).
  • Maintain vigilance and increased monitoring for the first 48 h — increased cardiac output, fluid shifts and acute pain needs raise the chance of adverse events.
13 · Conclusions
  • Pregnancy and the physiological changes of labour, pain and haemodynamic swings may affect ICP, especially with coexisting disease.
  • Neuraxial analgesia may not be contraindicated in milder intracranial disease such as Chiari type 1 malformation.
  • Alternative labour pain relief methods can also have harmful effects on ICP.
  • GA for CD has physiological effects on ICP and cerebral haemodynamics.
  • Patients with significant intracranial pathology are best served by interdisciplinary discussion of the individual pathology — with specific attention to intracranial mass effect, obstruction to CSF flow and risk of herniation with dural puncture — within a shared decision-making framework.
