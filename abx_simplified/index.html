1 · Core principles

▶ PK/PD in one page foundation

Time-dependent (T > MIC)

Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams).

Maximise the duration above MIC → frequent dosing, prolonged or continuous infusion (e.g. piperacillin/tazobactam, meropenem, cefepime, ceftazidime).

Concentration-dependent (AUC/MIC)

Aminoglycosides (once-daily), fluoroquinolones, vancomycin (AUC/MIC 400–600), daptomycin, metronidazole.

Maximise the peak / total exposure relative to MIC.

Static vs cidal

Cidal: beta-lactams, vancomycin (slow), fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, metronidazole, daptomycin.

Static: macrolides, tetracyclines, linezolid, clindamycin.

Cidal preferred in endocarditis, meningitis, neutropenic sepsis.

Reading a susceptibility report

S susceptible · SDD susceptible dose-dependent (needs high dose, e.g. cefepime) · I intermediate · R resistant.

MIC numbers are not comparable between different drugs — each drug reaches different concentrations.

ICU pearl: an MIC at the top of the susceptible range + a drug that only just achieves that concentration at the infected site (e.g. lung, CSF, abscess) = treatment failure. Escalate dose (prolonged infusion) or change drug.
▶ Absorption, distribution & the critically ill patient high yield
  • Bioavailability matters when switching IV→PO. High (>90%) and roughly 1:1 with IV: metronidazole, levofloxacin, linezolid, fluconazole, doxycycline, clindamycin, TMP-SMX, fluconazole, moxifloxacin. Lower: ciprofloxacin (~80%, higher oral dose used), cefuroxime (~40%), aciclovir (~25%).
  • Chelation. Tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones and integrase inhibitors bind Ca, Mg, Al, Fe, Zn — separate from enteral feeds, antacids and mineral supplements by ≥2 h. A "nil by mouth" ICU patient on nasogastric feed is a classic trap.
  • Acid suppression. Itraconazole capsules, posaconazole suspension, atazanavir and rilpivirine need gastric acid — avoid co-prescribing PPIs/H2RAs.
  • Food. Improves: posaconazole suspension, itraconazole capsules, atazanavir, darunavir, rilpivirine, atovaquone. Impairs: voriconazole tablets, itraconazole solution, omadacycline, rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide.
  • Distribution. Protein binding reduces tissue/CNS penetration (ceftriaxone is highly bound, but 2 g q12h still exceeds the meningitis MIC). Macrolides concentrate in lung macrophages. Metronidazole penetrates CNS well; clindamycin does not.
  • Septic shock / vasopressors / abscess / devitalised tissue all reduce delivery to the infection site — source control is part of the prescription.
  • Obesity. Dose aminoglycosides on ideal or adjusted body weight; many beta-lactams need loading doses. Weigh your patient, don't guess.
  • Renal replacement. CRRT clearance differs from intermittent haemodialysis — follow your local renal dosing protocol for beta-lactams and vancomycin.
Renal dosing traps: ceftriaxone needs no adjustment (dual renal/biliary elimination) but moxifloxacin is not renally cleared and is not for UTI. Avoid nitrofurantoin if CrCl < 50 mL/min. Most beta-lactams, vancomycin and aminoglycosides do need adjustment — and need re-escalation when renal function recovers.

▶ Resistance, collateral damage & C. difficile stewardship
  • ESKAPE threats: Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter — plus Candida auris, CRE, C. difficile, drug-resistant gonorrhoea.
  • Resistance genes travel on plasmids — one conjugation event can confer multiple resistances at once.
  • Antibiotic pressure: killing susceptible strains lets resistant ones expand. Collateral damage: the same dose hits the patient's whole microbiome.
  • Highest C. difficile risk: clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins. Any antibiotic can do it.
Stewardship in four rules: don't treat colonisation/contamination · use the narrowest effective agent · use the right dose · use the shortest effective course (CAP 5 d, HAP 8 d, intra-abdominal 4 d, cellulitis 5–6 d, pyelonephritis 5–7 d, uncomplicated gram-negative bacteraemia 7 d).
▶ The treatment pathway (prophylaxis → empiric → definitive)

Prophylaxis = no infection yet (e.g. cefazolin before surgery — one dose, never >24 h). Empiric = suspected infection, organism unknown. Definitive = known organism and susceptibilities.

2 · Antibacterials

▶ Beta-lactams — the shared rules overview
  • All inhibit peptidoglycan cross-linking (penicillin-binding proteins) → autolysis. Combining two beta-lactams adds nothing and is not antagonistic (few exceptions).
  • All can cause hypersensitivity: rash → drug fever → acute interstitial nephritis → anaphylaxis. Cross-reactivity between different beta-lactam subclasses is lower than traditionally taught (<3%); side-chain similarity drives it.
  • Seizures at high dose, especially with renal impairment. Always check renal function.
  • All are destroyed by beta-lactamases — resistance emerges to every new beta-lactam eventually.
  • No atypical cover (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia) — add a macrolide/tetracycline for CAP.
  • No MRSA cover — except ceftaroline.
Neurotoxicity: cefepime can cause non-convulsive status epilepticus (any dose, worse with renal impairment); imipenem has the highest seizure risk of the carbapenems. Unexplained encephalopathy on a beta-lactam → get an EEG.
▶ Natural penicillins penicillin G / V

Agents: penicillin G IV, penicillin V PO, benzathine & procaine penicillin (IM depot).

Spectrum: good — Treponema pallidum, most streptococci incl. S. pneumoniae; moderate — enterococci; poor — almost everything else (staphylococci are almost universally resistant).

Key points: very short half-life (<2 h) — frequent dosing or continuous infusion. Never give procaine or benzathine penicillin IV — can be fatal. Drug of choice for syphilis, including neurosyphilis.

Oral penicillin and IV penicillin for CNS infection share a breakpoint of 0.06 mcg/mL; IV penicillin for other sites is 2 mcg/mL. Penicillin is a poor empiric choice today.
▶ Antistaphylococcal penicillins MSSA

Agents: nafcillin IV, oxacillin IV, dicloxacillin PO, methicillin (historical).

Spectrum: good — MSSA, streptococci; poor — gram-negatives, enterococci, anaerobes, MRSA.

Key points: hepatically eliminated (no renal adjustment). Cause phlebitis and a higher incidence of acute interstitial nephritis. "MSSA = OSSA = NSSA" — susceptibility to one implies the others.

ICU pearl: beta-lactams kill MSSA faster than vancomycin. If your patient has MSSA bacteraemia and no severe beta-lactam allergy, switch to cefazolin or an antistaphylococcal penicillin — this improves outcomes in serious infection.
▶ Aminopenicillins ampicillin / amoxicillin

Agents: ampicillin IV, amoxicillin PO.

Spectrum: good — streptococci, enterococci (E. faecalis almost always susceptible; E. faecium usually resistant); moderate — enteric GNRs, Haemophilus; poor — staphylococci, anaerobes, Pseudomonas.

Key points: use ampicillin IV / amoxicillin PO (amoxicillin is better absorbed and dosed less often). High incidence of diarrhoea orally. Beta-lactamases have eroded gram-negative utility — if a GNR is susceptible to ampicillin alone, don't add a beta-lactamase inhibitor.

Good for: enterococcal infection, streptococcal pharyngitis, otitis media, susceptible GNRs.

▶ Penicillin / beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations

Agents: ampicillin/sulbactam IV, amoxicillin/clavulanate PO, piperacillin/tazobactam IV.

Spectrum: MSSA, streptococci, enterococci, many anaerobes, enteric GNRs; Pseudomonas only with piperacillin/tazobactam; poor against MRSA.

Key rule: the inhibitor restores activity, it does not add it. Amp/sulbactam has no Pseudomonas cover (ampicillin doesn't); pip/tazo does.

  • Sulbactam has useful activity against Acinetobacter baumannii — high-dose ampicillin/sulbactam is used for this.
  • ESBL bacteraemia: the MERINO trial showed a carbapenem beat piperacillin/tazobactam — use a carbapenem for serious ESBL infection.
  • Great empiric choices for mixed aerobic/anaerobic infection (intra-abdominal, diabetic foot, aspiration pneumonia); poor definitive choices when something narrower will do.
  • Amoxicillin/clavulanate has more diarrhoea at higher clavulanate doses.
▶ Cephalosporins — by generation big card

Historically grouped into "generations" that roughly track spectrum. Learn the generation, then the quirks. Penicillin–cephalosporin cross-reactivity is <3% overall; side-chain similarity is the driver.

1st generation — cefazolin IV, cephalexin PO, cefadroxil PO

Good: MSSA, streptococci. Moderate: some enteric GNRs. Poor: enterococci, anaerobes, MRSA, Pseudomonas.

The surgical prophylaxis workhorse. Less phlebitis and less nephrotoxicity than antistaphylococcal penicillins, infused less often. Cefazolin shares no side chain with other beta-lactams → very low cross-reactivity.

2nd generation — cefuroxime, cefoxitin, cefotetan, cefprozil, cefaclor

Better gram-negative cover, slightly weaker gram-positive. Good for Haemophilus, Neisseria.

Cephamycins (cefoxitin, cefotetan) cover gut anaerobes — used for abdominal surgical prophylaxis, though B. fragilis resistance is rising. Cefotetan has an MTT side chain: vitamin K inhibition/bleeding and a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol.

3rd generation — ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefdinir, cefpodoxime, cefixime

Good: streptococci (except ceftazidime), enteric GNRs, Pseudomonas (ceftazidime only). Moderate: MSSA (except ceftazidime). Poor: enterococci, anaerobes, MRSA.

  • Ceftazidime is the odd one out: antipseudomonal, poor gram-positive cover — a bad choice for community-acquired meningitis.
  • Ceftriaxone: once daily for almost everything, but 2 g IV q12h for meningitis. Dual renal/biliary elimination → no renal adjustment. Avoid in neonates (calcium–ceftriaxone crystals; biliary sludging/hyperbilirubinaemia) — use cefotaxime. Not ideal for MSSA; if used, use 2–4 g/day.
  • Strongly associated with C. difficile and with selection of resistant GNRs.

4th generation — cefepime IV

Broadest cephalosporin: "cefazolin + ceftazidime". Good: MSSA, streptococci, Pseudomonas, enteric GNRs. Poor: enterococci, anaerobes, MRSA.

Workhorse for febrile neutropenia, nosocomial pneumonia, post-neurosurgical meningitis. Has an SDD category — low doses (500 mg–1 g BD) are inadequate for SDD isolates. Neurotoxicity, including non-convulsive status epilepticus.

Anti-MRSA cephalosporin — ceftaroline IV

Binds PBP2a → the only beta-lactam with MRSA activity. Gram-negative activity similar to ceftriaxone (lost cefepime's Pseudomonas cover).

Approved for complicated SSTI and CAP; used off-label for MRSA bacteraemia, endocarditis, meningitis, osteomyelitis. Watch for neutropenia with prolonged courses. Sometimes paired with daptomycin for refractory MRSA infection.

Siderophore cephalosporin — cefiderocol IV

"Trojan horse": an iron-chelating side chain tricks bacteria into actively importing the drug. Active against ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing GNRs, P. aeruginosa, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia; moderate against Acinetobacter; no gram-positive or anaerobic cover.

Reserve for documented resistant GNR infection. Susceptibility testing needs iron-deficient media.

▶ Cephalosporin / beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations

Agents: ceftazidime/avibactam IV, ceftolozane/tazobactam IV.

Spectrum: good — Pseudomonas (both), enteric GNRs (ceftazidime/avibactam covers carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales); poor — MRSA, MSSA, Acinetobacter, and many anaerobes.

  • Ceftolozane evades many Pseudomonas resistance mechanisms itself; avibactam inhibits a wider range of beta-lactamases (including KPC).
  • Isolates resistant to one may be susceptible to the other — ask the lab to test both.
  • Because anaerobic cover is unreliable, add metronidazole if anaerobes are suspected.
▶ Carbapenems broadest

Agents: imipenem/cilastatin IV, meropenem IV, ertapenem IV.

Spectrum: good — MSSA, streptococci, anaerobes, enteric GNRs, Pseudomonas (not ertapenem), ESBL producers; moderate — enterococci (not ertapenem), Acinetobacter (not ertapenem); poor — MRSA, penicillin-resistant streptococci.

  • Imipenem seizure risk is highest — adjust for renal function and avoid in meningitis. Cilastatin blocks renal metabolism to a nephrotoxic metabolite and is always co-formulated (dose is expressed as imipenem content).
  • Carbapenem + valproate = contraindicated (valproate levels fall, seizures break through).
  • Ertapenem is the Exception — no Pseudomonas, no enterococci, no Acinetobacter. But it is once daily, which makes it attractive for outpatient/home infusion.
  • Carbapenemases are increasing (most common in the north-east USA) — escalate to a carbapenem/beta-lactamase inhibitor combination when suspected.
  • Consider prolonged infusion for meropenem in critically ill patients (time-dependent killing).
▶ Carbapenem / beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations

Agents: meropenem/vaborbactam IV, imipenem/cilastatin/relebactam IV.

Spectrum: MSSA, streptococci, anaerobes, enteric GNRs, Pseudomonas, ESBL and many carbapenemase producers (especially KPC); moderate — enterococci, Acinetobacter; poor — MRSA.

  • The key difference is Pseudomonas: relebactam restores imipenem activity against many carbapenem-resistant strains; vaborbactam does little for meropenem against Pseudomonas.
  • Dosing trap: imipenem/cilastatin/relebactam 1.25 g q6h = 500 mg imipenem + 125 mg cilastatin + 500 mg relebactam — the same imipenem dose as imipenem/cilastatin 500 mg q6h.
▶ Monobactam — aztreonam

Agent: aztreonam IV (also inhaled in cystic fibrosis).

Spectrum: good — Pseudomonas, most GNRs; moderate — Acinetobacter; poor — all gram-positive organisms and anaerobes.

ICU pearl: aztreonam is the beta-lactam to reach for in a patient with severe beta-lactam anaphylaxis — it shares no cross-reactivity with other beta-lactams except ceftazidime (identical side chain; ceftolozane also shares it). It is chemically unrelated to aminoglycosides, so it carries none of their toxicities. Remember to add gram-positive and anaerobic cover separately.
▶ Glycopeptides — vancomycin, telavancin MRSA

Agents: vancomycin IV PO, telavancin IV (teicoplanin not available in the US).

Mechanism: bind D-Ala-D-Ala on peptidoglycan, blocking chain elongation. Telavancin also disrupts the cell membrane.

Spectrum: good — MSSA, MRSA, streptococci, C. difficile (oral route only); moderate — enterococci (VRE is resistant); poor — all gram-negatives.

  • Vancomycin infusion reaction (histamine-mediated, formerly "red man syndrome"): flushing, warmth, hypotension. Prevent by slowing the infusion; treat with antihistamine. It is not an allergy.
  • Nephrotoxicity is real at the high doses used for MRSA. Monitor AUC 400–600 rather than troughs; adequate AUC can be achieved with lower troughs than the old 15–20 mg/L target.
  • Oral vancomycin is not absorbed — it is for C. difficile only. IV vancomycin does not reach the colon. Use the lowest effective oral dose.
  • Beta-lactams kill MSSA faster — switch when susceptibilities allow.
  • Telavancin: more rapidly bactericidal; taste disturbance, foamy urine, renal toxicity; contraindicated in pregnancy.
▶ Long-acting lipoglycopeptides — dalbavancin, oritavancin

Agents: dalbavancin IV, oritavancin IV. Half-lives >1 week — a single dose can cover an entire 2-week course.

Spectrum: strictly gram-positive, including MRSA and streptococci; oritavancin also covers enterococci (including VRE).

  • Approved for skin and soft-tissue infection. Excellent for adherence/outpatient discharge — but expensive and patients still need follow-up.
  • Oritavancin interferes with PT (12 h) and aPTT (120 h) assays — coagulation monitoring becomes unreliable.
  • Dalbavancin: 1000 mg then 500 mg one week later, or a single 1500 mg dose.
  • Oritavancin: Orbactiv® over 3 h; Kimyrsa® over 1 h (better for outpatient).
▶ Fluoroquinolones QT · interactions

Agents: ciprofloxacin IVPO, levofloxacin IVPO, moxifloxacin IVPO, delafloxacin IVPO.

Spectrum: ciprofloxacin — best gram-negative and antipseudomonal, weak gram-positive; levofloxacin/moxifloxacin — pneumococci and atypicals (moxifloxacin loses Pseudomonas); delafloxacin — adds MRSA.

Adverse effects: CNS (dizziness, confusion, hallucinations — especially elderly; peripheral neuropathy), QT prolongation, tendinopathy/Achilles rupture (elderly, steroids, renal impairment), myasthenia gravis exacerbation, photosensitivity, and a boxed warning for all of the above.

  • Chelation: oral bioavailability collapses with calcium, iron, antacids, milk, multivitamins, tube feeds. Separate by ≥2 h. IV route avoids this.
  • Renal: ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin and delafloxacin need dose reduction; moxifloxacin does not — and is not approved for UTI (not renally excreted).
  • Antipseudomonal dosing: ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q8h or 750 mg PO q12h; levofloxacin 750 mg daily.
  • FDA 2016: risks outweigh benefits for sinusitis, bronchitis and uncomplicated UTI when alternatives exist.
  • Moxifloxacin has useful anaerobic activity; levofloxacin does not.
ICU pearl: before prescribing, look at the ECG and the rest of the drug chart (amiodarone, ondansetron, antipsychotics, methadone) and check K⁺/Mg²⁺.
▶ Aminoglycosides NMB interaction

Agents: gentamicin IV, tobramycin IV, amikacin IV, plazomicin IV, streptomycin, neomycin (topical).

Mechanism: bind the 30S ribosomal subunit → misreading of mRNA.

Spectrum: gram-negatives including Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter; mycobacteria (amikacin, streptomycin). Synergy with beta-lactams/glycopeptides against staphylococci, viridans streptococci and enterococci. No anaerobic, atypical or gram-positive monotherapy cover.

  • Nephrotoxicity — oliguric AKI, dose-related; avoid other nephrotoxins.
  • Ototoxicity — cochlear and vestibular, dose-related and irreversible. Baseline and follow-up audiology for courses ≥2 weeks. Easy to miss in a sedated ICU patient.
  • Neuromuscular blockade — especially with high doses or therapeutic paralysis. Potentiates non-depolarising muscle relaxants. Use with great caution in myasthenia gravis.
  • Once-daily/extended-interval dosing exploits concentration-dependent killing. Less well studied in pregnancy, critical illness, significant renal dysfunction and morbid obesity.
  • Dose on ideal or adjusted body weight — never total body weight. Poor penetration into lung and CSF → not monotherapy for pneumonia.
  • Activity ranking — Pseudomonas: amikacin > tobramycin > gentamicin > plazomicin. Klebsiella: plazomicin > amikacin ≈ gentamicin > tobramycin.
▶ Tetracyclines & modified tetracyclines

Agents: doxycycline IVPO, minocycline PO, tetracycline PO, tigecycline IV, eravacycline IV, omadacycline IVPO.

Spectrum: atypicals, rickettsia, spirochetes (Lyme), Plasmodium, H. pylori; doxycycline also covers MRSA and pneumococcus moderately. Modified agents (tigecycline, eravacycline, omadacycline) restore broad cover including VRE and MRSA — but not Pseudomonas, Proteus or Providencia.

  • Chelation with calcium, iron, antacids, mineral supplements, milk — separate by ≥2 h. Do not give with enteral feed.
  • Photosensitivity; oesophageal irritation (take with water, sitting up); contraindicated in pregnancy and children <8 y (dental staining).
  • Hepatically eliminated → poor choices for UTI (tetracycline is the exception, and it worsens renal function).
  • Large volume of distribution → low blood concentrations → not for primary bacteraemia.
  • Tigecycline carries an FDA mortality signal (driven by hospital-acquired pneumonia) — reserve for highly resistant organisms with no alternative.
  • Minocycline: dizziness/vertigo, blue tissue discolouration. Omadacycline: raises heart rate (does not prolong QTc).
▶ Macrolides QT · CYP3A4

Agents: azithromycin IVPO, clarithromycin PO, erythromycin.

Spectrum: good — atypicals, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, H. pylori, M. avium; moderate — S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes; poor — staphylococci, enteric GNRs, anaerobes, enterococci.

  • QT prolongation, worst with erythromycin.
  • Potent CYP450 inhibitors — all except azithromycin. Screen the whole drug chart.
  • GI upset is common; erythromycin is used as a prokinetic.
  • Azithromycin's long half-life allows short courses (Z-pak; single-dose Zmax).
  • Rare but serious hepatotoxicity.
ICU pearl: macrolides are attractive for CAP, but pneumococcal resistance is high — never use a macrolide alone in a patient who is more than mildly unwell. Add a beta-lactam or use a different agent.
▶ Oxazolidinones — linezolid, tedizolid

Agents: linezolid IVPO (600 mg q12h), tedizolid IVPO (200 mg daily).

Spectrum: MSSA, MRSA, streptococci including multidrug-resistant pneumococcus, enterococci including VRE, Nocardia; some atypicals and TB. No gram-negative or anaerobic cover.

  • >90% oral bioavailability — an excellent IV-to-oral switch and cheaper/more convenient than outpatient IV vancomycin.
  • Myelosuppression (especially thrombocytopenia) after ~2 weeks — monitor FBC.
  • Peripheral neuropathy and lactic acidosis with prolonged (months) therapy — mitochondrial toxicity.
  • Linezolid is a weak MAO inhibitor → serotonin syndrome with SSRIs/SNRIs. Tedizolid has minimal MAO inhibition. Monitor if the combination is unavoidable.
  • Linezolid: no renal or hepatic adjustment. Tedizolid: hepatic elimination, not for UTI.
  • Good lung penetration — a reasonable alternative to vancomycin for MRSA pneumonia.
▶ Nitroimidazoles — metronidazole, tinidazole

Agents: metronidazole IVPO, tinidazole PO (parasites only).

Spectrum: good — gram-negative and gram-positive anaerobes (Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Clostridium), protozoa (Trichomonas, Entamoeba, Giardia); moderate — H. pylori; poor — all aerobes and some oral/skin anaerobes.

  • ~100% bioavailability, no chelation issues → switch IV to PO as soon as the patient tolerates enteral medication.
  • Warfarin interaction is the important one — INR rises; monitor and reduce the warfarin dose.
  • The disulfiram-like alcohol reaction is probably not clinically relevant; many guidelines have dropped the warning.
  • Dose-related reversible peripheral neuropathy; rare confusion/seizures; metallic taste, nausea.
  • Disturbs anaerobic gut flora → risk of VRE colonisation. Ask whether anaerobic cover is really needed.
▶ Nitrofurantoin & fosfomycin cystitis only

Agents: nitrofurantoin PO (Macrobid® BD, Macrodantin® QDS), fosfomycin PO (single sachet).

Spectrum: good — E. coli (>90% in most areas), S. saprophyticus; moderate — Citrobacter, Klebsiella, Proteus, enterococci, Serratia (fosfomycin); poor — Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas.

Lower urinary tract only. These drugs concentrate in urine and achieve inadequate tissue concentrations — using them for pyelonephritis or urosepsis is a guaranteed treatment failure. Avoid nitrofurantoin if CrCl < 50 mL/min (insufficient urinary concentration).
  • Nitrofurantoin pulmonary toxicity: acute pneumonitis (cough, fever, dyspnoea — resolves on stopping) and chronic pulmonary fibrosis with prolonged use (poor recovery).
  • 5 days of nitrofurantoin outperformed single-dose fosfomycin in a comparative trial.
▶ Streptogramins — quinupristin/dalfopristin

Agent: quinupristin/dalfopristin IV.

Spectrum: MSSA, MRSA, streptococci, Enterococcus faecium (including VRE); poor — E. faecalis and all gram-negatives.

  • Two streptogramins that are bacteriostatic alone but bactericidal together ("Synercid").
  • Myalgias and arthralgias are common and treatment-limiting; phlebitis (use a central line); inhibits CYP3A4.
  • Must be diluted in 5% dextrose — it crystallises in saline, even when flushing the line. Make sure nursing knows.
  • Better as definitive therapy than empiric — E. faecalis is the more common enterococcus and is intrinsically resistant. Linezolid and daptomycin are usually better choices.
▶ Cyclic lipopeptide — daptomycin not for pneumonia

Agent: daptomycin IV.

Mechanism: inserts into the gram-positive cell membrane → rapid depolarisation and cell death.

Spectrum: MSSA, MRSA, streptococci, enterococci including VRE; no gram-negative cover.

  • Never use for pneumonia — pulmonary surfactant binds and inactivates daptomycin. This is a classic exam and bedside error.
  • Skeletal muscle toxicity — myalgia, weakness, rarely rhabdomyolysis. Check CK weekly; avoid concomitant statins where possible. Once-daily dosing and interval adjustment in renal impairment reduce risk.
  • Eosinophilic pneumonia has been reported.
  • Proven effective in right-sided staphylococcal endocarditis.
  • Dose: 4–6 mg/kg/d licensed; 8–10 mg/kg/d used for severe staphylococcal infection; 10–12 mg/kg/d recommended for enterococcal endocarditis.
  • Synergy with beta-lactams (including against MRSA and VRE) — used in refractory infection.
▶ Folate antagonists — TMP/SMX and friends

Agents: trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole IVPO, dapsone, pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine.

Spectrum: good — S. aureus including many MRSA, H. influenzae, Stenotrophomonas, Listeria, Pneumocystis jirovecii, Toxoplasma; moderate — enteric GNRs, pneumococcus, Salmonella, Shigella, Nocardia; poor — Pseudomonas, enterococci, anaerobes.

  • Hyperkalaemia — trimethoprim acts like a potassium-sparing diuretic. Watch K⁺ in renal impairment and with ACE inhibitors/ARBs/spironolactone.
  • Creatinine rises without a true fall in GFR (trimethoprim blocks tubular creatinine secretion) — and it can also cause true AKI (crystalluria, AIN).
  • Myelosuppression (dose-related; especially at PJP doses) — consider leucovorin.
  • Rash is common, particularly in HIV; SJS/TEN occurs rarely.
  • Warfarin interaction — avoid, or monitor INR closely.
  • IV preparation needs a large diluent volume — a real problem in fluid-overloaded ICU patients.
  • Dosing is based on the trimethoprim component (1:5 ratio; single = 80/400 mg, double = 160/800 mg).
  • Local E. coli resistance >15–20% → do not use empirically for cystitis.
A "sulfa allergy" label usually does not preclude furosemide, thiazides, acetazolamide, sulfonylureas or sulfadiazine — but other sulfa antimicrobials should be avoided. Get an allergist involved after severe reactions.
▶ Lincosamides — clindamycin C. diff risk

Agent: clindamycin IVPO (also topical).

Spectrum: good — gram-positive anaerobes, S. pyogenes; moderate — S. aureus including many MRSA, B. fragilis, Chlamydia, Pneumocystis, Actinomyces, Toxoplasma; poor — enterococci, C. difficile, gram-negative aerobes.

  • Highest incidence of C. difficile among antibiotics. Any diarrhoea on clindamycin needs evaluation.
  • D-test: erythromycin-resistant, clindamycin-susceptible staphylococci may harbour inducible resistance — the lab must screen before you use clindamycin.
  • ~100% oral bioavailability, but oral doses are lower than IV for GI tolerance.
  • Protein-synthesis inhibition + activity against stationary-phase organisms → add clindamycin to a beta-lactam in necrotising fasciitis and other toxin-mediated disease (anti-toxin effect).
▶ Polymyxins — colistin, polymyxin B last resort

Agents: colistin (colistimethate sodium) IV, polymyxin B IV.

Spectrum: many GNRs including multidrug-resistant A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa and CRE; moderate — Stenotrophomonas; poor — all gram-positives, anaerobes, Proteus, Providencia, Burkholderia, Serratia.

  • Nephrotoxicity (acute tubular necrosis) is common — avoid other nephrotoxins, including vancomycin and aminoglycosides. Polymyxin B may be less nephrotoxic than colistin.
  • Neurotoxicity — dizziness, weakness, paraesthesia, confusion, and neuromuscular blockade that can cause respiratory arrest.
  • Dosing unit chaos — a genuine source of fatal error:
    1 mg colistin base activity ≈ 30,000 IU ≈ 2.7 mg colistimethate.
    1,000,000 IU ≈ 80 mg colistimethate ≈ 30 mg colistin base activity.
    Never prescribe colistin in milligrams of colistimethate. Polymyxin B: 1 mg = 10,000 IU.
  • Colistin is a renally cleared prodrug (give a loading dose, adjust in renal impairment); polymyxin B is not renally cleared — no renal adjustment (contrary to the package insert) and not useful for UTI.
  • Colistin is preferred for urinary infection; polymyxin B for other sites. Oral colistin is for bowel decontamination only. Aerosolised polymyxins reduce colonisation in cystic fibrosis.
Use polymyxins only when there is no alternative — better-studied agents should be used whenever the organism is susceptible.
▶ C. difficile-specific agents

Agents: fidaxomicin PO, bezlotoxumab IV — plus oral vancomycin and metronidazole.

  • Fidaxomicin: non-absorbed macrocyclic antibiotic, narrow spectrum, spares gut flora. Similar cure rate to oral vancomycin but fewer recurrences; now recommended first line. Do not combine with oral vancomycin or metronidazole — the flora-sparing advantage is lost.
  • Bezlotoxumab: monoclonal antibody against toxin B. It does not kill the organism — the patient still needs an antibacterial. Reduces recurrence, not initial cure; use in high-risk patients. More adverse events in patients with heart failure.
  • Both are expensive — but cheaper than an admission for relapse. Check patient assistance programmes.
  • Metronidazole remains an option for mild–moderate first episodes but is not first line.

3 · Antimycobacterials

▶ TB drugs — RIPE / HRZE interactions

Mycobacteria grow slowly, live inside cells, and have a waxy mycolic-acid cell wall that resists drug penetration. Active disease is always treated with combination therapy — starting regimen rifampin + isoniazid + pyrazinamide + ethambutol (RIPE / HRZE), then rifampin + isoniazid consolidation. MDR-TB needs a specialist.

Rifamycins — rifampin, rifabutin, rifapentine (rifaximin = non-absorbed, gut only)

Inhibit RNA polymerase. Active against mycobacteria and many ordinary bacteria; used as an add-on for prosthetic-material staphylococcal infection.

  • Potent CYP450 inducers — the single most important interaction in the class. Loss of anticoagulation, transplant rejection, loss of seizure control, contraceptive failure.
  • Orange-red discolouration of urine, tears and sweat — warn the patient; it stains contact lenses (don't wear them).
  • Hepatotoxicity, rash, nausea, drug fever. Rifabutin: leucopenia and optic neuritis, and it is itself a CYP substrate (inhibitors raise its level).
  • Very low genetic barrier to resistance → never as monotherapy except in latent TB.

Isoniazid (INH)

Inhibits mycolic acid synthesis; active against M. tuberculosis and M. kansasii only. Bactericidal against growing organisms, bacteriostatic against dormant ones.

  • Hepatotoxicity — asymptomatic transaminase rises are common and usually settle; stop if markedly elevated or symptomatic (nausea, abdominal pain, jaundice).
  • Peripheral neuropathy — prevent with pyridoxine (vitamin B6), especially in alcohol use disorder, diabetes, pregnancy.
  • Drug-induced lupus (resolves on stopping); optic neuritis; seizures rarely. Advise against alcohol (additive hepatotoxicity).

Pyrazinamide (PZA)

Prodrug, active only in acidic environments (pH <6) — which is exactly the caseous granuloma, and it works intracellularly.

  • Hyperuricaemia — predictable; can precipitate gout and force withdrawal. Arthralgia is separate and manageable with simple analgesia.
  • Hepatotoxicity (dose-related, less common at modern doses). Counsel about dark urine, abdominal pain, anorexia.
  • Used only in the first 2 months of drug-sensitive TB (longer in MDR-TB).

Ethambutol (EMB)

Inhibits arabinosyl transferase → blocks arabinogalactan synthesis. Active against M. tuberculosis, MAC and M. kansasii.

  • Optic neuropathy — reduced acuity, red–green discrimination loss. Dose- and duration-dependent. Requires monitoring; recovery may be incomplete.
  • Not recommended under 5 years — children can't perform the vision tests.
  • The one RIPE drug that is not hepatotoxic — useful when the liver is the problem.
ICU pearl: a patient admitted on TB treatment is a walking drug-interaction. Rifampin will have already lowered the levels of many of their regular medicines — check anticoagulants, antiepileptics, immunosuppressants, steroids and analgesia. Also remember carbapenems + valproate if you add meropenem.

4 · Antifungals

▶ Polyenes — amphotericin B nephrotoxicity

Agents: amphotericin B deoxycholate IV, liposomal amphotericin B (LAmB) IV, amphotericin B lipid complex (ABLC) IV, nystatin (topical/oral rinse).

Mechanism: bind ergosterol in the fungal membrane → leakage and cell death.

Spectrum: good — most Candida and Aspergillus, Cryptococcus, dimorphic fungi, many moulds; moderate — Mucorales, Fusarium, Scedosporium; poor — C. lusitaniae, A. terreus.

  • Infusion reactions — fever, chills, rigors. Premedicate with paracetamol, hydrocortisone ± diphenhydramine; pethidine for rigors (careful — renally cleared neurotoxic metabolite). Least with LAmB.
  • Nephrotoxicity — direct tubular toxicity plus afferent vasoconstriction; causes Mg²⁺ and K⁺ wasting. Attenuate with saline loading before and after the infusion, and replace electrolytes.
  • Formulation dosing is a fatal-error trap: deoxycholate 0.5–1.5 mg/kg/d; lipid formulations 3–6 mg/kg/d. Giving the lipid dose of deoxycholate is a 3–5× overdose.
  • Still drugs of choice for cryptococcal meningitis and serious dimorphic/mould infection; used empirically in febrile neutropenia when the organism is unknown.
▶ Flucytosine (5-FC)

Agent: flucytosine POIV.

Mechanism: deaminated inside fungal cells to 5-fluorouracil → interferes with DNA and protein synthesis. Fungi lacking cytosine deaminase are resistant.

Spectrum: good — Cryptococcus (in combination); moderate — most Candida, Aspergillus; poor — many moulds, C. krusei.

  • Bone marrow suppression is the main concern — follow FBC more closely than drug levels (levels are often a send-out lab taking a week).
  • Hepatotoxicity; GI upset is more common but less serious.
  • Never as monotherapy — resistance emerges rapidly. Look for its "buddy" (usually amphotericin B for cryptococcal meningitis).
  • Avoid the abbreviation "5-FC" — it is easily confused with 5-FU.
▶ Azoles — the workhorses CYP · QT · formulations

Agents: fluconazole IVPO, itraconazole PO, voriconazole IVPO, posaconazole IVPO, isavuconazole IVPO, ketoconazole (topical).

Mechanism: inhibit fungal CYP450 14-alpha demethylase → block conversion of lanosterol to ergosterol.

Fluconazole

Good — C. albicans, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, C. lusitaniae, Cryptococcus, Coccidioides; moderate — C. glabrata (SDD or resistant); poor — all moulds, C. krusei, C. auris.

  • Excellent bioavailability (1:1 IV:PO) — the classic IV-to-oral switch.
  • Renally cleared — dose-adjust in renal impairment (unlike other azoles).
  • Check the species before committing: a patient who has had prolonged fluconazole prophylaxis may now have a resistant Candida — use an echinocandin instead.

Itraconazole

Broader than fluconazole: good for dimorphic fungi (Blastomyces, Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Sporothrix), most Candida, Aspergillus; poor for Mucorales.

  • Negative inotrope — contraindicated in heart failure.
  • Three formulations with different food and acid requirements — capsules with a full meal; solution on an empty stomach; regular capsules fail with any acid suppression. They are not interchangeable.
  • Absorption is erratic — monitor trough concentrations for serious or prolonged therapy. IV formulation is discontinued.

Voriconazole

Drug of choice for invasive aspergillosis; good for most Candida including C. krusei and fluconazole-resistant strains, Cryptococcus, many moulds; poor for Mucorales.

  • Highly variable pharmacokinetics — consider levels on prolonged therapy.
  • Visual disturbances (photopsia), rash/photosensitivity, hepatotoxicity, periostitis with long-term use.
  • Long, varied interaction list — rifampin is contraindicated; calcineurin inhibitors need dose adjustment.
  • Hepatically eliminated — not useful for candiduria. IV formulation contains a cyclodextrin vehicle (accumulates in renal failure).

Posaconazole

Good — Aspergillus, Mucorales, many moulds, dimorphic fungi, most Candida; moderate — Fusarium, C. glabrata. Recently shown non-inferior to voriconazole in invasive aspergillosis.

  • Mainly used for prophylaxis in high-risk patients and for mucormycosis.
  • Suspension needs food (especially fatty food/low-pH drinks) and is variably absorbed — the delayed-release tablets are far more reliable. Different formulations are not interchangeable.
  • Pseudohyperaldosteronism → hypertension and hypokalaemia.

Isavuconazole

Good — Candida, Aspergillus, Mucorales, some other moulds; poor — Fusarium.

  • Does not prolong the QT — it can actually shorten it. Useful when other azoles are risky.
  • Excellent oral bioavailability, unaffected by food or acid; capsules must not be opened or crushed (a problem for nasogastric tubes).
  • Dosing trap: the prodrug isavuconazonium 372 mg = isavuconazole 200 mg. Give an 8-hourly loading regimen (6 doses) before once-daily maintenance.
  • Not approved for invasive candidiasis — use an echinocandin or fluconazole.
▶ Echinocandins 1st line for candidaemia

Agents: caspofungin IV, micafungin IV, anidulafungin IV.

Mechanism: inhibit beta-1,3-D-glucan synthase → defective fungal cell wall.

Spectrum: good — all common Candida species including C. glabrata and C. krusei; moderate — Aspergillus (aberrant, non-functional hyphae rather than outright killing), some dimorphic fungi, C. auris; poor — most non-Aspergillus moulds, Cryptococcus, Trichosporon.

  • Excellent safety profile — mild infusion reactions, uncommon hepatotoxicity, far fewer interactions than azoles.
  • No oral formulation.
  • Differences are mainly pharmacokinetic: caspofungin and micafungin undergo hepatic (non-CYP) metabolism; anidulafungin degrades in plasma. Watch caspofungin + ciclosporin and micafungin + sirolimus.
  • Drugs of choice for invasive candidiasis, particularly in unstable patients or where azole resistance is possible. Step down to an azole once susceptibilities are known and the patient is stable.
  • Combination with voriconazole (aspergillosis) or liposomal amphotericin (Mucorales) is used by some clinicians for refractory disease.
ICU pearl: "yeast in the blood" → start an echinocandin (after briefly considering whether it could be Cryptococcus), remove/change lines, and get ophthalmology review.
▶ Terpenoids — ibrexafungerp

Agent: ibrexafungerp PO. Same target as echinocandins (beta-1,3-D-glucan synthase) but structurally distinct — oral bioavailability, and incomplete cross-resistance with echinocandins.

Approved for: vulvovaginal candidiasis (one day, two doses). Trials in invasive disease are awaited.

Watch: GI adverse effects; it inhibits CYP450 and is a CYP3A4 substrate, so it has more interaction potential than the echinocandins. It is not simply an "oral echinocandin".

5 · Antivirals

▶ HSV / VZV — aciclovir, valaciclovir, famciclovir

Agents: aciclovir IVPO, valaciclovir PO, famciclovir PO.

Spectrum: good — HSV-1, HSV-2; moderate — VZV; poor — EBV, CMV, HIV.

  • IV aciclovir is the drug of choice for HSV encephalitis and severe HSV in immunocompromised patients.
  • Nephrotoxicity — crystalluria and acute interstitial nephritis, mostly with high-dose IV aciclovir. Keep the patient well hydrated and dose-adjust for renal function; avoid with other nephrotoxins and diuretics.
  • CNS effects: confusion, tremor, seizures. Nausea, diarrhoea, rash.
  • Valaciclovir is a prodrug of aciclovir with much better bioavailability and less frequent dosing — patients generally prefer it. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura has been reported with valaciclovir in HIV patients.
  • VZV is intrinsically less susceptible → higher doses needed.
▶ CMV — ganciclovir, foscarnet, cidofovir, letermovir

Agents: ganciclovir IV, valganciclovir PO, foscarnet IV, cidofovir IV, letermovir IVPO.

  • Ganciclovir / valganciclovir — first line for treatment and prevention. Dose-dependent myelosuppression (neutropenia, thrombocytopenia), worse in renal impairment. Valganciclovir is oral but has the same toxicity as IV ganciclovir and needs the same monitoring. Weight-based dosing matters — in a 50 kg patient, 900 mg BD valganciclovir delivers more than double the active drug of 5 mg/kg IV q12h.
  • Foscarnet — second line for CMV, also for resistant/severe HSV. Nephrotoxic and neurotoxic, causes profound electrolyte abnormalities; needs pre-hydration.
  • Cidofovir — nephrotoxic; requires saline prehydration and probenecid.
  • Letermovir — inhibits the terminase complex (viral DNA packaging). Much better tolerated (adverse effects similar to placebo) but resistance emerges rapidly in treatment settings, so its role is prophylaxis. No HSV or VZV cover — patients may need adjunctive aciclovir.
  • Genotype-based CMV susceptibility testing is available when treatment fails.
Don't confuse valGANciclovir with valACiclovir — similar names, very different drugs and toxicities.
▶ Influenza antivirals

Agents: oseltamivir PO, peramivir IV, zanamivir (inhaled), baloxavir PO (single dose).

Mechanism: neuraminidase inhibitors block release of new virions; baloxavir (endonuclease inhibitor) blocks viral mRNA transcription. No cross-resistance.

  • Most effective when started early (viral replication peaks in the first 72 h).
  • In severe or hospitalised influenza, treat regardless of the 48-hour window — most of this evidence is with oseltamivir.
  • Oseltamivir: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain; headache and fatigue with longer prophylactic courses.
  • Zanamivir: avoid in asthma/reactive airway disease — cough and bronchospasm.
  • Baloxavir is very well tolerated but up to 10% of patients develop resistance after exposure, and resistant strains transmit.
▶ COVID-19 antivirals

Agents: remdesivir IV, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir PO, molnupiravir PO.

  • Remdesivir — nucleotide analogue inhibiting viral RNA polymerase. Most benefit early, in hospitalised patients not requiring invasive ventilation; no benefit once ventilated. Transaminase rises (hold if >10× ULN); the IV vehicle is a cyclodextrin cleared renally — avoid in significant renal impairment unless the benefit justifies it.
  • Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid) — protease inhibitor boosted with ritonavir. Ritonavir is a powerful CYP3A4 inhibitor: in an ICU patient on amiodarone, tacrolimus, statins, DOACs, calcium channel blockers or sedatives, check every single interaction before prescribing.
  • Remember that the drugs with the biggest mortality benefit in severe COVID-19 are the anti-inflammatory agents (dexamethasone, baricitinib, tocilizumab) — prioritise those in the sickest patients.
▶ Antiretrovirals — class overview

HIV is now a chronic disease managed with combination therapy. For an ICU patient, the priority is: know the regimen, know the interactions, and know the toxicities.

NRTIs (nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors)

Tenofovir (TDF/TAF), emtricitabine, lamivudine, abacavir, zidovudine. Two NRTIs usually form the "backbone" of a regimen. Also active against HBV.

  • Abacavir hypersensitivity — screen for HLA-B*5701 before use; rechallenge can be fatal.
  • Tenofovir: nephrotoxicity and reduced bone mineral density (worse with TDF than TAF); TAF causes more weight gain and dyslipidaemia.
  • Zidovudine: bone marrow suppression; mitochondrial toxicity (lactic acidosis, hepatic steatosis) — mostly historical agents.
  • All except abacavir need renal dose adjustment — fixed-dose combinations may prevent this.

NNRTIs

Efavirenz, nevirapine, rilpivirine, doravirine, etravirine. Low genetic barrier to resistance — a single mutation confers class resistance.

  • Efavirenz: vivid dreams, dizziness, insomnia, depression, psychosis — take at bedtime on an empty stomach; relative contraindication with a psychiatric history.
  • Nevirapine: rash (including SJS/TEN) and hepatotoxicity, sometimes as a hypersensitivity syndrome; risk reduced by a 2-week low-dose lead-in.
  • Mixed CYP inducing/inhibiting effects — screen every drug.
  • Rilpivirine + cabotegravir is available as a once-monthly intramuscular regimen after an oral lead-in — useful when adherence is a concern.

Protease inhibitors

Darunavir, atazanavir, ritonavir (as a booster). Always boosted with ritonavir or cobicistat.

  • GI intolerance, hepatotoxicity, dyslipidaemia, lipodystrophy, increased cardiovascular risk.
  • Atazanavir: nephrolithiasis; hyperbilirubinaemia.
  • Massive CYP interaction potential — substrates and inhibitors.
  • Ritonavir 400 mg BD is almost never correct now — a prescription for that dose usually signals an error. Check it.

Integrase inhibitors (INSTIs)

Dolutegravir, bictegravir, raltegravir, cabotegravir, elvitegravir. Now the anchor of all first-line regimens.

  • Well tolerated; weight gain; insomnia/depression; asymptomatic CK rises.
  • Few drug interactions (except elvitegravir, which is boosted with cobicistat).
  • Chelation with divalent/trivalent cations (antacids, multivitamins) reduces absorption — separate dosing.
  • Cobicistat, dolutegravir and bictegravir raise serum creatinine without a true fall in GFR.

Entry / attachment / fusion inhibitors

Maraviroc, enfuvirtide, ibalizumab, fostemsavir — reserved for heavily treatment-experienced patients.

  • Enfuvirtide: twice-daily subcutaneous injection; injection-site reactions in essentially everyone.
  • Maraviroc needs a tropism test first — it only works on CCR5-tropic virus.
  • Fostemsavir can prolong the QT interval.
▶ Hepatitis C — direct-acting antivirals

Suffixes tell you the class: -previr = protease inhibitor, -asvir = NS5A inhibitor, -buvir = NS5B polymerase inhibitor. Most are co-formulated. Pan-genotypic regimens: glecaprevir/pibrentasvir and sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (± voxilaprevir).

  • Very well tolerated — nausea, vomiting, fatigue.
  • Protease-inhibitor regimens can cause hepatic decompensation — monitor ALT, and avoid in decompensated cirrhosis.
  • HBV reactivation can occur with any DAA regimen — check HBV status first and monitor.
  • Drug interactions: the protease inhibitors are CYP3A4 substrates and inhibitors; the NS5A/NS5B agents are sensitive to acid suppression.
  • Treatment durations are ~3–4 months with cure rates of 80–100%.
▶ Ribavirin & hepatitis B nucleoside analogues

Ribavirin PO — used in combination for HCV and for severe RSV (oral or aerosolised).

  • Haemolytic anaemia — dose-related, dose-limiting and sometimes severe. Monitor haemoglobin; manage with dose reduction ± erythropoietin.
  • Teratogenic — reliable contraception in women of childbearing age; pregnant staff should avoid aerosolised ribavirin.
  • Never as HCV monotherapy — resistance emerges rapidly.

HBV nucleoside analogues — entecavir PO, tenofovir (TDF/TAF) PO, lamivudine PO, adefovir (obsolete).

  • Well tolerated. Adefovir is nephrotoxic. Lactic acidosis with hepatic steatosis is rare.
  • Test for both HIV and HBV before starting — using an HBV drug with weak HIV activity alone risks selecting resistant HIV.
  • Entecavir is first line for HBV without HIV coinfection; tenofovir is preferred if HIV is also present. Lamivudine resistance is too common now.
  • Stopping therapy can cause an HBV reactivation "flare".

6 · Antiparasitics

▶ Malaria — quinolines & artemisinins

Malaria is the most important cause of fever in returning travellers. Because most clinicians see it rarely, always check current national guidelines — the CDC runs a clinician malaria hotline.

Quinolines — chloroquine, mefloquine, quinine, primaquine

  • Cardiovascular: dose-related QT prolongation, hypotension, ventricular arrhythmia.
  • Quinine: hypoglycaemia (stimulates insulin release) and "cinchonism" — tinnitus, headache, nausea, visual disturbance.
  • Mefloquine: psychiatric disturbance from insomnia and vivid dreams to depression, psychosis and suicide. Avoid with any psychiatric history.
  • Primaquine: test for G6PD deficiency first — causes haemolysis. It is the only agent active against the dormant liver hypnozoites of P. vivax/P. ovale, so a 2-week course is added to prevent relapse.
  • IV quinidine has been discontinued in the US — IV artesunate is now standard for severe malaria.

Artemisinins — artesunate, artemether/lumefantrine

  • IV artesunate for severe malaria; it acts rapidly but has a short half-life, so it must be followed by a full oral antimalarial course (e.g. artemether/lumefantrine or atovaquone/proguanil) or the patient will relapse.
  • Delayed haemolytic anaemia — check FBC weekly for 4 weeks after IV artesunate.
  • Highly active against chloroquine- and mefloquine-resistant strains, but artemisinin resistance is emerging (slow parasite clearance).
  • Not used for chemoprophylaxis.
▶ Atovaquone, benzimidazoles, pentamidine, ivermectin

Atovaquone / atovaquone–proguanil

Active against Pneumocystis, Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, Babesia. Very well tolerated (GI upset).

  • Take with food — a fatty meal increases bioavailability roughly five-fold.
  • Atovaquone alone is slightly less effective than dapsone/pentamidine for mild–moderate PJP but better tolerated; do not use in severe PJP or if absorption is poor.
  • Atovaquone–proguanil (Malarone) is an excellent traveller's prophylaxis — start only 1–2 days before travel and continue 7 days after return.

Benzimidazoles — albendazole, mebendazole

Disrupt parasite microtubules. Single dose cures most intestinal nematodes; prolonged courses for tissue-invasive disease (Echinococcus, neurocysticercosis).

  • Very well tolerated; with multiple doses — GI upset, rarely hepatotoxicity and neutropenia.
  • Avoid in pregnancy where possible (probably safe after the first trimester).
  • Enzyme inducers (phenytoin, rifampin) may lower levels — relevant in systemic infection.

Pentamidine

Alternative to TMP/SMX for Pneumocystis pneumonia (IV) and prophylaxis (inhaled). Also used for leishmaniasis and trypanosomiasis.

  • Hypotension with rapid infusion — infuse over at least an hour. QT prolongation and ventricular arrhythmia.
  • Pancreatic toxicity → dysglycaemia in up to 25% — initial hypoglycaemia from insulin release, later hyperglycaemia and potentially permanent diabetes. Pancreatitis.
  • Nephrotoxicity (usually reversible), hypokalaemia, hypocalcaemia.
  • Inhaled route: bronchospasm (pre-treat with a bronchodilator); does not protect against extrapulmonary Pneumocystis, Toxoplasma or bacterial pneumonia as TMP/SMX does.
  • Only about half of patients tolerate a full IV course.

Ivermectin

Paralyses parasites at the neuromuscular junction. Used for scabies (including crusted/Norwegian scabies), lice, strongyloidiasis, onchocerciasis (river blindness) and cutaneous larva migrans.

  • Very well tolerated for scabies. In nematode infection, fever, myalgia and hypotension can occur from the immune response to dying parasites — worse with high worm burden, resolves quickly.
  • For scabies/lice, give two doses about 1 week apart — a single dose risks relapse.
  • Worth knowing: mass ivermectin donation has prevented more than half a million cases of blindness.

▶ The 12 things not to forget on the ICU round
  • 1. Daptomycin is inactivated by surfactant — never for pneumonia.
  • 2. Aminoglycosides and polymyxins potentiate neuromuscular blockade — anticipate delayed weaning and prolonged paralysis.
  • 3. Carbapenems + valproate = seizures. Check the drug chart.
  • 4. Rifampin will have lowered the level of half the patient's other drugs.
  • 5. Oral vancomycin is not absorbed — it does nothing outside the colon.
  • 6. Cefepime and imipenem can cause non-convulsive status epilepticus.
  • 7. Fluoroquinolones, macrolides and azoles all prolong the QT — check K⁺, Mg²⁺ and the ECG.
  • 8. Tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones are chelated by enteral feed, antacids and mineral supplements.
  • 9. Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin are for cystitis only — never pyelonephritis.
  • 10. Amphotericin B: which formulation? The doses differ by a factor of 3–5.
  • 11. Colistin: which unit? mg of base activity vs mg of colistimethate vs IU — a fatal confusion.
  • 12. MSSA should be treated with a beta-lactam (cefazolin/nafcillin/oxacillin) whenever the allergy history permits — they kill faster than vancomycin.
