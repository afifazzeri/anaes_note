Part A · Anaesthesia for thyroid & parathyroid surgery

1. Learning objectives & key points

Learning objectives

  • Describe the important considerations in anaesthesia for thyroid and parathyroid surgery.
  • Deliver safe and appropriate anaesthesia to these patients.
  • Outline the issues in airway management.
  • Discuss the potential emergencies related to thyroid and parathyroid surgery.

Key points

  • IONM techniques need advanced planning and effective communication between surgical and anaesthetic teams.
  • There is no standardised anaesthetic technique, but TIVA has some benefits (stable EMG signals, smooth emergence).
  • Screen every patient for a potentially difficult airway.
  • A significant proportion have pre-existing endocrine disorders that may precipitate clinical emergencies.
  • Postoperative airway and metabolic complications are likely — plan for them.

Historical perspective

From the first published paper on anaesthesia for thyroid surgery to the 6th National Audit Report (BAETS), the key challenges have remained the risks of perioperative thyroid toxaemia, complex airway management and preventing injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN). The BAETS audit highlighted increasing use of IONM and the importance of preoperative laryngoscopy to identify RLN palsy. There is growing interest in one-day / day-case procedures.

2. Intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM)

What it does

  • Helps the surgeon identify neural structures close to the thyroid and parathyroid glands.
  • Allows mapping of the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (EBSLN), the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) and the vagus nerve by direct electrical nerve stimulation.
  • Detects anatomical variations, checks and clarifies mechanisms of potential nerve injury, and predicts the outcome of vocal cord movement.
  • Use has increased worldwide (UKRETS), but many disparities remain between centres.

Anaesthetic implications

  • Requires advanced planning and effective communication between surgical and anaesthetic teams.
  • Neuromuscular blockade must be managed so that adequate EMG signals are obtainable — plan relaxant dose and reversal (sugammadex) with the surgeon.
  • Preoperative fibreoptic visualisation of the glottis is recommended to identify pre-existing vocal cord palsy and to visualise anatomical alteration/deviation.
  • TIVA is often preferred because it produces more stable, reliable EMG signals and a smoother emergence.
3. Airway management & the goitre patient

Why it matters

  • Thyroid pathology is a predictor of difficult tracheal intubation and facemask ventilation.
  • A randomised trial found tracheal tube placement during direct laryngoscopy had a higher rate of insufficient EMG signals (23%) than the video-assisted approach (9%).
  • Videolaryngoscopy (VL) reduces the rate of difficult intubation in elective thyroid surgery and reduces the Cormack–Lehane grade. It is also effective because the whole surgical team can correctly visualise the tube electrodes in the proper position with real-time EMG monitoring.

Awake tracheal intubation (ATI)

  • Used when a difficult airway is anticipated because of tracheal compression. Encompasses ATI:FB (flexible bronchoscopy) and ATI:VL (videolaryngoscopy).
  • DAS guidelines emphasise workspace ergonomics before starting the procedure.
  • Patients may obstruct when supine or during induction — consider fibreoptic intubation in the seated or semi-recumbent position (30° to the operating table).
  • Manage all key components: sedation, topicalisation, oxygenation, performance.
  • In airway obstruction or a narrowed airway: minimise sedation, consider lower lidocaine concentrations, use high-flow nasal oxygen, and involve experienced practitioners.
  • General anaesthesia is induced only after confirming correct tracheal tube position — visualise the tracheal lumen (ATI:FB) or the tube through the vocal cords (ATI:VL), then confirm with capnography to exclude oesophageal intubation.

If ATI fails

  • Plan must include: "stop and think", front-of-neck airway (FONA) access, or high-risk general anaesthesia.
  • If immediate airway access is essential, FONA or high-risk GA must be managed by the most appropriately skilled clinicians; otherwise postpone surgery.
  • A short neck, goitre or indistinct landmarks may make ATI:FONA difficult.
  • If high-risk GA is the only remaining option, declare an achievable A to D airway strategy based on the 2015 DAS guidelines. Pillars: neuromuscular block, first airway approach with VL, and the most skilled clinician present.
  • 2022 ASA airway guidelines for "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" (CICO): surgical cricothyrotomy, needle cricothyrotomy with a pressure-regulated device, large-bore cannula cricothyrotomy, or surgical tracheostomy.

Extubation

  • In patients subjected to ATI, follow DAS extubation guidelines: planning, preparation, performance and post-extubation care using the "at risk" algorithm.
  • In retrosternal goitre no consensus exists, although ATI appears to be a safe method.

4. Thyroid disorders & perioperative optimisation

Primary perioperative challenges

  • Thyrotoxicosis (with or without hyperthyroidism), hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism.
  • Diagnostic criteria (e.g. Akamizu criteria) assist the decision to proceed.
  • Plasmapheresis is a therapeutic option to reduce blood thyroid hormone levels.
  • Several guidelines recommend the patient be euthyroid before surgery; however, in selected cases surgery must proceed while the patient is hyperthyroid.
  • In subclinical hyperthyroidism: consult an endocrinologist and modify medication if TSH remains persistently altered in the presence of vascular and cardiac pathology.

Hypothyroidism

  • Emergency surgery in severe hypothyroidism (myxoedema coma, altered mentation, pericardial effusions, heart failure): loading dose i.v. levothyroxine 200–500 µg, then 50–100 µg daily.
  • Consider i.v. liothyronine in myxoedema coma, and glucocorticoids if adrenal insufficiency.
  • Subclinical hypothyroidism: preoperative endocrinology consultation.
  • Cardiac evaluation is necessary if there are signs/symptoms of heart failure — risk of impaired diastolic function.

5. Parathyroid disorders

Indications & priorities

  • Guidelines suggest performing surgical parathyroidectomy in cases with normal serum calcium concentrations.
  • Priority surgical interventions:
    • Primary hyperparathyroidism with adjusted calcium > 3 mmol L⁻¹ despite medical treatment
    • Pregnant patients
    • Recurrent renal stones associated with sepsis in primary hyperparathyroidism

Hypercalcaemic crisis risk

  • Patients with unsatisfactory preoperative serum calcium who need surgery may develop a hypercalcaemic crisis.
  • Rehydrate with i.v. fluids and arrange an urgent endocrinology consultation.
  • Medical agents: denosumab, cinacalcet, calcitonin, bisphosphonates.

Anaesthetic priorities

  • Focus on neuromuscular function — quantitative neuromuscular monitoring is recommended.
  • All clinicians involved must be aware of the possibility of abrupt cardiovascular collapse.
  • Preoperative hypercalcaemia may alter QT length with dysrhythmia.
  • A rapid decrease in serum calcium after parathyroid removal precedes a marked decrease in vascular resistance and impaired cardiac function.
  • Management: vasopressors and inotropes; prompt infusion of large volumes of i.v. fluids with calcium and vitamin D supplementation.
6. Emergencies — RLN palsy, haematoma, hypocalcaemia

A · Immediate postoperative bilateral RLN palsy

  • Patients with bilateral RLN palsy may develop airway distress after tracheal extubation and require reintubation, which can be difficult.
  • Call for help and proceed according to DAS guidelines.
  • Some authors consider immediate revision surgery to improve airway patency with better postoperative voice quality.
  • Tracheostomy after securing the airway is an option.
  • All patients should be admitted to ICU until a protected airway is established.

B · Postoperative haematoma

A challenging and potentially life-threatening complication. Reported incidence varies; a recent analysis of the Collaborative Endocrine Surgery Quality Improvement Program database (20,980 patients) reported an incidence of 0.6%. Increased age appears to be an additional hazard because of the use of antiplatelet and anticoagulant drugs.

Day-case eligibility

  • A simple, reproducible predictive score has been developed to establish postoperative haematoma risk during ambulatory surgery.
  • BAETS four criteria for day-case endocrine neck operations:
    1. Low risk of postoperative haemorrhage
    2. Necessary informed consent
    3. Verbal and written instructions about postoperative bleeding protocols
    4. Easy access to healthcare facilities
  • The "low risk of haemorrhage" criterion is debatable — a no-risk scenario is not possible. The ATA is extremely prudent, stressing the need for evidence-based clinical protocols and systematic pathways.
  • In 2022, BAETS + British Association of Otorhinolaryngology – Head and Neck Surgery + DAS published recommendations for post-thyroid surgery haematoma management (hoped to apply to parathyroid surgery).
Haematoma — the mandatory sequence

Oxygenate and evaluate → Evacuate → Intubate.

Before signs of airway compromise: "skin exposure, cut sutures, open skin, open muscles, pack wound" — then the anaesthetist or skilled clinician can consider tracheal intubation.

The six "triggers" to evaluate postoperatively

  1. Difficulty in swallowing
  2. Increase in the Early Warning Score (EWS) or National Early Warning Score (NEWS)
  3. Swelling
  4. Anxiety
  5. Tachypnoea or difficulty in breathing
  6. Stridor

Any of these mandates an urgent clinical review by a senior doctor. Patients should be closely monitored to avoid late signs of airway obstruction such as desaturation and increasing oxygen requirements.

DAS recommendations: VL performed by the most experienced operator; attempts limited to three, with one additional attempt, proceeding early to emergency FONA in time-critical settings.

C · Acute postoperative hypocalcaemia

  • The incidence of transient or permanent hypoparathyroidism differs between clinical settings.
  • Manifestations: neurological impairment with perioral paraesthesia, restlessness and altered neuromuscular activity, with typical bedside signs (Chvostek and Trousseau signs).
  • Cardiac: life-threatening — prolonged QT interval that can generate torsades de pointes.

7. Postoperative pain management
  • The endocrine surgery section of the American Head and Neck Society (AHNS-ES) recommends opioid administration only in case of contraindications or failure of first-line non-opioid drugs.
  • The Society of Onco-Anaesthesia and Perioperative Care encourages a multimodal approach for analgesia in head and neck oncological surgery.
  • Focus on the preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative periods; combine analgesics of different classes.

Part B · Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis)

8. Thyroid storm — definitions, physiology & epidemiology
Key points — thyroid storm
  • Life-threatening condition characterised by overt thyrotoxicosis in the setting of long-standing untreated or undertreated hyperthyroidism.
  • May be triggered by an acute stress event: abrupt discontinuation of antithyroid medication, infection, or surgery.
  • In the awake patient: neurological, cardiovascular, thermoregulatory and gastrointestinal dysfunction predominate.
  • In the anaesthetised patient, signs may be limited to pyrexia, tachycardia and atrial fibrillation — diagnosis is harder.
  • Differential diagnosis: sepsis, malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, phaeochromocytoma, anticholinergic syndrome.
  • Management: general supportive measures (A, B, C, temperature) plus specific pharmacology (beta-blockade, thionamide, iodine, glucocorticosteroids).

Definitions — three distinct conditions

TermDefinition
Hyperthyroidism A biochemical diagnosis defined by an increase in circulating thyroid hormone levels.
Thyrotoxicosis A group of signs and symptoms induced by inappropriate activation of thyroid hormones in the tissues — the clinical manifestation of hyperthyroidism.
Thyroid storm An acute life-threatening endocrine emergency resembling an exaggerated presentation of thyrotoxicosis.

Basic thyroid physiology

  • The thyroid converts iodine into thyroxine (T4, prohormone) and triiodothyronine (T3, bioactive hormone); thyroid cells combine iodine and tyrosine to form T3 and T4.
  • The normal gland produces about 80% T4 and 20% T3. Most T3 is produced by enzymatic deiodination of T4 in peripheral tissues by iodothyronine deiodinases.
  • The anterior pituitary secretes TSH in response to TRH from the hypothalamus (via the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system); somatostatin can inhibit TSH secretion.
  • Free T3 and free T4 exert negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary.

Incidence and mortality

  • Most epidemiological data come from national surveys in Japan and the United States.
  • Incidence: 0.20–0.76 per 100,000 people per year.
  • Intraoperative and postoperative thyroid storm following non-thyroid surgery is rare — 24 cases identified in the literature between 1930 and 2018. Rarity reflects the widespread use of antithyroid medication to achieve a euthyroid state before surgery.
  • Mortality 10–30% despite early diagnosis.
9. Thyroid storm — clinical features & triggers

Clinical features of thyrotoxicosis

Symptoms: heat intolerance, palpitations, anxiety, fatigue, weight loss, muscle weakness, and irregular menses in women. Signs: tremors, tachycardia, lid lag, warm moist skin.

The clinical picture of thyroid storm is characterised by nonspecific signs ranging from thermoregulatory dysfunction to thyrotoxic heart disease (cardiotyrolysis).

Mechanism

  • The exact mechanisms are poorly understood. Thyroid storm occurs in undiagnosed or uncontrolled hyperthyroidism/thyrotoxicosis in the presence of a trigger.
  • A heightened cellular response to thyroid hormone is implicated, as seen during acute stress; increased or abrupt availability of free hormones enhances responsiveness to catecholamines.
  • It is postulated that a rapid increase in thyroid hormone, rather than the actual hormone level, leads to storm.
  • In up to 30% of patients no trigger can be identified.

10. Thyroid storm — diagnosis & scoring

The diagnostic problem

  • Diagnosis is challenging because of nonspecific clinical features, rarity, and the continuum of severity from thyrotoxicosis to storm.
  • No laboratory abnormality is specific to thyroid storm.
  • In the awake patient: hyperthermia, mental state changes (irritability, delirium, psychosis, lethargy, coma), hypotension, tachycardia, dysrhythmias, and gastrointestinal/hepatocellular dysfunction.
  • Intraoperative diagnosis is hardest — the only clues may be pyrexia and tachycardia. Mental state changes and gastrointestinal dysfunction are not detectable under general anaesthesia.
  • Tachycardia has many causes: pain, awareness, hypovolaemia, haemorrhage, medication side effects, bladder distension.
  • Rising temperature and end-tidal CO₂ may indicate sepsis, malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, phaeochromocytoma or anticholinergic syndrome.
  • Atypical presentations without fever have been described under general anaesthesia. During caesarean section under neuraxial anaesthesia, haemorrhage and hormonal changes can obscure thyrotoxicosis.
Practical approach

Follow a stepwise diagnostic approach when a patient under general anaesthesia develops intraoperative tachycardia or pyrexia: consider and exclude the differential diagnoses first, then treat.

Cases are excluded if other underlying diseases clearly cause any of the features — e.g. fever (pneumonia, malignant hyperthermia), impaired consciousness (psychiatric disorders, cerebrovascular disease), heart failure (acute myocardial infarction), liver disorders (viral hepatitis, acute liver failure). Where it is difficult to attribute a symptom to storm versus an underlying disease, the symptom should be regarded as due to the storm provoked by the precipitating factor. Clinical judgement is required.

Using the scores

Using both scores in an awake patient is recommended to increase diagnostic accuracy. They provide a systematic framework — but do not adhere rigidly for two reasons:

  1. Thyroid storm is partly a diagnosis of exclusion — sepsis with multi-organ failure could easily score > 45 on the BWPS, so a score > 45 does not prove the diagnosis.
  2. Treatment is reasonable in any patient with severe hyperthyroidism causing organ failure (especially heart failure), even if the BWPS is < 45.
11. Thyroid storm — management: "ABCs of the Convulsing Hot Hormonal Heart"
Overarching principles
  1. Consider and exclude the differential diagnoses.
  2. If thyroid storm is likely, implement the ABCs of the Convulsing Hot Hormonal Heart.
  3. Consider thyroidectomy in refractory cases and plasma exchange in storm complicated by acute hepatic failure.

A · General considerations

  • Inform the surgeon, declare an emergency, call for help.
  • Protect the airway in an awake and patient and administer supplemental oxygen.
  • Increase FiO₂ and optimise ventilation if intubated; confirm functioning intravenous access and establish a second intravenous access.
  • Consider the differential diagnosis (Table B3).
  • Treat the trigger — e.g. manage infection (Table B2).
  • Refractory disease: consider thyroidectomy.
  • Storm complicated with acute hepatic failure: consider plasma exchange.

12. Optimisation before surgery (thyrotoxic patient)
  • If hyperthyroidism or thyrotoxicosis is diagnosed preoperatively in a patient scheduled for elective surgery, postpone surgery and anaesthesia until clinically and biochemically euthyroid.
  • Achieving euthyroidism can take from 6 weeks up to 18 months.
  • In consultation with an endocrinologist, follow the patient up initially after 4–6 weeks, then at month 3, month 6, and then at yearly intervals.

Emergency surgery — anticipate a storm

When optimisation is not an option, anticipate and prepare for a perioperative thyroid storm. Premedication:

  • Beta-blocker: propranolol 0.1–0.15 mg/kg i.v.
  • Antithyroid drug: propylthiouracil 200–400 mg orally.
  • Corticosteroid: hydrocortisone 300 mg i.v.

Anaesthetic technique

  • Where possible, regional anaesthesia and peripheral nerve block are preferable to general anaesthesia.
  • If the procedure does not allow a regional technique, proceed with general anaesthesia ensuring:
    • The intubation response is blunted
    • Analgesia and depth of anaesthesia are adequate
    • A smooth emergence and extubation are performed
  • Vigilance regarding thyroid storm must extend into the postoperative period — monitor in a high-dependency unit.
Summary

Thyroid storm in an awake patient remains an elusive diagnosis; in an anaesthetised patient the diagnosis is even more challenging, and pharmacological options are limited to a few intravenous medications. In the awake patient, the BWPS and Akimizu criteria should form part of the assessment. When tachycardia and pyrexia occur under general anaesthesia, the differential diagnosis must include thyroid storm.
