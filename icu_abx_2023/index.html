1 Antimicrobial Stewardship & Dose Optimisation

Core aim: the right drug, at the right time, at the right dose, for the right duration — eradicate infection while minimising resistance selection, adverse effects and cost. Rapid source control is as important as the antibiotic.

The seven stewardship steps

  1. Rapid identification of infection. Make a clinical diagnosis before any antimicrobial. Obtain specimens for culture before the first dose, but do not delay therapy waiting for results — they are rarely available in the first 24 h. CRP and procalcitonin have suboptimal sensitivity/specificity; molecular diagnostics may give faster organism identification.
  2. Appropriate empirical therapy. Base on regularly updated local incidence and susceptibility data (community and hospital-acquired). Take recent antimicrobial exposure into account.
  3. Minimise time to first dose.
    • Septic shock, febrile neutropenia, bacterial meningitis: start immediately after obtaining specimens — or before, if the procedure will be delayed.
    • Possible sepsis without shock: obtain appropriate specimens/investigations first.
  4. Optimise dose and interval — critically ill patients have altered pharmacokinetics (see PK/PD table and appendices).
  5. De-escalation. When culture and sensitivity are available, choose the susceptible agent with the narrowest spectrum, least toxicity and lowest cost. Discontinue empirical therapy if testing or clinical correlation does not support infection.
  6. Appropriate duration. 7 days is generally appropriate — including septic shock ± bacteraemia and MDR organisms. Tailor to site/type of infection, source control, response and immune status. Procalcitonin-guided therapy shortens duration in units where courses exceed 10 days.
  7. IV → PO conversion. Proven efficacy, safety and economic benefit; eliminates IV-related adverse events, improves comfort and mobility, enables earlier discharge.

Key pharmacokinetic changes in the critically ill

  • Hydrophilic antibiotics (β-lactams, aminoglycosides, glycopeptides, polymyxins): low Vd, predominantly renal clearance, poor intracellular penetration — but increased Vd and altered clearance in critical illness → give a loading dose and monitor levels.
  • Hypoalbuminaemia → ↑ free fraction of highly protein-bound drugs (ceftriaxone, cloxacillin, ertapenem, vancomycin) → ↑ Vd and clearance → reduced exposure. ‎Ertapenem is not recommended in critically ill patients with hypoalbuminaemia.
  • Augmented renal clearance (ARC) — CrCl ≥ 130 mL/min/1.73 m², common in sepsis, burns, polytrauma, TBI, febrile neutropenia. Subtherapeutic β-lactam/vancomycin levels are significantly correlated with ARC → increase dose and monitor levels. An 8-hour urine collection confirms ARC.
  • Extracorporeal therapies. CRRT and SLED increase Vd and eliminate hydrophilic, low-protein-bound drugs — loading dose needed; maintenance depends on mode, dose delivered, blood/dialysate flow, filter material and surface area. Time-dependent drugs are more affected by SLED than intermittent HD (prolonged sub-MIC periods) — give supplemental doses during/after SLED or prolong infusion. No single RRT dosing guideline fits all modalities.
  • ECMO. Limited data. Sequestration in the circuit, ↑ Vd, ↓ elimination and direct membrane adsorption. Small-Vd hydrophilic drugs are prone to haemodilution and adsorption; lipophilic, highly protein-bound drugs with large Vd (e.g. voriconazole) are sequestered in the circuit. Use TDM where available.

Re-evaluation at 48–72 hours

Re-assess empirical therapy at 48–72 h or when cultures return. If the patient is improving and definitive source control has been achieved, 5–7 days is usually sufficient. Prolonged therapy increases resistance risk; exceptions include complicated S. aureus infection, osteomyelitis and infective endocarditis.

If there is poor clinical response at 48–72 h, consider:

  • Secondary (superadded) infection
  • Resistant organisms
  • Inadequate source control (undrained abscess, retained infected prosthesis)
  • Inadequate penetration of antimicrobial to the site
  • Inadequate spectrum of coverage
  • Inadequate dose or interval
  • Non-infective causes — DVT, acute myocardial or pulmonary infarction, acute pancreatitis, hyperthyroidism, Addisonian crisis, malignancy, CNS haemorrhage

Caution: in-vitro susceptibility ≠ clinical effectiveness — e.g. ESCAPPM organisms (Enterobacter, Serratia, Citrobacter freundii, Aeromonas, Proteus vulgaris, Providencia, Morganella). AmpC induction can cause resistance on therapy.

2 Prophylaxis & Empirical Therapy — Principles

Surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis

  • Timing: IV prophylaxis within 30–60 minutes before surgical incision or tourniquet inflation. Vancomycin within 120 minutes of incision (longer infusion time).
  • If the patient is already on an appropriate antimicrobial for another infection, an extra dose may be given within 60 minutes of incision.
  • Intraoperative redosing (interval timed from the first operative dose) if surgery lasts > 4 hours or blood loss > 1.5 L.
  • Discontinue within 24 hours unless a known infection is being treated.
  • Do not give additional prophylactic antibiotics after the incision is closed in clean and clean-contaminated procedures — this applies whether or not a drain is present.
  • Clean surgery generally does not require prophylaxis unless an implant/foreign body is placed, bone grafting is performed, or extensive dissection/high expected blood loss is anticipated.

Non-surgical prophylaxis examples: infective endocarditis in valvular heart disease undergoing dental procedures; encapsulated organisms in asplenic patients.

Choosing empirical therapy — three pillars

1. Likely causative organism

  • Community vs nosocomial. Identify the most likely source.
  • Use local epidemiological data and laboratory surveillance; know community/hospital/ICU resistance profiles.
  • Assess risk factors for MDR organisms (MDR Gram-negative bacilli, MRSA, VRE).

3. Antimicrobial profile

  • Route: IV for severe infection — oral absorption is unpredictable. Intrathecal or inhalational routes may improve target-site concentration.
  • Tissue penetration: aminoglycosides and glycopeptides penetrate poorly — aminoglycosides should not be used as monotherapy; higher plasma glycopeptide levels are needed for adequate tissue penetration. β-lactams and quinolones penetrate well, but higher doses are still needed for CNS infection.
  • Post-antibiotic effect (PAE): persistent suppression of growth after serum concentration falls below MIC — aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and carbapenems have PAE against Gram-negative bacteria.
  • Ecological profile: limit agents that select resistant organisms — e.g. third-generation cephalosporins select ESBL-producing Enterobacterales.
  • Adverse events: weigh risk–benefit case by case; monitor serum levels for drugs with serious toxicity (e.g. aminoglycosides).
3 MDR Gram-Negative Pathogens — CRE, AmpC-E, DTR-P. aeruginosa

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE)

  • Mechanisms: carbapenemase production, or non-carbapenemase enzymes with amplification of β-lactamase genes plus outer membrane porin disruption.
  • Most common carbapenemase variants in Malaysia: NDM-1 and OXA-48.
  • Risk factors for colonisation: underlying co-morbidity, prior antimicrobial exposure, indwelling catheters/devices, prolonged ICU or hospital stay, co-colonisation with other MDR organisms. Carbapenem use is a risk factor (15–75% of colonised patients).
  • Colonisation may persist up to 12 months; most Malaysian hospitals continue surveillance for a year. In high-incidence settings, active surveillance with pre-emptive contact isolation reduces transmission. Screening: rectal/perianal swab, molecular method.

AmpC β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (AmpC-E)

  • Emerging in ICU. β-lactam exposure can trigger a cascade leading to significant AmpC production and resistance.
  • Mechanisms: inducible chromosomal AmpC genes; non-inducible chromosomal; plasmid-mediated.

  • Carbapenems give reliable coverage against AmpC hyperproducers. Cefepime is an alternative — weak AmpC inducer and withstands AmpC hydrolysis.

4 Severe Community-Acquired Pneumonia (sCAP)

sCAP = pneumonia requiring respiratory (invasive/non-invasive) or vasopressor support. In Malaysia it is one of the three leading causes of ICU admission, with ~35% hospital mortality. Mortality risk factors: advanced age, co-morbidities, severity of illness on admission.

Common pathogens

S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Legionella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, respiratory viruses (COVID-19, influenza A & B). In Southeast Asia, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Burkholderia pseudomallei and Mycobacterium tuberculosis are identified in a higher proportion.

Treatment principles

  • Combination β-lactam + macrolide achieves better outcomes; most patients improve within 48–72 h.
  • If not responding, consider parapneumonic effusion, empyema, lung abscess — or non-infective causes (congestive heart failure, pulmonary embolism, neoplasm, pulmonary haemorrhage).
  • Empyema requires prolonged antibiotics; VATS debridement (VATS-D) reduces antibiotic duration and hospital stay.
  • A recent single-centre RCT showed benefit from early hydrocortisone in sCAP without contraindications to steroids — further studies needed before routine use.

5 Aspiration Pneumonia & Lung Abscess
  • Aspiration pneumonia = entry of gastric or oropharyngeal contents into the lower airways. Chemical pneumonitis = non-infectious inflammatory reaction to irritative gastric contents.
  • Antimicrobials are not indicated in aspiration without evidence of infection.
  • Consider antibiotics in chemical pneumonitis if it fails to resolve within 48 hours, in patients with small bowel obstruction, or on acid suppression therapy.

Lung abscess

  • Definition: liquefactive necrosis of lung parenchyma with cavity formation caused by microbial infection.
  • Primary — from an existing parenchymal process (aspiration pneumonia, necrotising pneumonia).
  • Secondary — bronchial obstruction (foreign body, neoplasm), co-existing lung disease (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis) or haematogenous dissemination (infective endocarditis, Lemierre's disease).
  • Aspiration-related abscesses are typically polymicrobial: Bacteroides, Prevotella, Peptostreptococcus, Fusobacterium, Streptococcus.
  • Less commonly complicating monomicrobial infection with pyogenic bacteria — S. aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa.
6 Hospital-Acquired & Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia
  • HAP: pneumonia occurring 48 hours after hospitalisation.
  • VAP: pneumonia occurring 48 hours after invasive mechanical ventilation.

Risk factors for MDR organisms

  • Prior IV antibiotics within 90 days
  • Previous colonisation or infection with an MDR organism
  • Additional MDR-VAP risks: septic shock at VAP onset; ARDS or acute RRT preceding VAP; ≥ 5 days hospitalisation before VAP onset

Malaysian Registry of Intensive Care (2020–2021): the commonest isolates were Acinetobacter spp., Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Key management points

  • Duration should not exceed 7 days — including MDR organisms. Longer may be appropriate with delayed clinical response.
  • In suspected VAP, non-invasive sampling (endotracheal aspirate) is preferred over BAL.
  • A positive respiratory culture does not distinguish true pathogen from coloniser — interpret in context to avoid unnecessary prescribing.

9 Infectious Diarrhoea & Clostridioides difficile

Acute infectious diarrhoea

  • Most cases in adults are self-limiting. Causes: viruses (norovirus, rotavirus), bacteria (Salmonella, Shigella, enterotoxigenic E. coli), protozoa (Cryptosporidium, Giardia, Cyclospora, Entamoeba).
  • Relevant history: stool characteristics, food ingestion, water exposure, travel, animal exposure.
  • Antimicrobials are not recommended in clinically stable patients without hypovolaemia (mild–moderate disease).
  • Consider empirical treatment in: significant hypovolaemia (severe disease), immunocompromised hosts, immunocompetent hosts with high-grade fever, severe symptoms, or bloody diarrhoea.

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI)

  • Definition: ≥ 3 watery stools per day with a positive C. difficile toxin. The commonest infectious cause of diarrhoea in ICU; may cause fulminant colitis, toxic megacolon and death.
  • Major risk factor: antibiotic use — clindamycin, cephalosporins, quinolones, penicillin.
  • Diagnosis: stool toxin detection. Colonoscopy findings of pseudomembranous colitis may aid diagnosis when lab confirmation is delayed or negative.
  • Discontinue the offending antibiotic whenever possible.
  • Recurrence in up to 27% — defined as symptom recurrence with a positive stool test within 8 weeks of completing therapy with symptom resolution. Patients excrete C. difficile for weeks after recovery — an infection-control challenge. Risk factors: age ≥ 65, immunocompromised, severe CDI. Fidaxomicin reduces recurrence but is not available locally.

10 Severe Acute Pancreatitis & Infected Pancreatic Necrosis
  • Prophylactic antibiotics are NOT recommended in severe acute pancreatitis — they do not reduce morbidity or mortality.
  • Antibiotics to prevent infection in sterile pancreatic necrosis are also not recommended.
  • Infected pancreatic necrosis with persistent organ failure carries high mortality. Diagnosis can be challenging — the clinical picture cannot distinguish other infections, persistent inflammation or necrosis.
  • Drugs that penetrate pancreatic necrosis well: β-lactams (ceftazidime, cefepime, piperacillin/tazobactam), carbapenems, quinolones and metronidazole.
  • Consider antifungal coverage if multiple risk factors for invasive candidiasis are present.
  • If not responding to antimicrobials, consider percutaneous or endoscopic drainage of pancreatic necrosis, peripancreatic collections, pseudocysts and abscesses.

13 Intra-Abdominal Infections

Classification

  • Uncomplicated IAI — infection originating from an abdominal organ without extension to the peritoneum (e.g. acute appendicitis).
  • Complicated IAI — extension into the peritoneum → localised peritonitis with abscess, or diffuse peritonitis.
  • Community-acquired IAIs: narrower-spectrum empirical therapy is adequate. Hospital-acquired IAIs often involve MDR organisms.

Diffuse peritonitis

  • Primary / spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) — bacterial translocation across an intact gut wall.
  • Secondary — microbial contamination through a perforation, laceration or necrotic GI segment.
  • Tertiary — persistent or recurrent infection ≥ 48 h after appropriate management of primary or secondary peritonitis.

Cornerstones of treatment: early recognition, adequate source control and appropriate antimicrobial therapy. After adequate source control, antimicrobials may be stopped in uncomplicated IAI. In complicated IAI, therapy is usually continued after source control and duration tailored to response; if source control is suboptimal, treatment is prolonged.

Peritoneal fluid specimen

  • Paracentesis to determine whether ascitic fluid infection is present in septic patients with ascites. Cultures from in-situ abdominal drains may be inaccurate due to contamination risk.
  • The decision to start early empirical antibiotics for suspected ascitic fluid infection is based largely on the absolute neutrophil count, since culture takes 24–48 h.

14 Central Nervous System Infections

General approach

  • Acute CNS infections have high morbidity and mortality. Approach: early recognition, rapid diagnostic tests, prompt antimicrobials and adjunctive therapy.
  • Classical bacterial meningitis: fever, headache, neck stiffness, then altered mental status — but most patients do not present with the full spectrum.
  • Perform lumbar puncture for CSF analysis and blood cultures. Choice of empirical antibiotic depends on age, immune status and predisposing conditions.
  • The antibiotic must be bactericidal, penetrate the CNS and achieve adequate CSF concentration (immune activity in CSF is poor).
  • Corticosteroids significantly reduce hearing loss and neurological sequelae but not overall mortality. Give dexamethasone before or with the first dose of empirical antibiotic in all adults with acute bacterial meningitis. Discontinue if the organism is not H. influenzae, S. pneumoniae or M. tuberculosis.
  • Viral encephalitis presents similarly but with more prominent neurological symptoms — altered sensorium, focal signs, seizures. Until CSF analysis suggests otherwise, empirical therapy includes acyclovir (specific only for herpes simplex and varicella zoster).
  • Healthcare-associated ventriculitis/meningitis — associated with EVDs, lumbar drains, craniotomy/plasty, VP shunts. Likely organisms: MDR Gram-negative bacilli and staphylococci.
  • LP and blood cultures before antibiotics. LP is crucial for aetiology and should be done after neurological examination without delaying antimicrobials. CT to rule out raised ICP before LP. CSF analysed within 1 hour (or stored at 4–8 °C).
  • PCR is standard for viruses associated with aseptic meningitis/encephalitis. Serology may be more appropriate for suspected arbovirus (West Nile, St. Louis, Eastern equine, Japanese encephalitis).

15 Urinary Tract Infection

Definitions

  • UTI: significant bacteriuria with signs/symptoms attributable to the urinary tract and no alternate source.
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria: ≥ 1 bacterial species with ≥ 10⁵ cfu/mL in a patient without signs or symptoms. Do not treat, except in pregnancy and before invasive urological procedures (TURP, ureteroscopy, lithotripsy).
  • Sterile pyuria: persistent white cells in urine without bacteria. Common in catheterised patients, no predictive value, no treatment warranted.
  • CAUTI: symptoms/signs consistent with UTI with no other source, with ≥ 1 bacterial species at ≥ 10³ cfu/mL in a catheterised patient — or in a patient whose catheter was removed within the past 48 h. Treat with antimicrobials and remove or change the catheter.
  • Complicated UTI: cystitis or pyelonephritis with underlying anatomical/functional abnormality (nephrolithiasis, strictures, stents, urinary diversion, neurogenic bladder), or in immunocompromised patients and males.

Acute pyelonephritis

  • Presume in flank pain or tenderness (± fever) with urinalysis showing bacteriuria and/or pyuria.
  • Cornerstones: antimicrobial therapy and source control.
  • Imaging (ultrasound or CT) to identify obstruction, abscess or necrotising infection (emphysematous pyelonephritis).
  • Hydronephrosis → percutaneous or endourological drainage. Abscess → drainage if large enough or the patient remains unstable. Emphysematous pyelonephritis usually requires partial or total nephrectomy.
  • Corticomedullary and perinephric abscesses usually arise from ascending infection. Renal abscesses > 5 cm should be considered for image-guided aspiration or surgical drainage.
  • S. aureus is the most likely organism in renal cortical abscesses (haematogenous spread) — often multiple and not drainable.

Urine specimen collection

  • Aseptic technique; send within 1 hour of collection (bacteria proliferate in warm fresh urine, increasing counts). If not sent immediately, store at 4 °C (may affect leucocyte counts).
  • If catheterisation is needed, discard the first few mL and collect the rest in a sterile container.
  • If already catheterised: clamp the catheter, clean the sampling port with 70% alcohol, collect 10 mL.
  • Do not take samples from the drainage bag — high risk of bacterial overgrowth causing false positives.
  • In-and-out catheterisation is acceptable in uncatheterised patients. In long-term catheters, replace the catheter before collecting.

17 Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infection (CLABSI)

Definitions

  • CLABSI: laboratory-confirmed bloodstream infection not related to infection at another site, developing within 48 hours of central line insertion. Diagnosis requires a positive blood culture and collaborative clinical and microbiological review.
  • CRBSI: bloodstream infection attributed to any intravascular catheter (central venous, peripheral or intra-arterial). Diagnosed when the same organism grows from paired peripheral and catheter-lumen samples with either:
    • Quantitative cultures — 3-fold greater colony count from the catheter sample, or
    • Differential time to positivity — catheter growth detected at least 2 hours earlier than the peripheral sample.
  • This section deals only with CLABSI associated with short-term CVCs. Suspect CLABSI with fever, chills ± hypotension and bacteraemia with no other possible site — catheter removal is warranted.
  • Initial empirical choice depends on severity of illness, risk factors and likely pathogens. In the Malaysian Registry of Intensive Care 2021 report, Gram-negative organisms predominate — so empirical Gram-negative cover is recommended.
  • Duration is generally 7 days in the absence of distant complications.
  • Complicated CLABSI: persistent bacteraemia/fungaemia despite CVC removal and appropriate therapy, or suppurative thrombophlebitis, or metastatic foci (endocarditis, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, septic emboli).

Blood cultures

  • Take before starting antibiotics. Avoid contamination with normal skin flora — Corynebacterium spp., Propionibacterium spp., CoNS, Bacillus sp., Micrococcus sp. In some circumstances these may be clinically significant — clinical correlation required.
  • Venepuncture is the preferred site; avoid sampling from an intravascular device unless diagnosing CRBSI.
  • Minimum 20 mL of blood — 10 mL each into aerobic and anaerobic bottles. Increasing to 40–60 mL from different venepuncture sites (2–3 pairs) increases yield further.
  • Skin antisepsis: 2% chlorhexidine in 70% isopropyl alcohol, circular scrubbing motion spiralling out from the planned site. Use a fresh swab for each scrub; use 2–3 scrubs.

  • Diverse presentations: pneumonia, localised cutaneous lesion, bacteraemia without evident focus, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, sepsis with multiple organ abscesses.

  • Males: CrCl (mL/min) = [(140 − age) × IBW (kg) × 1.23] ÷ serum creatinine (µmol/L)
  • Females: CrCl (mL/min) = [(140 − age) × IBW (kg) × 1.04] ÷ serum creatinine (µmol/L)
  • IBW = ideal body weight. In obese patients use adjusted body weight (AdjBW). If BMI < 18.5 kg/m², use actual body weight.
  • Alternative: GFR by CKD-EPI equation with removal of body surface area normalisation.

B Appendix B — Dosage Adjustment for Hypoalbuminaemia

Hypoalbuminaemia (serum albumin ≤ 25 g/L) reduces protein binding of highly protein-bound antimicrobials (ceftriaxone, cloxacillin, ertapenem, vancomycin), causing altered pharmacokinetics. The increased free (unbound) fraction raises Vd and drug clearance, which can reduce antimicrobial exposure and compromise the pharmacodynamic target.

Ertapenem is not recommended in critically ill patients with hypoalbuminaemia.

C Appendix C — β-Lactam Extended / Continuous Infusion

β-lactams exhibit time-dependent killing — the duration for which free drug concentration remains above the MIC correlates with bacterial killing.

  • TDM-guided dosing is recommended in critically ill patients because of uncertainty in pharmacodynamic targets.
  • Without TDM, extended infusion over 40–50% of the dosing interval can achieve the PK/PD target. Continuous infusion for 100% of the interval may be needed for serious infections or pathogens with elevated MIC.
  • Drug stability (which may differ between manufacturers) determines the choice between extended and continuous infusion. Given stability and feasibility concerns, extended infusion is often used to maximise PK/PD.

D Appendix D — Polymyxins Dosing Guide

Polymyxins are polypeptide antibiotics used for MDR organisms such as CRAB, CRE and CRPA. Some organisms are intrinsically resistant: Serratia spp., Proteus spp., Providencia spp., Morganella spp., Burkholderia cepacia.

  • Hydrophilic drugs with concentration-dependent and time-dependent kill characteristics.
  • Polymyxin B is administered in its active form and requires no renal dose adjustment (non-renal clearance).
  • Polymyxin E (colistin) is an inactive prodrug and requires dose adjustment in renal impairment. Both have the same spectrum of activity.

F Appendix F — Antimicrobial Dosing in Obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²)

Obese patients have a significant increase in fat mass with a smaller increase in lean tissue, making dosing weight difficult to determine. Vd determines the loading dose; clearance determines the maintenance dose.

  • Oral bioavailability does not change.
  • Hydrophilic antibiotics (β-lactams, glycopeptides): no change in Vd (limited distribution into fat) → loading dose unaffected.
  • Lipophilic antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, macrolides): large increases in Vd → larger loading doses required.
  • Renal and hepatic clearance of many drugs is increased → maintenance dose based on adjusted body weight.
