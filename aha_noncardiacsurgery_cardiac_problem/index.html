★ Top Take-Home Messages
Nine principles that drive every decision
  • Stepwise assessment — decide when to proceed vs. pause for further evaluation.
  • Same indications as non-surgical patients — screening/treatment should not be triggered by surgery itself; avoid overscreening, overtreatment and delays.
  • Stress testing judiciously — only if it would be appropriate independent of the planned surgery; especially avoid in low-risk patients.
  • Team-based care for complex anatomy or unstable cardiovascular disease.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: stop 3–4 days pre-op to avoid perioperative ketoacidosis (new diabetes/HF/obesity therapies matter).
  • MINS is a real disease — myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery must not be ignored.
  • New-onset perioperative AF → higher stroke risk; follow up closely, treat reversible triggers, assess rhythm control and long-term anticoagulation.
  • Bridging anticoagulation — selective use only in highest thrombotic risk; not recommended for most patients.
  • FoCUS can be used for unexplained haemodynamic instability when expertise exists — but it does not replace comprehensive TTE.

Functional capacity — METs & DASI

COR 2a B-NR In elevated-risk NCS, structured functional capacity assessment (e.g. DASI) is reasonable to stratify risk of perioperative adverse cardiovascular events.

Thresholds: Poor functional capacity = <4 METs or DASI ≤34. Climbing 2 flights of stairs ≈ >4 METs.

Key evidence: Subjective clinician assessment of functional capacity was not associated with outcomes in the METS study — use a structured tool (DASI). DASI ≤34 was associated with increased odds of 30-day death or MI.

Frailty assessment

COR 2a B-NR In all patients ≥65 years, and in those <64 with perceived frailty undergoing elevated-risk NCS, preoperative frailty assessment using a validated tool can be useful for evaluating risk and guiding management.

Impact: Meta-analysis of 56 studies (1.1 million older adults) — frailty associated with 30-day mortality RR 3.71 (95% CI 2.89–4.77) and 30-day complications RR 2.39 (2.02–2.83). Routine frailty screening in a single centre was associated with reduced 30-day mortality.

Preoperative biomarkers — BNP / NT-proBNP / troponin

COR 2a B-NR In patients with known CVD, or age ≥65, or age ≥45 with symptoms suggestive of CVD undergoing elevated-risk NCS — measure BNP or NT-proBNP before surgery to supplement perioperative risk evaluation.

COR 2b B-NR Same population — measuring cardiac troponin before surgery may be reasonable to supplement risk evaluation.

Practical points: Both have high prognostic value and excellent negative predictive value. Preoperative NT-proBNP >100 pg/mL was independently associated with all-cause mortality (VISION substudy). Adding NT-proBNP to traditional risk scores did not clearly improve prediction beyond risk scores + self-reported functional status. A preoperative baseline troponin helps interpret postoperative troponins and identify chronic myocardial injury.
Value is uncertain: No studies show that acting on an elevated preoperative biomarker improves perioperative outcomes. Utility in low-risk patients has not been evaluated, and downstream testing is a potential harm.
2 · Preoperative Cardiovascular Testing
12-lead ECG
  • 2a B-NR Known coronary heart disease, significant arrhythmia, peripheral arterial disease, cerebrovascular disease, other significant structural heart disease, or CVD symptoms undergoing elevated-risk surgery → preoperative resting 12-lead ECG reasonable to establish baseline and guide management.
  • 2a B-NR New abnormalities on preoperative ECG → further evaluation reasonable to refine cardiovascular risk.
  • 2b B-NR Asymptomatic patients, elevated-risk surgery, no known CVD → preoperative ECG may be considered.
  • 3: No benefit B-NR Asymptomatic patients undergoing low-risk procedures → routine preoperative ECG not recommended.
Abnormalities that may change management: Mobitz II or higher AV block, AF with rapid ventricular response / new-onset AF, prolonged QT (informs anaesthetic, antiemetic and antibiotic choice). Incidental Q-waves or bundle branch block should not trigger revascularisation before NCS.

Optimal interval between ECG and elective NCS is unknown. Comparing with prior tracings is helpful.

Left & right ventricular function

Left ventricle

  • 1 B-NR New dyspnoea, examination findings of HF, or suspected new/worsening ventricular dysfunction → perform preoperative LV function evaluation.
  • 2a C-LD Known HF with worsening dyspnoea or other change in clinical status → preoperative LV assessment reasonable.
  • 3: No benefit B-NR Asymptomatic, clinically stable → routine preoperative LV evaluation not recommended.

Lower LVEF, higher-risk surgery and additional comorbidity raise MACE risk. Grade 3 diastolic dysfunction carries ~70% higher MACE risk vs. grade 1–2. FoCUS by trained operators may identify patients needing fuller evaluation, but comprehensive TTE remains the standard.

Right ventricle: Reduced RV function (in MR, TR, PH) is independently associated with adverse outcomes. Routine preoperative RV assessment is not recommended in asymptomatic, stable patients. CMR is the gold standard for RV volumes/function in selected cases.

Stress testing & modality selection
  • 2b B-NR Elevated-risk NCS with poor or unknown functional capacity AND elevated risk by validated tool → stress testing may be considered for inducible ischaemia.
  • 3: No benefit B-R Low risk, adequate functional capacity with stable symptoms, or low-risk procedures → routine stress testing not recommended.
Central principle: The goal is not to find undiagnosed CAD. It is to identify patients in whom revascularisation would improve outcomes — essentially left main disease or severe multivessel disease with reduced LVEF. Preoperative revascularisation has not been shown to reduce perioperative MACE or cardiac mortality in stable patients. An abnormal stress test should not prompt angiography or revascularisation unless it shows high-risk features.

Exercise testing is preferred over pharmacological stress when the patient can exercise.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET)

In high-risk patients undergoing elevated-risk procedures with objectively reduced functional capacity, where extra physiological data are needed to inform perioperative care or guide optimisation, CPET may be beneficial for risk assessment of morbidity and mortality.

  • Gold standard for physiological response to exercise; diagnoses cardiac vs. pulmonary cause of exercise intolerance; guides prehabilitation.
  • Predicts all-cause morbidity (more common than cardiovascular complications alone) in major abdominal, vascular, bariatric and thoracic surgery.
  • Anaerobic threshold thresholds have fallen over time (11 → 9–10 mL/min/kg) reflecting evolution in surgical/perioperative practice.
  • Most perioperative CPET studies are retrospective, single-centre, with variable predictive precision.
  • The 6-minute walk test is easier (no specialist equipment) and also used for risk prediction; correlation with CPET is variable.
Coronary CTA & invasive coronary angiography

CCTA

  • 2b B-NR Elevated-risk surgery, poor/unknown functional capacity, elevated risk by validated tool → CCTA to detect high-risk coronary anatomy (≥50% left main stenosis or anatomically significant 3-vessel disease ≥70%) may be considered.
  • 3: No benefit B-NR Low risk, adequate functional capacity with stable symptoms, or low-risk procedures → routine CCTA not recommended.
CCTA overestimates risk — >5× as likely as clinical risk scores to inappropriately overestimate risk in patients who will not have MACE, potentially delaying/cancelling surgery. High negative predictive value. A prior coronary calcium score of 0 within 2 years → reasonable to proceed without further testing. Contraindicated/harmful in urgent or emergency surgery.

Invasive coronary angiography (ICA)

3: No benefit C-LD Routine preoperative ICA is not recommended to improve perioperative outcomes. Reserve ICA for the highest-risk patients and for indications similar to non-operative settings: ACS, accelerating angina despite maximal antianginal therapy, newly diagnosed moderate–severe ischaemia on stress testing, or indicators of obstructive left main disease on non-invasive testing.

3 · Cardiovascular Comorbidities
Coronary artery disease & revascularisation

CAD is prevalent in ~18% of major NCS and history of MI confers ~3.5× increased risk of perioperative MACE. Coronary stents in the prior 2 years → ~2× higher odds of perioperative MACE vs. matched controls.

  • 1 C-LD ACS being considered for elective NCS → revascularise as appropriate and defer surgery.
  • 2a C-LD CCD with haemodynamically significant left main stenosis ≥50% planning elective NCS → revascularisation + deferral is reasonable.
  • 3: No benefit B-R Non-left-main CAD planned for NCS → routine preoperative revascularisation not recommended.
Apply the 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI revascularisation guideline to NCS patients as long as surgery can be safely postponed to accommodate DAPT. Multidisciplinary input recommended for transplant, vascular and/or multivessel CAD.
Hypertension & perioperative blood pressure

Preoperative

  • 2a C-EO Most patients with hypertension planned for elective NCS → continue antihypertensive therapy throughout the perioperative period.
  • 2b C-LD Elective elevated-risk surgery + cardiovascular risk factors + poorly controlled hypertension (SBP ≥180 or DBP ≥110 mm Hg before the day of surgery) → deferring surgery may be considered.

Intraoperative

  • 1 B-NR Maintain MAP ≥60–65 mm Hg or SBP ≥90 mm Hg to reduce risk of myocardial injury.

Postoperative

  • 1 B-NR Treat hypotension (MAP <60–65 or SBP <90 mm Hg) to limit cardiovascular, cerebrovascular and renal events and mortality.
  • 1 C-EO Restart preoperative antihypertensives as soon as clinically reasonable to avoid complications of postoperative hypertension.
Caution with continuing antihypertensives in low/low-normal perioperative BP, adults ≥65 years, and when risk of perioperative hypotension is high (assess overall status, surgery type, anaesthetic plan).
Observational harm threshold: MAP <65 mm Hg or SBP <90 mm Hg sustained ~15 minutes. Three key trials (INPRESS, a single-centre MAP ≥60 vs ≥75 trial, POISE-3) do not support higher BP targets — there is insufficient trial evidence for targets above MAP 60–65/SBP 90.
Heart failure & cardiomyopathies

Heart failure (general)

  • HF is an established risk factor for poor outcomes after NCS. In a cohort of 159,237 patients, 18% had HF; HF carried significantly higher mortality and HF readmission than CAD.
  • Symptomatic HF: 90-day mortality 5.49%; asymptomatic HF 4.9%; no HF history 1.2%.
  • LVEF ≤30% identified on preoperative echo was an independent predictor of 30-day mortality in intermediate/high-risk procedures.
  • Continue guideline-directed medical therapy for HF through the perioperative period — discontinuation is harmful.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: stop 3–4 days before surgery (see Drugs section).

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)

3: Harm C-LD Avoid factors that aggravate or trigger dynamic outflow obstruction — positive inotropes, tachycardia, reduced preload — to reduce risk of haemodynamic instability.

Pulmonary hypertension
  • 1 C-LD Stable doses of targeted therapy for PAH undergoing NCS → continue these agents to reduce risk of perioperative MACE.
  • 2a C-LD Severe PH undergoing elevated-risk NCS → referral/consultation with a specialised PH centre (risk assessment, optimisation, postoperative management, consider ICU) is reasonable.
  • 2a C-LD Severe PH undergoing elevated-risk NCS → invasive haemodynamic monitoring is reasonable to guide intraoperative and postoperative care.
  • 2b C-EO Precapillary PH undergoing elevated-risk NCS → short-acting inhaled pulmonary vasodilators (nitric oxide, aerosolised prostacyclin) may be reasonable to reduce RV afterload and prevent acute decompensated right HF.
Definitions: Precapillary PH = mean PA pressure >20 mm Hg, PA wedge <15 mm Hg, PVR >2 Wood units. Severe PH = mean PA >40 mm Hg, PVR >5 Wood units, or significant RV morphological change (RV:LV diastolic diameter ratio >0.8, or moderate/severe RV dysfunction).

Outcome data: PH in NCS hospitalisations — 43% increased odds of death, MI or stroke; ~2× risk of cardiogenic shock and cardiac arrest. Group 1 disease: 2.5× MACE and 5× cardiogenic shock. PAH: major complications 6.1%, perioperative mortality 3.5% (15% in emergency procedures vs. 2% non-emergency).

VA-ECMO is the mechanical support of choice for PH-induced right HF; avoid RV assist devices.

Adult congenital heart disease (ACHD)

Patients with ACHD have increased in-hospital mortality, longer hospitalisation and higher 30-day readmission after high-risk NCS. Preoperative consultation with an ACHD specialist is advised. Where possible, perform NCS at a facility with an established ACHD programme.

LVAD & heart transplant recipients

LVAD — 1 C-EO Coordinate with the LVAD care team on timing and perioperative considerations of elective NCS to mitigate perioperative MACE.

  • Require therapeutic anticoagulation (pump thrombosis, stroke risk) → risk of bleeding.
  • Other risks: major infection, right HF, arrhythmias, respiratory failure, renal and hepatic dysfunction.
  • Higher odds of some MACE with elective NCS <6 months vs. ≥6 months after implantation — but data predate contemporary devices.
  • Weigh personalised surgical benefit against MACE risk and the risk of delaying elective NCS.

Heart transplant

  • Adult heart transplants in the US 2022: 3,668; 1-year survival >90%, 3-year >85%, 5-year >80%.
  • Unique issues: infection, wound-healing complications, acute kidney injury; acute rejection and immunosuppression complications (infection, steroid-induced hyperglycaemia, leukopenia) dominate in year 1; cardiac allograft vasculopathy, malignancy and CKD thereafter.
  • Consider adjusting immunosuppression if prolonged oral fasting is anticipated, or to minimise wound-healing complications.
  • Account for cardiac denervation of the transplanted heart, anaesthetic–immunosuppressant interactions, and transfusion decisions minimising HLA allosensitisation and CMV infection.
  • ISHLT guidance: preoperative assessment in collaboration with the transplant team, especially for major procedures requiring general or regional anaesthesia.
Valvular heart disease — aortic stenosis
  • 1 C-LD Severe AS → evaluate the need for aortic valve intervention before elective NCS.
  • 1 C-EO Suspected moderate or severe AS undergoing elevated-risk NCS → preoperative echocardiography recommended before elective NCS.
  • 2a C-LD Asymptomatic moderate or severe AS with normal LV systolic function on echo within the past year → reasonable to proceed with elective low-risk NCS.

Severe AS definition: aortic valve area <1.0 cm², mean gradient ≥40 mm Hg, or peak velocity ≥4.0 m/s (including low-flow, low-gradient AS).

Valvular heart disease — mitral stenosis, AR & MR, prior TAVI/TEER

Mitral stenosis

  • 1 B-NR Severe MS → evaluate need for MV intervention before elective NCS.
  • 2a C-EO Severe MS who cannot undergo MV intervention before NCS → perioperative invasive haemodynamic monitoring reasonable.
  • 2b C-LD Severe MS who cannot undergo MV intervention → perioperative heart-rate control (beta blockers, CCBs, ivabradine, digoxin) may be considered to prolong diastolic filling and reduce complications.

Goals: low-normal heart rate, high-normal systemic vascular resistance, adequate preload, rhythm control to maintain sinus rhythm. Tachycardia ↑ transvalvular gradient → ↑ LA pressure, pulmonary oedema, systemic hypotension. Avoid hypoxia/hypercapnia which worsen PH.

Chronic AR & MR

  • 1 C-EO Suspected moderate or severe regurgitation → preoperative echocardiography recommended before elective NCS.
  • 1 C-EO VHD meeting indications for intervention → consider valvular intervention before elective elevated-risk NCS.
  • 2a C-LD Asymptomatic moderate or severe MR, normal LV systolic function, PA systolic <50 mm Hg → reasonable to perform elective NCS.
  • 2a C-LD Asymptomatic moderate or severe AR, normal LV systolic function (LVEF >55%) → reasonable to perform elective NCS.
MR: avoid increased afterload and bradycardia. Vasodilation (general anaesthesia, neuraxial + opioid) is generally favourable — but maintain preload. Consider invasive monitoring ± TEE; intensive monitoring up to 24–72 h post-op. Risk factors for adverse outcome: lower EF, pre-existing AF.

AR: avoid bradycardia (prolongs diastolic filling time and worsens regurgitation). Meticulous volume status. Risk factors: LV systolic dysfunction, creatinine >2 mg/dL, intermediate/high-risk NCS. Consider arterial/venous catheters and TEE; intensive postoperative monitoring.

Prior TAVI or MV TEER

  • 2a B-NR After successful TAVI → reasonable to perform NCS early as clinically indicated (evidence supports safety within 30 days if indicated).
  • 2a C-EO After MV TEER → reasonable to perform NCS after successful intervention as clinically indicated (may be early, e.g. within 30 days, if residual MR is no longer severe).
Atrial fibrillation (pre-existing & new-onset POAF)
  • 2a C-LD Rapid AF in the setting of NCS → treat potential underlying triggers (sepsis, anaemia, pain).
  • 2a C-LD New-onset AF in the setting of NCS → initiating postoperative anticoagulation can be beneficial after weighing competing thromboembolic vs. bleeding risks.
  • 1 C-LD New-onset AF in the setting of NCS → outpatient follow-up for thromboembolic risk stratification and AF surveillance is recommended (high risk of recurrence).
Outcome data: POAF → 62% increased risk of early stroke and 44% increased risk of early mortality within 30 days; 37% increased long-term stroke risk and 37% increased long-term mortality. POAF is more strongly associated with stroke in NCS (HR 2.00) than cardiac surgery (HR 1.20). In a Danish registry, OAC within 30 days post-discharge was associated with 48% reduced thromboembolic events. POAF after NCS → 39% risk of AF recurrence at 5 years.

Rate/rhythm control: target heart rate <110 bpm in stable patients. AV nodal blockers (beta blockers, CCBs); digoxin as adjunct or if others contraindicated. Rhythm control (synchronised DCCV or pharmacological) if refractory. Exclude LA appendage thrombus before rhythm control if AF duration >48 h or high thromboembolic risk. Synchronised DCCV is recommended for haemodynamically unstable AF with rapid ventricular response and hypotension.

Pre-existing AF: higher risk of all-cause mortality, HF and ischaemic stroke within 30 days. If haemodynamically stable, generally no change in medical management other than OAC interruption. Optimise rate control before surgery if poorly controlled.

Cardiovascular implantable electronic devices (CIED)
  • 1 B-NR Elective NCS with anticipated EMI → develop a management plan before surgery: identify CIED type (pacemaker, ICD, implantable monitor), manufacturer and model.
  • 1 B-NR Pacemaker-dependent + surgery above the umbilicus with anticipated EMI → reprogramme the pacemaker or place a magnet on the generator to provide asynchronous mode (avoid pacing inhibition).
  • 1 B-NR Pacemaker-dependent with transvenous ICD, surgery above umbilicus with anticipated EMI → reprogramme; if not pacemaker-dependent → either reprogramming or magnet to inhibit tachytherapies/inappropriate shocks.
  • 1 B-NR Devices reprogrammed to asynchronous pacing or with tachytherapies off → restore device function postoperatively before hospital discharge.
  • 1 C-LD Leadless pacemakers, pacemaker-dependent, anticipated EMI above the umbilicus → reprogramme to asynchronous mode.
  • 2a C-LD Subcutaneous ICD, noncardiac/nonthoracic surgery with anticipated EMI above the groin → reasonable to reprogramme or use a magnet to temporarily disable tachytherapies.

Previous stroke / TIA & obstructive sleep apnoea

Stroke / TIA

2a B-NR History of stroke or TIA → delay elective NCS for ≥3 months after the most recent cerebrovascular event to reduce recurrent stroke and MACE.

Risk decay: Danish registry — OR for recurrent stroke/MI/CV death 14.23 (<3 months), 4.85 (3–6 months), 3.04 (6–11 months), 2.47 (≥12 months). Medicare analysis (~6 million patients): OR 8.0 (<30 days) and 5.0 (61–90 days), with only a very small decrement between 61–90 days and 6–12 months — delaying beyond 6 months does not appear to add protection.

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA)

2a B-NR Patients scheduled for NCS → OSA screening using a validated questionnaire is reasonable to assess risk of perioperative complications.

~34% of men and 17% of women aged 40–60 meet diagnostic criteria. OSA associated with increased composite postoperative cardiac and cerebrovascular complications; 2.5× risk of postoperative pulmonary complications; increased perioperative MI and AF (not HF). Risk increases with OSA severity. In one prospective study of 1,218 patients without prior OSA diagnosis scheduled for major NCS: 67.6% newly diagnosed, 30.5% at least moderate, 11.2% severe; severe OSA → increased composite of myocardial injury, cardiac death, HF, thromboembolism, AF and stroke within 30 days.
4 · Perioperative Medications
Statins
  • 1 B-NR Currently on statins and scheduled for NCS → continue statin therapy to reduce MACE risk.
  • 1 B-R Statin-naïve adults meeting criteria for statin use (ASCVD history or 10-year risk assessment) scheduled for NCS → initiate statin perioperatively with intention of long-term use.
The LOAD trial (largest RCT, n=648 statin-naïve) found no difference in 30-day MACE with atorvastatin loading. Benefits of reloading/intensification are uncertain. Do not delay surgery if LDL-cholesterol is not already available. The NCS setting is an opportunity to start therapy for long-term benefit.
RAAS inhibitors (ACEi / ARB)
  • 2b B-R Select patients on chronic RAASi for hypertension undergoing elevated-risk NCS → omission 24 hours before surgery may be beneficial to limit intraoperative hypotension.
  • 2a C-EO Patients on chronic RAASi for HFrEF → perioperative continuation is reasonable.
RCTs show intraoperative hypotension (MAP <60 mm Hg) is more common with continuation, but continuation vs. omission did not result in worse clinical outcomes (e.g. MINS). Patients with SBP >160 or <105 mm Hg and those with HFrEF are largely excluded from RCTs. No data on sacubitril/valsartan. Individualised approach warranted.
Calcium channel blockers & alpha-2 agonists

Calcium channel blockers — no formal recommendation. Meta-analyses show initiation does not reduce perioperative mortality or MI. Continuation may be reasonable, recognising potential for intraoperative hypotension (dihydropyridines) and/or bradycardia (non-dihydropyridines).

Alpha-2 receptor agonists

3: No benefit B-R Initiation of low-dose clonidine perioperatively is not recommended to reduce cardiovascular risk.

POISE-2 (n=10,010): no difference in death or nonfatal MI at 30 days; significantly more nonfatal cardiac arrest, clinically significant bradycardia and hypotension with clonidine. Perioperative continuation of chronic therapy has not been studied in RCTs; abrupt discontinuation can cause rebound hypertension.

1 B-NR CAD undergoing elective NCS → perioperative antiplatelet management and timing of surgery should be determined by a multidisciplinary team with shared decision-making, weighing bleeding, thrombosis and consequences of delay.

Perioperative beta blockers
  • 1 B-NR On stable doses → continue beta blockers through the perioperative period as appropriate to clinical circumstances.

Perioperative blood glucose & diabetes drugs
  • 2a B-NR Diabetes or at risk, elective NCS → preoperative HbA1c testing reasonable if not performed in ≤3 months.

SGLT2i hold times: canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin — stop ≥3 days; ertugliflozin — stop ≥4 days before scheduled surgery. HbA1c: no validated risk thresholds; it may be reasonable to postpone elective surgery if HbA1c >8%. Do not delay emergency/time-sensitive procedures to achieve a target HbA1c — focus on perioperative glucose control. Preoperative glucose ≥200 mg/dL was associated with >2-fold all-cause and >4-fold cardiovascular mortality.

5 · Anaesthetic & Intraoperative Management
Choice of anaesthetic technique & agent
  • 2a A Volatile-based anaesthetic or total intravenous anaesthesia — no apparent difference in associated cardiovascular events (MI, ischaemia) for general anaesthesia.
  • 2a B-R Where neuraxial is feasible → either neuraxial or general anaesthesia is reasonable with no apparent difference in cardiovascular events.

Multiple RCTs (sevoflurane vs. propofol ± remifentanil) showed no difference in troponin release, NT-proBNP, MACE, delirium or mortality. Oxygen concentration (30% vs. 80% FiO₂) was not associated with myocardial injury or NT-proBNP release.

Assess non-cardiac risk factors too: type and duration of surgery, comorbidities, patient preference, coagulation status.

Perioperative pain management
  • 2a B-R Major abdominal surgery → epidural analgesia for postoperative pain relief is reasonable to decrease perioperative cardiac events.
  • 2b B-R Hip fracture awaiting surgical repair → epidural analgesia may be considered to decrease preoperative cardiac events.
Evidence: Thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA) in abdominal aortic surgery — Cochrane review found decreased incidence of MI vs. IV systemic opioid analgesia. RCT in major abdominal surgery: postoperative myocardial injury 8.33% (TEA) vs. 36.67% (IV); supraventricular tachyarrhythmia 11.66% vs. 36.67%. NSQIP analysis >8,000 patients: epidural analgesia reduced cardiopulmonary complications (OR 0.58) in open abdominal surgery. Lumbar epidural for hip fracture: 2022 meta-analysis of 4 RCTs supported reduced perioperative MACE.

Gabapentinoids are less successful as opioid-sparing adjuncts and cause visual disturbance, dizziness, respiratory depression and postoperative delirium in patients ≥65 years.

Intraoperative echocardiography (TEE / FoCUS)
  • 2a C-LD Unexplained haemodynamic instability during NCS → emergency perioperative TEE or FoCUS is reasonable to determine the cause if expertise is readily available.
  • 3: No benefit C-LD NCS without risk factors or procedural risks for significant haemodynamic compromise → routine intraoperative TEE not recommended to screen for cardiac abnormalities or monitor for myocardial ischaemia.

Echo assesses biventricular and valvular function, intracardiac structures, pericardial space and thoracic aorta. The left atrial appendage and thoracic aorta are better evaluated with TEE. If more information is needed, perform a comprehensive examination. Recommendations suggest appropriately trained practitioners perform TEE/FoCUS.

Body temperature, mechanical circulatory support & PA catheters

Body temperature

2a B-R CVD undergoing NCS → maintenance of normothermia is reasonable to avoid perioperative complications overall.

Hypothermia is associated with wound infection, MACE, immune dysfunction, coagulopathy, increased blood loss, death and transfusion requirements. Hypothermia is proarrhythmogenic (↑ sympathetic activity) and shivering creates oxygen supply–demand imbalance. Note: PROTECT trial (n=5,056) found no significant difference in 30-day major cardiovascular outcomes between aggressive warming to 37°C vs. routine management to 35.5°C.

Temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS)

2b C-LD Acute, severe haemodynamic instability and cardiopulmonary dysfunction undergoing urgent/emergency NCS → temporary MCS devices may be used preemptively or as rescue therapy.

No high-quality evidence for routine use in patients at risk of cardiogenic shock undergoing NCS. Emergency use has increased. Options: intra-aortic balloon pump counterpulsation, ECMO, percutaneous ventricular assist devices. Multidisciplinary approach essential (anticoagulation, MCS management, haemodynamic monitoring, infection prevention).

Pulmonary artery catheters

  • 2b C-LD CVD undergoing NCS → PA catheterisation may be considered when underlying conditions that significantly affect haemodynamics (decompensated HF, severe valvular disease, combined shock states, PH) cannot be corrected before surgery.
  • 3: No benefit A Routine use of PA catheterisation is not recommended to reduce morbidity or mortality.
Perioperative anaemia management
  • 2a A NCS with expected blood loss → tranexamic acid is reasonable to reduce intraoperative blood loss, reduce transfusions and avoid anaemia.
  • 2a B-R Iron deficiency anaemia undergoing elective NCS → preoperative iron therapy (oral or IV) is reasonable to reduce blood transfusions and increase haemoglobin.
Definitions & thresholds: WHO anaemia = Hb <13 g/dL (men), <12 g/dL (women). Up to 64% of surgical patients are anaemic; iron deficiency causes 40–50% of cases. Ferritin <100 ng/mL, transferrin saturation <20%, and/or microcytic hypochromic red cells (MCV <80 fL, MCHC <27 g/dL) indicate iron deficiency. Restrictive transfusion threshold of 8 g/dL recommended for orthopaedic surgery and patients with CVD (7 g/dL likely comparable, but evidence lacking).

Iron therapy: IV iron is more effective than oral (which has low bioavailability, poor tolerance, long duration) — even a single dose increases Hb and iron stores and reduces transfusion and readmission. Ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose and ferumoxytol allow higher single doses; iron sucrose is less stable, max 200–300 mg per infusion.

Tranexamic acid: safe even in high-risk arthroplasty patients with prior VTE, MI, seizures, stroke, TIA, renal disease and AF — associated with fewer transfusions without increased VTE, MI, seizures, stroke or TIA. In POISE-3, the composite bleeding outcome was significantly lower with tranexamic acid; net clinical benefit is patient-specific (worthwhile for those at increased bleeding risk, potentially harmful for those at increased adverse cardiovascular event risk).

6 · Postoperative Surveillance: MINS, STEMI / NSTEMI
Myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS)

Surveillance

  • 2b B-NR Known CVD, symptoms of CVD, or age ≥65 with cardiovascular risk factors undergoing elevated-risk NCS → measuring cTn at 24 and 48 hours after surgery may be reasonable to identify myocardial injury.
  • 3: No benefit B-NR Low-risk NCS → routine postoperative cTn screening not indicated without signs/symptoms suggestive of ischaemia or MI.

Management

  • 2a B-NR MINS → outpatient follow-up is reasonable for optimisation of cardiovascular risk factors, especially in those not previously known to have excess risk.
  • 2b C-LD MINS → antithrombotic therapy may be considered to reduce thromboembolic events.
Epidemiology & prognosis: Perioperative myocardial injury occurs in ~20% of NCS. Overall 30-day mortality associated with MINS is ~10%, with risk proportional to peak cTn (17% in highest quartile vs. 1% in lowest); 34% population attributable risk of 30-day postoperative mortality. 80–90% of MINS patients have no ischaemic signs or symptoms but still have substantial 30-day mortality. Predictors: cardiovascular risk factors and disease, kidney disease, urgent/emergency surgery.
Diagnosis: ≥1 elevated cTn >99th percentile URL of presumed ischaemic origin (exclude non-ischaemic causes such as pulmonary embolism, stroke, sepsis). A baseline preoperative value or serial postoperative measures help determine whether injury is acute or chronic.
MANAGE trial (only RCT of medical therapy for MINS, n=1,754): dabigatran 110 mg twice daily significantly decreased major vascular events without increasing major bleeding, but increased minor and GI bleeding; ~half discontinued study drug; post hoc change in primary outcome. Optimal medical therapy for MINS remains an important evidence gap. Observational data support cardiology consultation/referral (associated with lower early mortality), and statin/antiplatelet use (associated with lower 1-year mortality).

Management of postoperative STEMI / NSTEMI
  • 1 B-NR STEMI after NCS → consider GDMT including consideration of invasive coronary angiography, balancing bleeding and thrombotic risks with severity of clinical presentation.
STEMI: due to acute plaque rupture in a minority of perioperative MI but associated with in-hospital mortality of 30–35%. Initiate GDMT promptly, balancing bleeding and hypotension; strongly consider emergent ICA. Team-based decisions (surgeon, anaesthesiologist, cardiologist).
NSTEMI: receive GDMT as recommended for nonsurgical spontaneous MI, tailored to haemodynamic status and bleeding risk. Include at least 1 antiplatelet (if benefits outweigh bleeding risk) and a high-intensity statin. Beta blockers, ACEi and nitrates may be considered for symptom relief and long-term risk reduction if haemodynamics permit.
Invasive management for NSTEMI: may be appropriate in selected patients — retrospective analyses showed lower in-hospital mortality with invasive vs. conservative management. Balance against residual postoperative bleeding risk, type of surgery and time since surgery. Greatest benefit likely in ongoing symptoms unresponsive to GDMT, haemodynamic instability, persistent biomarker elevation, or imaging evidence of new regional wall motion abnormality or reduced ventricular function.

Incidence & risk: perioperative MI 0.9–15% depending on definitions, risk factors and surgical type. Type 2 MI (supply–demand mismatch) is more common than type 1. Recognition is difficult — sedation and analgesia limit symptoms and reporting. Nearly one-third of patients with perioperative STEMI/NSTEMI die or are readmitted at 30 days. Mortality risk factors: peak cTn, bleeding events, peripheral artery disease.

7 · Special Populations
Liver & kidney transplantation candidates

Patients with end-stage kidney or liver disease have higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors and CAD, plus risk of HF and PH.

  • A targeted approach to preoperative CAD screening before kidney transplantation appears associated with similar postoperative outcomes vs. routine preoperative testing.
  • US Renal Data System/Medicare: more frequent invasive or non-invasive CAD testing in the year before kidney transplantation was not associated with lower 30-day post-transplant death or acute MI.
  • ISCHEMIA-CKD (advanced CKD, CCD, moderate–severe ischaemia): initial invasive approach did not reduce death or MI vs. initial medical therapy at median 2.2 years.

Refer to the 2022 AHA scientific statement on coronary heart disease screening in kidney and liver transplantation candidates.

Obesity & bariatric surgery

Obesity affects more than one-third of US adults. Bariatric surgery is the most effective and durable weight-loss intervention for BMI ≥35 kg/m², improving or resolving type 2 diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidaemia.

Outcomes: population-based cohort (n=2,638) — bariatric surgery associated with lower MACE incidence in patients with CVD and obesity over median 4.6 years. Large meta-analyses: perioperative MI in 0.37% of bariatric surgeries; all-cause mortality 0.08%. In a retrospective multicentre study (n=494,611) of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass or sleeve gastrectomy, prior cardiac history was associated with greater risk of perioperative cardiac arrest and 30-day mortality; sleeve gastrectomy had fewer adverse events than Roux-en-Y.

Careful attention to history and risk factors during preoperative assessment is essential. Patients with obesity are increasingly prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonists — see glucose section for discontinuation guidance.

8 · 10 High-Yield Clinical Cases
Case 1 — DES placed for ACS 2 months ago, needs elective hemicolectomy

A 68-year-old man had a drug-eluting stent placed for an acute coronary syndrome 2 months ago. He is on aspirin and ticagrelor. Now presents for elective right hemicolectomy for colon cancer.

Answer Elective NCS should be delayed ≥12 months after DES-PCI for ACS when interruption of ≥1 antiplatelet agent is required (1 B-NR). This is not a case for elective surgery at 2 months — the stent thrombosis risk is highest in the first 3 months and remains elevated to 1 year when PCI was for ACS. If the indication becomes time-sensitive (e.g. obstructing tumour), NCS may be considered ≥3 months after PCI only if the risk of delaying surgery outweighs MACE risk (2b B-NR), and DAPT should be continued through the perioperative period unless bleeding risk outweighs the benefit of preventing stent thrombosis. Multidisciplinary team decision with the patient, surgeon, anaesthetist and cardiologist is mandatory.
Case 2 — Asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis, LVEF 60%, low-risk surgery

A 74-year-old woman has asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis (AVA 0.8 cm², mean gradient 45 mm Hg), LVEF 60%, and an echocardiogram from 4 months ago. She needs an elective inguinal hernia repair under general anaesthesia.

Answer In asymptomatic moderate or severe AS with normal LV systolic function assessed by echocardiography within the past year, it is reasonable to proceed with elective low-risk NCS (2a C-LD). However, monitor closely throughout surgery and the early postoperative period to avoid hypotension, excessive hypertension and tachycardia, and to avoid dehydration or volume overload. If the surgery were elevated-risk, or if she were symptomatic (exertional dyspnoea, angina, HF, syncope/presyncope), she should be evaluated for AVR before elective elevated-risk NCS (1 C-LD), with a multidisciplinary heart valve team discussion regarding TAVI vs. SAVR. Note that general anaesthesia is appropriate here — do not be deterred by the AS alone, but have a low threshold for invasive monitoring and consider intraoperative TEE.
Case 3 — Pacemaker-dependent patient, surgery above the umbilicus with monopolar diathermy

A 79-year-old pacemaker-dependent man with a transvenous dual-chamber pacemaker is scheduled for an open cholecystectomy. Monopolar diathermy will be used above the umbilicus.

Answer Electromagnetic interference (EMI) is anticipated and the source will be <15 cm from the generator. Because he is pacemaker-dependent, the pacemaker should be reprogrammed to an asynchronous mode or a magnet placed on the generator to avoid pacing inhibition (1 B-NR). Confirm the device type, manufacturer and model beforehand, and confirm asynchronous (VOO/DOO) pacing at the expected rate (magnet rates typically 85–100 bpm and fall with battery depletion). Ensure external pacing and/or defibrillation is available. Use the lowest feasible energy with intermittent bursts of monopolar diathermy; place the dispersive electrode as far as possible from the generator and heart, with the current path not crossing the generator or leads. Restore device function postoperatively before hospital discharge (1 B-NR). Bipolar diathermy and ultrasonic scalpels are unlikely to cause EMI.
Case 4 — Patient on warfarin for AF, elective elevated-risk surgery

A 71-year-old man on warfarin for atrial fibrillation (CHA₂DS₂-VASc 4, no prior stroke) is scheduled for elective open ventral hernia repair with expected moderate bleeding risk. Should he be bridged with LMWH?

Answer No. In most patients undergoing elective NCS where OAC interruption is warranted, routine periprocedural bridging is not recommended due to increased bleeding risk (3: Harm C-LD). Use a multidisciplinary, time-based interruption approach (1 B-NR): hold warfarin ~5 days pre-op, check INR on the day of surgery, and resume once haemostasis is achieved — VKA can be restarted as early as 12–24 h post-op for low/moderate bleeding risk procedures, at the previous therapeutic dose. Bridging with parenteral heparin can be effective in high thrombotic risk patients on VKA (2a C-LD) — e.g. mechanical mitral valve — with careful consideration of bleeding risk (HAS-BLED, prior bleeding, procedural risk). The BRIDGE and PERIOP-2 trials both showed increased bleeding with bridging without a reduction in thromboembolism.
Case 5 — SGLT2 inhibitor before major surgery

A 63-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes and HFrEF (LVEF 35%) takes empagliflozin, metformin and bisoprolol. She is scheduled for elective laparoscopic sigmoid colectomy in 4 days.

Answer Stop empagliflozin 3–4 days before surgery (1 C-LD) — canagliflozin, dapagliflozin and empagliflozin should be stopped ≥3 days and ertugliflozin ≥4 days before scheduled surgery. The concern is euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (glucose <250 mg/dL with pH <7.3, bicarbonate <18 mEq/L, elevated serum/urine ketones). Continue metformin (2a C-LD) — it is not associated with lactic acidosis and has cardiovascular benefits. Continue bisoprolol — she is on a stable dose for HFrEF, and acutely discontinuing beta blockers for long-term indications is harmful (1 B-NR). Do not restart the SGLT2 inhibitor until she is clinically stable and has resumed a normal diet. Check HbA1c if not done within 3 months.
Case 6 — Postoperative troponin rise, asymptomatic, day 1

A 66-year-old man with hypertension and diabetes undergoes open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair. On day 1, high-sensitivity troponin is elevated above the 99th percentile URL with a rise-and-fall pattern. He has no chest pain, the ECG shows no ischaemic changes, and he is haemodynamically stable.

Answer This is MINS (myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery) — an elevated cTn >99th percentile URL of presumed ischaemic origin, without meeting the Universal Definition of MI criteria, in a patient who cannot reliably report symptoms due to anaesthesia/analgesia. Recognise and document the diagnosis; outpatient follow-up is reasonable for optimisation of cardiovascular risk factors (2a B-NR), and antithrombotic therapy may be considered (2b C-LD). MINS is associated with ~10% 30-day mortality and a 34% population attributable risk of 30-day postoperative mortality — even in the 80–90% without ischaemic signs or symptoms. Cardiology consultation/referral has been associated with reduced early mortality. Continue troponin surveillance at 24 and 48 hours in this elevated-risk patient (2b B-NR). The optimal medical therapy for MINS remains uncertain — the MANAGE trial (dabigatran) is the only RCT.
Case 7 — Postoperative AF with rapid ventricular response and hypotension

A 75-year-old woman develops new-onset atrial fibrillation on day 2 after major abdominal surgery. Ventricular rate is 150 bpm, BP 82/50 mm Hg. She has no prior history of AF.

Answer First, treat reversible triggers — pain, sepsis, anaemia, electrolyte imbalance, fluid shifts (2a C-LD). In haemodynamically unstable AF with a rapid ventricular response associated with hypotension, synchronised direct current cardioversion is recommended. Exclude left atrial appendage thrombus before rhythm control if AF duration >48 h or high thromboembolic risk. If stable, target heart rate <110 bpm using AV nodal blockers (beta blockers, CCBs), with digoxin as adjunct or if others are contraindicated. Initiation of postoperative anticoagulation can be beneficial after weighing competing thromboembolic and bleeding risks (2a C-LD). Arrange outpatient follow-up for thromboembolic risk stratification and AF surveillance (1 C-LD) — POAF after NCS carries a 39% risk of AF recurrence at 5 years, a 62% increased risk of early stroke and 44% increased risk of early mortality. Consider 1–2 week ambulatory ECG monitoring, 30-day event monitoring or implantable cardiac monitoring in selected patients.
Case 8 — Elderly patient on chronic beta blocker, urgent hip fracture

An 82-year-old man on long-term bisoprolol for ischaemic heart disease sustains a hip fracture and needs surgery within 24–48 hours. He has poor functional capacity (<4 METs).

Answer Continue bisoprolol through the perioperative period (1 B-NR) — acutely discontinuing beta blockers for long-term indications is harmful and should be avoided. This is a time-sensitive procedure, so do not delay surgery for extensive cardiac testing; the stepwise approach does not require further evaluation when surgery is urgent. Consider lumbar epidural analgesia for pain control — it may decrease the incidence of preoperative cardiac events in hip fracture patients awaiting repair (2b B-R). Maintain intraoperative MAP ≥60–65 mm Hg or SBP ≥90 mm Hg (1 B-NR) and treat postoperative hypotension (MAP <60–65 or SBP <90 mm Hg) promptly (1 B-NR). Restart his antihypertensives as soon as clinically reasonable. Frailty assessment is indicated in all patients ≥65 years undergoing elevated-risk NCS (2a B-NR) and may inform goals-of-care discussions.
Case 9 — Chronic ACE inhibitor and elevated-risk surgery

A 59-year-old woman with well-controlled hypertension on ramipril 10 mg daily (no HF, normal LVEF) is scheduled for elective open nephrectomy.

Answer In select patients on chronic RAAS inhibitors for hypertension undergoing elevated-risk NCS, omission 24 hours before surgery may be beneficial to limit intraoperative hypotension (2b B-R). RCTs show intraoperative hypotension (MAP <60 mm Hg) occurs more commonly with continuation, but continuation vs. omission did not result in worse clinical outcomes (e.g. MINS). Note that patients with high (SBP >160 mm Hg) or low (SBP <105 mm Hg) BP are often excluded from the RCTs, and there is limited enrolment of high-risk patients including those with HFrEF. If she had HFrEF, continuation would be reasonable (2a C-EO) given the important role of RAASi in preventing MI, stroke, HF and decline in kidney function. An individualised approach is warranted; larger RCTs (STOP-or-NOT) are ongoing.
Case 10 — Recent ischaemic stroke, elective surgery

A 70-year-old man had a small ischaemic stroke 6 weeks ago with good functional recovery. He now presents for elective transurethral resection of the prostate for bothersome symptoms.

Answer In patients with a history of stroke or TIA, it is reasonable to delay elective NCS for ≥3 months after the most recent cerebrovascular event to reduce the incidence of recurrent stroke and/or MACE (2a B-NR). At 6 weeks, he is still within the highest-risk window: the odds ratio for recurrent stroke/MI/cardiovascular death was 14.23 within 3 months, falling to 4.85 at 3–6 months. A Medicare analysis (~6 million patients) confirmed markedly elevated odds for operations within 30 days (OR 8.0) and 61–90 days (OR 5.0) after prior stroke. Importantly, there was only a very small decrement in risk between 61–90 days and 6–12 months — delaying beyond 6 months does not appear to provide additional protection. Defer the elective procedure, optimise secondary prevention per the 2021 AHA/ASA stroke prevention guideline, and reassess after the 3-month window. If the surgery becomes time-sensitive, individualise with the multidisciplinary team.
