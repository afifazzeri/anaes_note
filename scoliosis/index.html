1 · Overview, Classification & Indications for Surgery
  • Prevalence: abnormal lateral curvature of the spine in 2–3% of the population; usually becomes evident between 10–15 years.
  • Sex: slightly more common in females, but females progress to surgical correction 8× as often.
  • Idiopathic = most common (65% of cases; ~80% of adolescent cases). Other categories: congenital and neuromuscular (cerebral palsy, spinal cord trauma, spinal muscular atrophy, spina bifida, muscular dystrophy).
  • Curve pattern: usually right-sided (right convex), involving 7–10 vertebrae.
    Left convex curve → higher likelihood of associated anomalies: spinal cord 20%, genitourinary 20–33%, cardiac 10–15%.
  • Surgical indication: Cobb angle generally > 45–50° (idiopathic curves may stay functionally normal until ~65°).
  • Cobb angle ≥ 100° → likely severe cardiac and respiratory dysfunction.

2 · Preoperative Assessment

A detailed history and physical exam is essential — advanced scoliosis carries significant pulmonary, cardiac and neurologic comorbidity. Baseline exercise tolerance and respiratory function are general predictors of the ability to withstand major surgery.

Pulmonary function

  • Severity depends on: underlying cause (e.g. neuromuscular disease), speed of onset, and degree of curvature.
  • Mechanism: decreased lung volumes from restricted diaphragm/chest wall movement → poor ventilation distribution → hypercarbia, hypoxaemia, infection.
  • Reduced chest wall compliance causes restrictive disease; exercise tolerance may be reduced even with normal lung volumes.
  • PFT pattern in restriction: ↓ FEV₁, ↓ FVC, normal FEV₁/FVC ratio, ↓ TLC, but residual volume is generally maintained.
  • Clinical signs of significant impairment: dyspnoea at rest or on activity, inability to cough or clear secretions.
  • PFTs are important but not always possible — especially in severe cerebral palsy.

Cardiac function

  • Rare, but severe scoliosis can progress to significant cardiac disease: regional hypoventilation + chronic hypercarbia/hypoxaemia → pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular failure.
  • Neuromuscular scoliosis (e.g. Duchenne muscular dystrophy, long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency) — the primary disease can cause dilated cardiomyopathy.
  • Evaluate with echocardiography if there is significant exercise intolerance, JVP distension, hepatic congestion, or lower limb oedema.
  • Mitral valve prolapse may be present in 25% of children with scoliosis.

Non-idiopathic scoliosis — special considerations

ConditionAnaesthetic relevance
Cerebral palsy Non-progressive neurological disease of fetal/neonatal brain development; movement limitation and spastic paralysis. Often some degree of cognitive impairment — may be severe and limit cooperation with preoperative testing (e.g. PFTs) and preparation.
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) Genetic, progressive muscle degeneration. Proximal weakness and pseudohypertrophy from early childhood → skeletal deformity (incl. scoliosis), paralysis, cardiorespiratory failure.
Mitochondrial disease Multiorgan dysfunction from inadequate cellular energy; genetic defects of mitochondrial metabolism. Can affect every system, sometimes causing profound neuromuscular disease leading to scoliosis.
These patients often already receive specialised care — obtain and review those records before surgery.
3 · Preoperative Optimisation

Scoliosis correction is never an emergency — there is usually time to optimise, which benefits patients with significant comorbidity most.

  • Nutrition: optimise to aid recovery; correct underlying anaemia.
  • Pulmonary: treat and control reversible causes — asthma, pneumonia.
  • Cardiac: optimise volume status and blood pressure control.
  • Logistics: notify the blood bank and intensive care unit in advance for adequate preparation and resource allocation.
  • Pre-admission clinic (≥2 weeks before surgery): full assessment, optimisation, and a detailed discussion of the perioperative plan (including pain and symptom management) — reduces anxiety and improves satisfaction.
4 · Intraoperative Management

Surgical approaches

  1. Posterior spinal fusion with contoured or adjustable rods — commonly used in younger children (lengthening over time); fixed rods more common in older children.
  2. Anterior approach via thoracoabdominal incision — removal of intervertebral discs or hemivertebrae contributing to the curvature.
  3. Combined posterior fusion + anterior release (endoscopic anterior release in rare selected cases).

Monitoring

  • Standard: ECG, NIBP, pulse oximetry, temperature, capnography, Foley catheter for urine output.
  • Arterial line: for blood pressure monitoring, cardiac output monitoring (e.g. LiDCO) and intraoperative blood tests — consider if significant comorbidity or higher-than-normal blood loss expected.
  • Central venous pressure: to guide fluid resuscitation, or when peripheral IV access is difficult (syndromic patients, moderate–severe pulmonary or cardiac dysfunction). Not usually required for idiopathic scoliosis.
  • BIS (processed EEG): reassuring when using TIVA rather than volatile anaesthesia.
Right heart strain (e.g. muscular dystrophy-related cardiac failure): avoid factors that increase pulmonary vascular resistance — acidosis, hypoxaemia, hypercarbia, hypothermia.

Positioning (prone)

  • Challenging: abnormal body habitus and need to expose a large area of the spine.
  • Secure and monitor the endotracheal tube and IV lines — they can move during the case.
  • Pad all pressure points.
  • Support chest and pelvis so the abdomen is free to move — raised abdominal pressure compromises IVC venous return, raises epidural vein pressure and increases operative blood loss.
  • Avoid any pressure on the eyes — critical.
  • Specialised tables (e.g. Allen table) can help.

Haemorrhage & blood conservation

  • Large incision, vertebral bone removal at multiple levels, many hours; prone positioning engorges vertebral veins.
  • Loss of up to one half of a patient's blood volume or more is not unexpected — plan for large-volume haemorrhage throughout.
  • Preoperative iron supplementation or erythropoietin if anaemic; autologous blood donation where available.
  • Acute normovolaemic haemodilution (adults, occasionally): remove 2–3 units at start, replace with equal volume colloid or 3× volume crystalloid; transfuse back at the end.
  • Cell salvage (e.g. Cell Saver) may be considered.
  • Tranexamic acid (adolescent AIS): 20–30 mg/kg loading then 10 mg/kg/h infusion — reduces blood loss; most dose-finding studies suggest a higher dose. No clear evidence for the single best bolus/infusion regimen.
  • Intraoperative cell salvage is not usually necessary for AIS posterior spinal fusion (reported loss 275–907 mL).
Induced hypotension (targeting systolic ~80 mmHg) is used in some centres to reduce bleeding, but is associated with postoperative visual loss and anterior spinal ischaemia → postoperative paralysis. Avoid in any patient at high risk from reduced perfusion to vital organs (kidneys, eyes, brain, heart).

Goal-directed fluid therapy using stroke volume variation has been applied in major adult spine surgery in the prone position with positive results. Both hypovolaemia (→ AKI, end-organ dysfunction) and fluid overload (→ pulmonary oedema, ileus, reduced tissue oxygenation) are harmful.

Respiratory management

  • Baseline respiratory status is unlikely to improve during or immediately after correction — ventilation may be challenging.
  • Anticipate significant postoperative atelectasis; severe cases may need controlled ventilation postoperatively.
  • Neuromuscular causes contribute to greater respiratory dysfunction (respiratory muscle involvement).
  • For intrathoracic approaches: lung isolation with a double-lumen tube, bronchial blocker, or endobronchial intubation may be required.

Temperature & infection prevention

  • Large surface area exposed for prolonged periods → careful temperature monitoring to avoid hypothermia.
  • Use warmed IV fluids, forced-air warmers, elevated room temperature.
  • Active prewarming with a forced-air blanket before transfer to theatre reduces the duration of intraoperative hypothermia.
  • Maintain temperature 36–38 °C from incision until skin closure to reduce SSI.
  • Cefazolin 40 mg/kg (max 2 g) IV 10–60 min before incision; repeat dose at 4 h until skin closure.
  • Aseptic technique for all cannulae, including arterial and central venous catheters.
  • SSI incidence after AIS surgery is up to 6.7%.
5 · Intraoperative Neuromonitoring

Goal: identify disruption in nerve signals quickly so surgical adjustments can be made before the final spinal column configuration is set.

Modalities

  • SSEPs (somatosensory evoked potentials): stimulate a peripheral nerve → measure signal generated in the somatosensory cortex.
  • MEPs (motor evoked potentials): stimulate near the motor cortex → measure signals at the target muscle.
  • Together they assess the entire pathway (cortex, spinal cord, peripheral nerves) continuously.
  • Wake-up test: historical/basic test — patient awakened after rod placement to assess lower limb nerve function; requires ability to follow simple motor commands. May need titration of naloxone if excessive opioid; rapid-acting opioids (remifentanil) or dexmedetomidine may help. Used where neuromonitoring is unavailable.

Anaesthetic effects

  • Volatile anaesthetics disrupt SSEPs and MEPs — particularly above 0.5 MAC; nitrous oxide also disrupts them.
  • Neuromuscular blockers disrupt MEP signals (measured at the neuromuscular junction) → avoid non-depolarising NMBAs with MEP monitoring. Small doses (e.g. rocuronium 0.2–0.4 mg/kg) may be used for intubation but should be metabolised before surgery starts.
  • Because of signal interference, these cases are most commonly done with TIVA (propofol ± remifentanil ± ketamine).
  • Use a processed EEG (BIS) during TIVA to assess depth and avoid awareness.
  • Other factors that alter signal transduction: hypoxia, hypercarbia, hypothermia, hypotension — avoid all; maintain a constant depth of anaesthesia.
  • If the signal is disrupted, notify the surgeon immediately.

6 · Postoperative Care (ICU / HDU)

Respiratory

  • Postoperative pulmonary complications are more likely with more severe disease, congenital scoliosis and syndromic children, greater baseline curvature, and pre-existing pulmonary dysfunction.
  • Correction may prevent further decline but is not likely to improve baseline function, particularly in the immediate postoperative period.
  • Preoperative VC or FEV₁ < 40% of predicted → may require postoperative controlled ventilation.
  • Preoperative VC or FEV₁ ≥ 70% → usually has adequate pulmonary reserve for immediate extubation.
  • Optimise respiratory status postoperatively: atelectasis, hypotension, secretion retention, immobilisation and analgesics all worsen underlying pulmonary disease.
  • Manage in a high dependency or specialised postoperative area familiar with spinal corrective surgery.

Pain management

  • Challenging — large skin incisions and multiple osteotomies. Good control improves satisfaction and prevents hypoventilation/respiratory complications.
  • Multimodal: paracetamol (acetaminophen), NSAIDs, gabapentin, ketamine, opioids.
  • Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) improves patient satisfaction.
  • Intrathecal morphine (2–19 µg/kg; duration ~16 h) + PCA provided the most effective pain relief in a retrospective comparison vs PCA alone vs PCEA. Loss of effect on the morning of POD 1 often coincides with the start of physiotherapy.
  • Epidural placement may be considered, but weigh against hypotension and impaired postoperative neurological examination; intrathecal opioids carry a risk of pruritus.
  • Persistent chronic pain with a neuropathic component is common after AIS surgery and is associated with preoperative pain and high early postoperative opioid consumption.

Postoperative nausea & vomiting (PONV)

  • Often a major challenge; multiple prophylactic antiemetics may be required.
  • Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg (max 8 mg) + ondansetron 0.1 mg/kg (max 8 mg) intraoperatively.
  • Aprepitant (NK₁ antagonist, half-life 9–13 h): 40 mg PO 30–60 min before surgery; 40 and 80 mg appear to give similar antiemetic effects to ondansetron. Triple therapy (aprepitant + dexamethasone + ondansetron) was more effective than dexamethasone + ondansetron.
  • Rescue: fluid bolus 10–20 mL/kg, reassess opioid contribution, dimenhydrinate 0.5–1 mg/kg IV q6h PRN.

Postoperative visual loss (POVL)

Rare but devastating. More common in prolonged prone or steep Trendelenburg positioning. Two forms:
  • Ischaemic optic neuropathy (ION) — anterior or posterior (posterior occurs 3× as frequently). Associated with blood loss > 1000 mL or > 45% of estimated blood volume, operations > 6 h, preoperative anaemia, intraoperative hypotension, intraoperative haematocrit < 30; periorbital oedema from high-volume crystalloid resuscitation may also contribute.
  • Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) — from direct compression of the eye causing retinal ischaemia. Any direct pressure on the eye must be avoided.

Other postoperative issues

  • Ileus/constipation — opioid-induced; bowel routine helps (see ERAS pathway).
  • Pruritus — from intrathecal opioids; nalbuphine 0.05–0.1 mg/kg q6h PRN controls it without reversing analgesia.
  • Neurological complications — uncommon but significant; incidence 0.3% after posterior spinal fusion.

8 · Self-Test: True / False Answers

1 · Pulmonary function and scoliosis

  • 1a — False. Restrictive disease is diagnosed with ↓ FVC and ↓ FEV₁; TLC is also reduced (not elevated).
  • 1b — False. A preoperative FEV₁ < 40% indicates severe pulmonary disease and may require postoperative ventilation.
  • 1c — False. Atelectasis, pain, opioids and sedation contribute to diminished postoperative pulmonary status.
  • 1d — True. Postoperative pulmonary complications are greater in non-idiopathic than idiopathic scoliosis.
  • 1e — False. TLC is reduced, but residual volume is generally maintained.

2 · Complications of surgical correction

  • 2a — True. Cardiac complications are more common with certain non-idiopathic forms.
  • 2b — False. Large incisions and multiple osteotomies → significant postoperative pain.
  • 2c — True. Correction is generally indicated with a Cobb angle > 40–45°.
  • 2d — False. Large exposed surface area → temperature must be monitored closely for hypothermia.
  • 2e — True. Central retinal artery occlusion can occur with prolonged direct compression of the eye.

3 · Intraoperative neuromonitoring

  • 3a — False. Volatile anaesthetics disrupt both SSEP and MEP, especially above 0.5 MAC — use opioid infusion or TIVA.
  • 3b — True. SSEPs measure signal transduction from a peripheral nerve stimulus to the somatosensory cortex.
  • 3c — True. Hypotension can change SSEP and MEP signals.
  • 3d — False. Sevoflurane and other volatiles have a greater effect on SSEP signals than propofol.
  • 3e — False. MEP monitoring relies on signals conducted at the neuromuscular junction and is unreliable with neuromuscular blockade.
9 · Anaesthesia for Non-Scoliosis Procedures in Patients with Scoliosis
  • Positioning: careful attention to pressure points to avoid nerve injury and skin ulceration. Extra padding/support may be needed — curvature may prevent visual positioning on a flat table. Specialised tables (e.g. Allen) with chest pad, hip pad, thigh pad, head support and leg supports are often used.
  • Neuraxial anaesthesia in lumbar/thoracic scoliosis can be particularly challenging:
    • Choose an area of minimal abnormal curvature that still provides adequate coverage.
    • Warn the patient of possible multiple attempts and increased risk of accidental dural puncture.
    • Delineate the curvature by tracing the spinous processes; the intervertebral space is often larger more laterally, in the direction of the curvature away from midline (toward the convexity).
    • Ultrasound may help; alternatively the surgeon can inject intraoperatively.
    • Spread of medication may be unpredictable — often patchy or one-sided.
  • Postoperatively, epidural anaesthesia may confuse neurological assessment — now rarely used for this reason.
