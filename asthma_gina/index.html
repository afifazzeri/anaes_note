1 Perioperative essentials ▶

Before surgery

  • Elective surgery should, if possible, be performed when asthma is well controlled. Assess symptom control, recent exacerbations, reliever use, inhaler technique and adherence.
  • Confirm the diagnosis is documented (see Diagnosis & PFT) — 25–35% of primary-care "asthma" diagnoses are not confirmed objectively.
  • Identify triggers that could be encountered perioperatively: NSAIDs/aspirin (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease), beta-blockers, allergens, tobacco/vaping, air pollution, stress.
  • Screen for asthma + COPD (smokers, elderly, biomass exposure). These patients have worse outcomes than asthma or COPD alone and must receive ICS-containing treatment — bronchodilator-only therapy increases hospitalisation and death.
  • Screen for comorbidities that worsen control: rhinitis/rhinosinusitis, obesity, obstructive sleep apnoea, GERD, depression/anxiety.
  • Ensure the patient has a written asthma action plan and knows their usual maintenance and reliever regimen.

During the perioperative period

  • Continue ICS-containing treatment throughout the peri-operative period — do not stop inhaled steroids.
  • Adrenal suppression cover: give hydrocortisone during the operation for patients on long-term high-dose ICS, and for patients who have used oral corticosteroids for more than 2 weeks in the previous 6 months — to reduce the risk of adrenal crisis.
  • Avoid NSAIDs/aspirin in suspected or confirmed aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD).
  • Avoid beta-blockers where possible if they trigger symptoms.
  • Continue the patient's usual reliever; ICS-formoterol can be used before or during allergen exposure and before exercise to prevent symptoms.
Infection-control / device points Avoid nebulisers in patients with a respiratory infection or high probability of infection — deliver SABA by pMDI + spacer (mouthpiece or tightly fitting face mask). Avoid spirometry in confirmed or suspected COVID-19.

Post-operative

  • Provide or review an interim written asthma action plan and arrange follow-up within 2–7 days (2–5 days in children) if there has been an exacerbation.
  • Check and correct inhaler technique before discharge; confirm adherence to ICS-containing therapy.
  • If a course of OCS was needed, optimise inhaled therapy afterwards to reduce the need for further courses.
  • Check vaccination status for preventable respiratory infections (influenza, pneumococcus, COVID-19, RSV, pertussis) per local age-based advice.
2 Acute exacerbation — severity assessment ▶

Assess severity while starting SABA and oxygen. Check for anaphylaxis. Assess dyspnoea, respiratory rate, accessory muscle use, air entry, pulse rate, oxygen saturation and lung function (e.g. PEF). In children <18 years use a validated score such as PRAM (Paediatric Respiratory Assessment Measure).

Immediate ICU / acute-care transfer Any sign of severe exacerbation → arrange immediate transfer. Transfer to intensive care if drowsy, confused, or silent chest. If the patient has anaphylaxis and asthma, give epinephrine (IM or nasal) first.

Consider alternative diagnoses

  • Heart failure, upper airway dysfunction / inducible laryngeal obstruction, inhaled foreign body, pulmonary embolism.
  • Pneumothorax, pneumonia, acute cardiac failure — do not assume all acute breathlessness is asthma.
3 Acute exacerbation — management (ED / ICU) ▶

Initial treatment

  • SABA: salbutamol 4–6 puffs via pMDI + spacer (or 2.5 mg via nebuliser), repeat every 20 minutes for up to 3 doses. Give 1 puff at a time; shake the inhaler before every puff. If too breathless for a single breath with breath-hold, use tidal breathing.
  • Ipratropium: add 4 puffs by pMDI + spacer for moderate exacerbations or inadequate response to initial SABA; may be repeated every 20–30 min.
  • Oxygen: titrate to maintain saturation 92–95% (adjust targets for altitude).
  • Systemic corticosteroids (except mild exacerbations):
    • Adults: prednisolone 40–50 mg each morning × 5–7 days.
    • Children: prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day up to 40 mg each morning × 3–5 days.
    • If used for <2 weeks, no need to taper before stopping.
  • ICS-formoterol alternative: high-dose budesonide–formoterol was as effective and safe as high-dose salbutamol in ED studies of patients with FEV₁ >30% predicted.
Do NOT do routinely Do not use sedatives. Do not routinely perform chest X-ray or blood gases. Do not routinely prescribe antibiotics.

Monitoring

  • Check symptoms and oxygen saturation frequently; titrate treatment according to response.
  • Measure lung function after 1 hour.
  • Transfer to higher-level care if the condition worsens or symptoms fail to respond.

Before discharge / stepping down from acute care

  • Prescribe regular maintenance ICS-containing treatment.
    • Adults & adolescents: preferred is Step 4 ICS-formoterol MART if available; otherwise regular daily medium-dose ICS-LABA (Track 2, Step 4).
    • Children 6–11 y: medium-dose ICS-LABA + ICS-SABA or SABA as needed, or ICS-formoterol MART.
  • Reduce reliever use to as-needed only — tell the patient/parent to use it only for symptoms (not regularly), so worsening or improvement can be detected. Regular SABA use can worsen asthma.
  • Check inhaler technique and adherence.
  • Provide an interim written asthma action plan.
  • Arrange follow-up within 2–7 days (2–5 days for children).
  • Consider early specialist referral after hospitalisation, or for repeated emergency visits.

Follow-up (all patients until symptoms and lung function normal)

  • Confirm ICS-containing treatment is being taken, and reliever is used as needed rather than regularly.
  • Reassess long-term treatment — for adults/adolescents, preferably switch to GINA Track 1 (ICS-formoterol reliever) to reduce the risk of another exacerbation.
  • Check adherence, inhaler technique, and provide/review a written action plan.
  • Ask what the patient thinks caused the exacerbation; check risk factors (smoking, allergen exposure, SABA over-use) and vaccination status.
  • Refer for expert advice if >1 exacerbation in the previous year despite medium- or high-dose ICS-LABA.

Oral corticosteroid use in the action plan

A short course of OCS may be considered when:

  • Symptoms are getting worse over 2–3 days despite using more reliever.
  • Symptoms are rapidly getting worse.
  • Lung function is low (FEV₁ <60% predicted, PEF <60% personal best).
  • Symptoms are worsening in a person with a history of sudden severe exacerbations.

Doses: adults prednisolone 40–50 mg each morning × 5–7 days; children prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day up to 40 mg each morning × 3–5 days. Warn about sleep disturbance, increased appetite, reflux and mood changes. If a prescription is given for home use, the patient should contact their doctor before starting it if possible.

4 Diagnosis & lung function testing ▶

Before ICS treatment is started, asthma has two defining features:

  1. A history of typical respiratory symptoms that are variable (wheeze, difficult breathing, chest tightness, cough), AND
  2. Variable expiratory airflow — though in long-standing asthma, airflow limitation may become permanent.

Physical examination is often normal; wheezing may be heard, especially during forced expiration. Confirm and document the diagnosis in the medical record.

Type 2 biomarkers (supportive, not rule-out)

  • FeNO: adults/adolescents >50 ppb; children >35 ppb.
  • Blood eosinophils above the national/regional reference range.
  • Lower levels do not rule out asthma. Levels vary by sex, age, smoking, time of day and device.

Confirming a previous diagnosis

  • If the criteria have not been recorded, confirm by objective tests. In primary care, 25–35% of patients labelled with asthma may not have it.
  • If lung function is normal, repeat the bronchodilator response test when symptomatic or after withholding bronchodilators (see hold times above), if safe.
  • If already on ICS: with frequent symptoms and FEV₁ <70% predicted → step treatment up, then re-measure in 1–2 months; improvement confirms asthma. With few symptoms and FEV₁ >70% → consider stepping down ICS-containing treatment and re-measure in 2–4 weeks, with an action plan and close monitoring.

Diagnosis in specific contexts

  • Cough-variant asthma: cough may be the only symptom, and bronchodilator responsiveness may be negative. Treat with ICS-containing medication. Consider other causes of persistent dry cough (upper airway cough syndrome, chronic sinusitis, GERD, ACE inhibitors, inducible laryngeal obstruction, eosinophilic bronchitis).
  • Occupational / work-aggravated asthma: ask about workplace allergens/irritants and improvement away from work. Confirm objectively, refer without delay, and stop exposure immediately.
  • Pregnancy: start or continue ICS-containing treatment; wait until after delivery to confirm the diagnosis. Do not stop or reduce ICS, and do not perform bronchial challenge testing.
  • Older adults: under-diagnosed due to poor perception of airflow limitation, or breathlessness attributed to age, heart failure or ischaemic heart disease.
  • Persistent airflow limitation: distinguish asthma with persistent airflow limitation from COPD using history, symptom pattern and past records; refer if uncertain or if features of both are present.
  • LMIC settings: PEF meters can be used if spirometry is unavailable; consider endemic respiratory disease (TB, HIV/AIDS-associated lung disease, parasitic/fungal disease).

B. Risk factors for exacerbations

  • Uncontrolled symptoms (important risk factor).
  • Over-use of SABA (≥3 × 200-dose canisters/year).
  • Inadequate ICS: none, poor adherence, incorrect inhaler technique.
  • Medical conditions: obesity, chronic rhinosinusitis, GERD, confirmed food allergy; pregnancy.
  • Exposure to allergens (if sensitised), smoking, vaping, air pollution.
  • Major psychological or socioeconomic problems.
  • Poor lung function (especially FEV₁ <60% predicted); large FEV₁ response to bronchodilator.
  • Type 2 markers: higher blood eosinophils, high FeNO.
  • Severe exacerbation in the past year, or ever intubated / ICU admission for asthma.

Risk factors for persistent airflow limitation

  • History of preterm birth, low birth weight, rapid infant weight gain, frequent productive cough.
  • Lack of ICS treatment with a history of severe exacerbations.
  • Exposure to tobacco smoke or noxious chemicals.
  • Low FEV₁ at diagnosis.
  • High eosinophil levels in sputum or blood.

Risk factors for medication side-effects

  • Systemic: frequent OCS, long-term high-dose and/or potent ICS, P450 inhibitors (ritonavir, ketoconazole, itraconazole, erythromycin, clarithromycin).
  • Local: high-dose and/or potent ICS, poor inhaler technique.

Review intervals

  • 1–3 months after starting treatment, then every 3–12 months.
  • Every 4–6 weeks during pregnancy.
  • Within 1 week after an exacerbation.
  • More frequently if risk factors for a fatal exacerbation: previous near-fatal asthma, very severe exacerbation in the last year, current/recent OCS, SABA over-use (especially >1 canister/month), relevant comorbidities.
  • Measure lung function (spirometry, or PEF if unavailable) before starting ICS, 3–6 months later, then at least every 1–2 years.

Investigating poor control despite ICS-containing treatment

Always check inhaler technique and adherence first, then work through:

  1. Watch the patient use their inhaler — compare with a device-specific checklist, correct errors, recheck up to 2–3 times. Discuss adherence barriers empathetically.
  2. Confirm the diagnosis. If lung function is normal during symptoms, consider halving the ICS dose and repeating lung function after 2–3 weeks; consider Type 2 biomarkers.
  3. Remove potential risk factors and manage comorbidities — for adults/adolescents, switch to GINA Track 1 if available; check smoking, beta-blockers, NSAIDs, allergen exposure, rhinitis, obesity, GERD, depression/anxiety.
  4. Consider a short-term step-up using shared decision-making, balancing benefits and risks.
  5. Refer for expert advice if still uncontrolled after 3–6 months on Step 4 — refer earlier if symptoms are severe or the diagnosis is in doubt.

Definitions of severity

  • Mild: well controlled with as-needed ICS-formoterol, or with low-dose daily ICS.
  • Difficult-to-treat: uncontrolled despite medium- or high-dose ICS-LABA or maintenance OCS, or requiring high-dose treatment to maintain control. Often due to treatable factors (technique, adherence, smoking, comorbidity) or a wrong diagnosis — it does not mean a "difficult patient".
  • Severe: still uncontrolled despite good adherence to optimised high-dose ICS-LABA and management of contributory factors, or needing high-dose ICS-LABA to maintain control.
Patient communication point Many patients with infrequent symptoms believe their asthma is "mild" and that ICS is unnecessary. Explain that even infrequent symptoms can lead to severe or fatal exacerbations if treated with SABA alone, and that ICS-containing treatment reduces the risk of severe exacerbations by half to two-thirds compared with SABA alone.

Adjusting treatment

  • Reassess at every visit; step up or down within the same track using the same reliever.
  • Patients can switch between tracks if needed.
  • Before stepping up, check adherence, inhaler technique, risk factors and comorbidities.
  • If well controlled for 2–3 months or more, consider stepping down.

Stepping down

  • Choose a time with no respiratory infection; patient not travelling, not pregnant.
  • Record symptom control and lung function, provide a written action plan, monitor closely, and plan follow-up.
  • Reduce the maintenance ICS dose by 25–50% (lower-strength inhaler or fewer inhalations).
  • Review after each step-down and wait 2–3 months before stepping down again.
  • Do not completely stop ICS in adults or adolescents with asthma.

Other options for some adults and adolescents

  • House dust mite sublingual immunotherapy: consider if house dust mite allergy and asthma not controlled by ICS-containing treatment. Do not use if FEV₁ ≤70% predicted.
  • Leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast, pranlukast, zafirlukast, zileuton): less effective than daily ICS, particularly for preventing exacerbations. Montelukast has been associated with serious mental health effects.

Notes on the child pathway

  • In Steps 2–5 the child takes maintenance ICS-containing treatment every day; even low-dose ICS reduces severe exacerbations, improves lung function and reduces symptoms compared with no ICS.
  • If stepping up from as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol, the appropriate next step is low-dose MART.
  • In Steps 3–4, options are: medium-dose daily ICS; low-dose daily ICS-LABA; or low-dose ICS-formoterol MART. ICS-formoterol is the only ICS-LABA that can be used for MART.
  • If Step 4 is needed, consider referral to a paediatric asthma expert.
  • At any step the child may take the reliever before exercise if needed; ICS-formoterol can also be taken before or during allergen exposure.
  • Step-down can be considered after good control for 3 months or more (2–3 months is stated elsewhere in the guide).

Other options / step 5 for children

  • Tiotropium (separate mist inhaler) can be added at Step 4.
  • Leukotriene antagonist may be used instead of daily ICS at Step 2 (less effective for reducing exacerbations), or added at Steps 3–5. Montelukast has been associated with serious mental health effects in some children.
  • Ragweed sublingual immunotherapy may be considered before and during ragweed season. Do not use if FEV₁ <80% predicted.
  • Biologics (6–11 years): anti-IgE (omalizumab), anti-IL5 (mepolizumab), anti-IL4Rα (dupilumab).

ICS-LABA Beclometasone-formoterol, budesonide-formoterol, fluticasone furoate-vilanterol, fluticasone propionate-formoterol/salmeterol, mometasone-formoterol, mometasone-indacaterol Adding LABA when low-dose ICS fails improves symptoms, lung function and exacerbations — better than doubling ICS. LABA side-effects: tachycardia, headache, muscle cramps. LABA must never be used without ICS in asthma.
SABA Salbutamol (albuterol), terbutaline Quick relief of symptoms/bronchoconstriction and pre-exercise pretreatment. Use only when needed, not regularly. Use without ICS is not recommended. Fenoterol is not recommended (cardiovascular adverse effects, asthma mortality). Short-term: tremor, tachycardia. Overuse indicates poor control.
Short-acting antimuscarinic Ipratropium bromide, oxitropium bromide (may be combined with SABA) Short-term use in acute severe exacerbations — ipratropium + SABA reduces hospital admission vs SABA alone. As a reliever it is less effective than SABA with slower onset. Dry mouth, bitter taste; caution in narrow-angle glaucoma.
LAMA (add-on) Tiotropium (≥6 y, mist inhaler); ICS-LABA-LAMA combinations (≥12 y or ≥18 y depending on product) Add if uncontrolled despite medium-dose ICS-LABA. Small improvement in lung function and small reduction in exacerbations (no clinically important symptom benefit). Increase ICS to at least medium dose before adding a LAMA. Uncommon: dry mouth, urinary retention.
LTRA Montelukast, pranlukast, zafirlukast, zileuton Less effective than low-dose ICS; ICS + LTRA less effective than ICS-LABA. Zileuton/zafirlukast: elevated liver enzymes. Montelukast: risk of serious behavioural and mood changes including suicidal ideation — discuss with patient/carers.
Systemic corticosteroids Prednisone, prednisolone, methylprednisolone, hydrocortisone, dexamethasone Short course for severe acute exacerbations: oral 5–7 days adults, 3–5 days children. If used >2 weeks, reduce gradually. Short-course adverse effects: sepsis, thromboembolism, sleep disturbance, reflux, increased appetite, hyperglycaemia, mood changes; cumulative courses increase later risk of diabetes, osteoporosis, cataract, glaucoma, heart failure. Maintenance use: last resort only.

9 Step 5 — severe asthma & biologics ▶

If uncontrolled symptoms and/or exacerbations persist despite Step 4 (Track 1 or 2): assess causes, optimise treatment including inhaler technique and adherence, and refer for expert assessment — including inflammatory phenotype, comorbidities, risk factors and potential add-on treatment. Sputum eosinophil count, where available, may guide treatment in moderate–severe asthma.

High-dose ICS-LABA May occasionally be needed but should be limited to 3–6 months where possible, because of increased risk of side-effects including adrenal suppression.

Biologic safety notes

  • Common: headache, minor injection-site reactions.
  • Rare: anaphylaxis (omalizumab, tezepelumab).
  • Dupilumab: uncommon transient blood eosinophilia (4–13%); rare eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis after reducing/stopping oral corticosteroids. Not advised if current or past blood eosinophils ≥1500/microlitre.
  • Benralizumab and mepolizumab can significantly reduce maintenance OCS dose.
  • Always check local eligibility criteria and product information.
Remission on biologics Some patients with severe asthma achieve clinical remission on biologic therapy. Predictors: fewer symptoms, better lung function, little multimorbidity, earlier asthma onset, and no or lower baseline maintenance OCS use.
10 Special populations & comorbidities ▶

Pregnancy

  • Ask all pregnant women and those planning pregnancy about asthma. Monitor asthma every 4–6 weeks — control often changes during pregnancy.
  • All pregnant women with asthma should receive ICS-containing treatment: exacerbations increase the risk of preterm delivery, low birth weight and perinatal mortality, and ICS reduces these risks.
  • Do not stop or reduce ICS-containing treatment. ICS and beta-agonists are not associated with increased risk of fetal abnormalities.
  • Do not perform bronchial challenge testing during pregnancy.
  • Treat exacerbations as for non-pregnant adults, to avoid fetal hypoxia.

Anaphylaxis & food allergy

  • Food allergy may cause anaphylaxis but is rarely a trigger for asthma symptoms. Confirmed food allergy is a risk factor for asthma-related death — good asthma control is essential.
  • Ensure the patient (and carers) has an anaphylaxis plan, knows how to avoid the allergen, and knows how to use injectable or nasal epinephrine.
  • If a patient has anaphylaxis and asthma, give epinephrine (IM or nasal) first.

Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD)

  • A history of asthma exacerbation after aspirin or other NSAIDs strongly suggests AERD. Often associated with severe asthma and nasal polyposis.
  • Advise avoidance of all NSAIDs. Challenge testing only in a specialised centre with resuscitation facilities.
  • Adding a leukotriene receptor antagonist to ICS-containing treatment may improve lung function and reduce symptoms (note montelukast neuropsychiatric concerns).
  • If regular aspirin therapy is indicated, desensitisation is sometimes effective but must only be done under specialist care — significantly increased risk of exacerbation, gastritis and GI bleeding.

Allergic rhinitis & chronic rhinosinusitis

  • Allergic rhinitis is very common in asthma — treat with intranasal corticosteroids as well as asthma treatment.
  • Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps is associated with more severe asthma. Treating it reduces nasal symptoms but may not improve asthma control.
  • Biologics (anti-IgE, anti-IL-4Rα, anti-IL-5, anti-IL-5Rα) improve nasal symptoms in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps and asthma.

Obesity

Asthma is more difficult to control in obesity. Include weight reduction in the treatment plan — even 5–10% weight loss can improve asthma control.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)

Asymptomatic GERD is unlikely to cause poor asthma control. Treat symptomatic reflux, but do not treat poorly controlled asthma with anti-reflux therapy unless there is symptomatic reflux.

Older adults

  • Comorbidities and their treatment may complicate management — review all medicines and check for side-effects.
  • When choosing drugs and devices, consider arthritis, eyesight, inspiratory flow, regimen complexity, and inhaler environmental impact.

Occupational asthma

If asthma was caused by airborne irritants or allergens at work, start ICS treatment immediately, refer as soon as possible for expert care, and eliminate exposure to the cause.

Respiratory viruses / COVID-19

  • People with well-controlled asthma do not have a higher risk of severe COVID-19 than the general population. However, those with recent OCS use for asthma or hospitalisation with severe asthma have a higher risk of COVID-19-related death.
  • Advise patients to continue their asthma medications, including ICS, during a respiratory infection.
  • Avoid nebulisers in patients with a respiratory infection or high probability of infection — use pMDI + spacer with mouthpiece or tightly fitting face mask.
  • Avoid spirometry in confirmed or suspected COVID-19. Check for interactions between antivirals and asthma therapy before prescribing.

Exercise & non-pharmacological strategies

  • Encourage regular physical activity — general health benefits, and may slightly improve asthma control and lung function. Advise on managing exercise-induced bronchoconstriction.
  • Do not avoid exercise or laughter. Viral infections and stress are difficult to avoid and must be managed when they occur.
  • Smoking cessation: strongly encourage at every visit; provide counselling and resources; advise parents/carers not to smoke or vape in rooms or cars used by children with asthma.
  • Allergen avoidance is not recommended generally — strategies are complex, expensive, and there are no validated methods to identify those likely to benefit.
  • Guided self-management (symptom and/or PEF monitoring + written action plan + regular review) reduces exacerbation risk.
11 Non-recommended treatments & key cautions ▶
GINA does NOT recommend
  • Oral salbutamol
  • Oral theophylline
  • Oral epinephrine
  • Inhaled fenoterol (increased cardiovascular adverse effects and asthma mortality)
  • Regular (daily) use of SABA
  • SABA-only treatment of asthma — at any age

Why SABA-only treatment is unsafe

  • Most patients with asthma have airway inflammation even with only intermittent or infrequent symptoms.
  • SABA-only treatment is associated with increased risk of exacerbations, worse lung function, and increased risk of asthma death.
  • Regular SABA use increases allergic responses and airway inflammation, and reduces the bronchodilator response to SABA.
  • Over-use (≥3 × 200-dose canisters dispensed in a year) increases severe exacerbation risk vs 0–2 canisters.
  • Dispensing ≥12 canisters in a year (possibly fewer) is associated with increased risk of death due to asthma.
  • Home use of nebulised SABA is also associated with increased risk of asthma death.

Why every patient needs ICS

  • Any patient can have a severe exacerbation, even if asthma seems mild.
  • ICS-containing medication markedly reduces risk of hospitalisation and death, and is very effective at preventing severe exacerbations, reducing symptoms, improving lung function and preventing exercise-induced bronchoconstriction.
  • Early treatment with low-dose ICS is associated with better lung function than starting after symptoms have been present for more than 2–4 years.
  • Patients who have a severe exacerbation while not taking ICS have worse long-term lung function than those who have started ICS.

Maintenance oral corticosteroids

Last resort only, at the lowest possible dose, because short- and long-term systemic side-effects are common and serious. Monitor all patients for adrenal suppression and osteoporosis.

Inhaler technique

  • Incorrect use is very common and contributes to poor control, exacerbations and local adverse effects.
  • Choose the device before prescribing — consider arthritis, skills, cost, and (if options are equivalent) environmental impact.
  • Use a spacer with ICS delivered by pMDI.
  • Train with a device-specific checklist; check technique at every opportunity; correct errors by physical demonstration and recheck up to 2–3 times.
  • If FeNO is high, check and correct adherence and inhaler technique before considering a step-up.

Adherence

  • Poor adherence to ICS-containing treatment causes poor symptom control and increases exacerbation risk.
  • Ask empathically, e.g. "Most patients don't take their inhaler exactly as prescribed. In the last 4 weeks, how many days a week have you been taking it?"
  • Check prescription dates, inhaler dose counters and dispensing records.
  • Explore attitudes and beliefs about asthma and medications.
  • Strategies with evidence: shared decision-making on medication choice, comprehensive asthma education with home visits by asthma nurses, directly observed school administration in children, electronic reminders, and prescriber feedback on dispensing records.

Written asthma action plan — must include

  • The patient's usual asthma medications.
  • When to increase or add medications and how to do this.
  • How to get urgent medical care if symptoms continue or worsen.
  • Can be handwritten, printed, digital or pictorial — not just spoken instructions — and must suit the person's health literacy.
  • Can be based on symptoms and/or (in adults) PEF.
