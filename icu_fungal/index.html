Fungal Infections in the Critically Ill Adult

Anaesthetist's / Intensivist's quick reference — Candida · Aspergillus · Mucorales · Cryptococcus · PCP

1 · Key points at a glance
  • The incidence of fungal disease in critically ill patients is thought to be increasing, most commonly involving Candida and Aspergillus species.
  • Risk factors for invasive fungal infection: immunocompromised states including neutropenia; intravascular or other catheters (especially with parenteral nutrition); prostheses; loss of anatomical barriers (e.g. burns); broad-spectrum antimicrobial usage.
  • Delay in starting antifungal therapy is associated with worse outcomes. Because confirming fungal infection is difficult, empirical therapy is often commenced in high-risk patients.
  • Geographical variation in the prevalence of fungal species affects the choice of antifungal therapy.
  • Symptoms are usually non-specific (fever, tachycardia) or reflect end-organ damage (hypoxia, confusion). A high index of suspicion is essential.
  • Fungi are eukaryotes with a cell wall (chitin, chitosan, mannan, glucan) and a membrane containing ergosterol instead of cholesterol — this difference is the basis of most antifungal drugs.

One-line take-home: in the ICU, think fungus when a patient is not improving on broad-spectrum antibacterials and has the right risk profile — treat early, and always ask "is this colonisation or invasion?"

2 · Structure & classification of fungi

Although there are many different species of fungi, relatively few are responsible for human illness. Fungal infections associated with critical illness are thought to be increasing in incidence, possibly as a result of the increasing population of immunocompromised individuals, more aggressive medical interventions and procedures, and increased use of anti-bacterial therapies.

Fungi are eukaryotes (membranes cover the nucleus and other intracellular organelles); this makes them structurally similar to animals and plants, but different from prokaryotes such as bacteria. Fungi have rigid cell walls containing chitin, chitosan, mannan and glucan. Their cell membranes are structurally different from those of animals because they contain ergosterol rather than cholesterol.

Moulds vs yeasts

  • Yeasts (e.g. Candida) — small rounded cells that can bud.
  • Moulds (e.g. Aspergillus) — stranded, filamentous appearance caused by hyphae.
  • Dimorphic fungi can exist in both forms (e.g. Blastomyces).
  • Some yeasts develop pseudo-hyphae (e.g. Candida species).
  • A matted, intermeshed network of hyphae is a mycelium.

Growth & reproduction

  • Fungi are slow-growing; cell-doubling times can be as long as days — this limits the ability to identify clinically relevant infection quickly.
  • Reproduction may be sexual, asexual, or both, and may produce "daughter cells" or spores.
  • Many fungi and spores are environmentally ubiquitous — Aspergillus is common in soil and its spores are prevalent in the atmosphere.
  • Several fungi are common human flora (e.g. Candida in the gut) or colonise the gut, oropharynx, or upper and lower airways.

Clinical trap: it can be difficult to tell whether a positive fungal culture represents invasive disease or simply capture of normal flora / colonisation. Always interpret in the context of the patient.

4 · Candida — the commonest ICU fungus

Candida species are responsible for the majority of fungal infections in critically ill patients, with Candida albicans being the most common organism in the UK.

Non-albicans species

The genus also contains C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, C. glabrata, C. krusei and C. dubliniensis — collectively the non-albicans species. Their incidence varies geographically and overall appears to be increasing, possibly related to increased use of fluconazole, which may select out resistant non-albicans species such as C. glabrata and C. krusei. Non-albicans infection has been associated with worse outcomes.

Spectrum of disease

  • Superficial candidiasis/candidosis — mostly mucous membranes or skin.
  • Invasive candidiasis — Candida enters the bloodstream (candidaemia) with haematogenous spread to liver, spleen, brain, eyes (endophthalmitis) or heart (endocarditis).
  • Direct spread — peritoneum and thoracic cavity after bowel or oesophageal perforation, or via colonisation of intra-peritoneal dialysis catheters.

Epidemiology & mortality

  • USA data: Candida is the 4th most common cause of nosocomial ICU bloodstream infection (5–10% of infections).
  • European studies: between the 6th and 10th most common cause.
  • Mortality associated with candidaemia may be as high as 40%.

Respiratory secretions — usually colonisation

Candida spp. are often isolated from respiratory secretions in critically ill patients, but true infection of the lower respiratory tract is rare. Growth of Candida from respiratory specimens alone should not prompt antifungal therapy in most patients.

Urine — interpret with care

  • May simply represent colonisation of a urinary catheter or lower urinary tract in the absence of symptoms.
  • May cause ascending pyelonephritis, frequently complicated by fungal balls, or spread haematogenously to the urinary tract.
  • Pyuria is of limited value — it may represent bacterial co-infection in as many as 25% of cases.
  • In severely immunocompromised patients, fever + candiduria may be a surrogate marker of disseminated disease and requires treatment. In other groups, removing predisposing factors (e.g. indwelling catheters) may be sufficient.

Devices & line removal

  • Candida commonly colonises urinary catheters and intravascular devices (especially with parenteral nutrition); removal of the indwelling device is often required to eradicate and cure associated infection.
  • In non-neutropaenic patients there is evidence of improved outcomes after early line removal, regardless of the putative source.
  • Where C. parapsilosis is the causative organism, line removal should be undertaken whenever possible — the organism is thought to form resistant biofilms.

Eye & heart

  • The two most commonly cited complications of disseminated candidiasis are infective endocarditis and ocular involvement — hence recommendations that patients with candidaemia undergo echocardiography and dilated retinal examination.
  • A more targeted approach has been suggested: persistently positive blood cultures despite treatment and removal of an infected focus indicates the need to investigate for endovascular involvement.
  • Patients reporting new visual disturbance should be prioritised — but early ophthalmological review is required even without symptoms, as infection may be clinically silent, especially in the critically ill.
5 · Aspergillus — the mould that invades

Aspergillus species are responsible for a range of invasive and saprophytic human diseases, and also common but non-infective conditions such as allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis and allergic sinusitis. The lung is the most common site of true infection.

Spectrum of pulmonary disease

  • Aspergilloma (fungal ball) — regarded as a saprophytic condition.
  • Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA).
  • Chronic necrotizing pulmonary aspergillosis.
  • Chronic cavitary aspergillosis.
  • Tracheobronchial aspergillosis and Aspergillus bronchitis.
  • Extrapulmonary aspergillosis — via haematogenous dissemination or contiguous spread (e.g. sinus disease leading to cerebral involvement).

Species

Only a very few of the several hundred species cause human disease, and of these Aspergillus fumigatus is the most common. Less common potential pathogens include A. niger, A. flavus, A. nidulans, A. oryzae, A. glaucus and A. versicolor. A. terreus is resistant to amphotericin B.

Clinical presentation

  • Typically non-specific: fever, cough, dyspnoea, and occasionally pleuritic pain or haemoptysis.
  • Dissemination from the lungs may cause abscess/aspergilloma formation and angio-invasion.
  • Angio-invasion occasionally results in pseudo-aneurysms that rupture, causing massive haemoptysis where the lungs are affected.

Imaging

  • Chest X-ray changes are typically non-specific: pleural infiltrates, cavitation, rounded densities. Pleural effusions are uncommon.
  • Chest CT may identify features more specific to invasive Aspergillus infection — see the diagnostic techniques section.

Microbiology & histology

  • Direct sampling of bronchial secretions/washings or lung tissue may indicate the presence of Aspergillus, but presence in secretions alone cannot distinguish colonisation from true infection — interpret in the clinical context.
  • Lung histology is rarely available but can confirm the diagnosis by demonstrating septate, branching hyphae invading lung tissue.
  • In neutropaenic patients the diagnostic yield of any culture may be particularly low, and Aspergillus-specific antifungal therapy is often commenced on an empirical basis in patients with risk factors.
6 · Zygomycosis (mucormycosis)

Zygomycetes are a class of fungus that includes several orders and species. The taxonomy contains many synonymous or obsolete terms, but as the order Mucorales are most commonly involved in human infection, the terms "mucormycosis" and "zygomycosis" are often used interchangeably. The literature is confusing to interpret; an up-to-date list of fungal terms is available at www.doctorfungus.org.

Most commonly implicated species: Absidia corymbifera, Rhizopus arrhizus, Rhizomucor pusillus, Mucor indicus.

Key features

  • Rare, and may affect any organ — in particular the sinuses, brain, skin, lungs and gastrointestinal tract.
  • Angio-invasion and disseminated infection with associated necrosis are hallmarks of the disease.
  • Prompt surgical debridement together with antifungal therapy may improve survival, although mortality rates are high.
  • Typically affects immunocompromised hosts, but also has a specific association with diabetic ketoacidosis, where a classic rhinocerebral form can occur that carries a poor prognosis.

Do not miss: DKA + facial/orbital pain, periorbital swelling, black necrotic eschar on the palate or nasal mucosa = rhinocerebral mucormycosis. This is a surgical emergency — call ENT/maxillofacial immediately.

7 · Cryptococcosis

Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii are responsible for most cases of cryptococcosis, which is strongly associated with immunocompromise, and in particular HIV infection. As with tuberculous infections, cryptococci are known to remain dormant within lymph nodes.

Cryptococcosis primarily affects either the lungs or the central nervous system, but can result in disseminated infection affecting almost any organ.

Pulmonary cryptococcosis

  • Infiltrative pneumonia, occasionally with associated pleural effusions or hilar lymphadenopathy.
  • Pulmonary nodules may occur, which can cavitate or result in distal lung collapse.
  • Chest X-ray appearances may be similar to those of lung cancer.

Cryptococcal meningo-encephalitis

  • May develop slowly over weeks.
  • Fever and non-specific neurological symptoms: headache, cranial nerve palsies, memory loss, meningeal irritation, reduced consciousness.
  • Cryptococcomas can develop, as can raised intracranial pressure — especially if hydrocephalus develops as a result of "sludging" within the CSF, cryptococcoma formation, or scarring.
  • Even with optimal treatment, mortality associated with cryptococcal meningoencephalitis is as high as 20%.
8 · Blastomyces, Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Paracoccidioides

Blastomyces dermatitidis, Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis and Paracoccidioides brasiliensis are thermally dimorphic fungi found in soil from the Americas (and in the case of B. dermatitidis also from Africa). These fungi are capable of causing self-limiting pneumonia when inhaled. In immunocompromised individuals, haematogenous spread may affect the liver, bones, joints, brain or skin.

Diagnostic & safety warning: in non-endemic areas, diagnosing these infections is difficult because of the lack of availability of indirect laboratory tests and reliance on slow and relatively insensitive direct culture techniques. Propagating these organisms is hazardous to laboratory personnel — samples should be labelled as high risk with the relevant history attached. Consultation with a medical microbiologist is essential if infection with dimorphic fungi is suspected.

9 · Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP)

There are different species of Pneumocystis, but they appear to be species specific, with Pneumocystis jirovecii affecting the human lungs. P. jirovecii (also known as Pneumocystis carinii) was previously thought to be a protozoa but is genetically classified as a fungus. It demonstrates certain aspects that are different from other fungi, including a lack of ergosterol.

Presentation

  • Gradually progressive dyspnoea.
  • Unproductive cough — although up to 30% of patients may expectorate.
  • Low-grade fever.
  • Associated with HIV seropositive individuals (specifically advanced disease/AIDS), cancer, or the administration of immunosuppressants (including after organ transplantation).

Investigations

  • Chest X-ray: bilateral, proximal interstitial infiltrates; pneumatocoeles and pneumothoraces are common findings.
  • Dissemination beyond the lungs is rare.
  • Person-to-person spread is suspected to occur — isolation of patients is advisable.

Treatment

Most antifungal agents are ineffective against P. jirovecii because it lacks ergosterol. Treatment is co-trimoxazole, pentamidine, atovaquone, primaquine, dapsone or clindamycin. There is some evidence that echinocandins may be active, in particular micafungin. Antimicrobial treatment is normally accompanied by the administration of steroids.

PCP has a high associated mortality, with estimates ranging from 20 to 60%.

10 · Diagnostic techniques (Table 2 + imaging)

Fungi, or fungal invasion of tissues, can sometimes be seen directly under the microscope, and certain fungi can also be cultured using specialist culture plates, which can contain anti-bacterial elements. Where there is skin involvement, microscopy and culture of skin biopsies may help in identifying the organism.

Specialist tests can detect fungal elements including polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection of fungal DNA, and galactomannan, which is present in the cell wall of Aspergillus species and can be detected in blood and broncho-alveolar lavage (BAL) specimens.

Serum markers

  • Serum inflammatory markers are non-specific for fungal infections.
  • Moderate increases in procalcitonin may assist in differentiating between fungal and bacterial infections, but procalcitonin has a low sensitivity for invasive fungal infections.

Imaging

  • Chest X-ray changes in fungal pneumonia are non-specific.
  • In Aspergillus lung disease, the presence of aspergillomas or "air crescent" formations on chest CT can be diagnostic if present.
  • More common but less specific CT findings: nodules surrounded by a diffuse "halo" of ground-glass appearance (the halo sign), or pleural "wedges" of consolidation.
  • Fundoscopy may reveal cotton-wool ball changes within the retina if candidal chorioretinitis is present.
11 · Antifungal agents — mechanisms & formulary (Table 3)

Antifungal agents work by a variety of mechanisms. The three main targets are the cell wall, the cell membrane and the nucleus.

  1. Cell wall — echinocandins inhibit glucan synthesis.
  2. Cell membrane — polyenes (amphotericin B and nystatin) bind to ergosterol; imidazoles, triazoles and terbinafine prevent ergosterol synthesis.
  3. Cell nucleus — flucytosine inhibits DNA and RNA synthesis; griseofulvin inhibits cell mitosis.

Amphotericin B — the broadest, but toxic

  • An IV polyene antifungal with the broadest range of antifungal activity, but usage is limited by side-effects: nephrotoxicity, anaphylactoid reactions, electrolyte disturbance and bone-marrow suppression.
  • Liposomal formulations (AmBisome, Abelcet) have a reduced incidence of nephrotoxicity.
  • Different formulations of amphotericin should not be stored together — their dosing regimens are very different and there is the potential for severe toxicity if conventional amphotericin is dosed in the same manner as liposomal amphotericin.

Echinocandins and azoles

  • Both are well tolerated, but both classes may have a tendency to promote varying degrees of liver dysfunction.
  • Certain azoles prolong the QT interval, particularly when used in combination with other drugs.
  • Azoles also affect cytochrome P450, causing drug interactions — for example with ciclosporin and rifampicin.

13 · Prophylaxis & empirical therapy

Prophylaxis

Antifungal agents may be used as prophylaxis against invasive fungal infections in:

  • Individuals with neutropenia.
  • Patients treated with immunosuppressants after solid organ transplants.
  • Patients with HIV who have low CD4 counts.

Prophylaxis may reduce opportunistic infection with:

  • Pneumocystis — using reduced doses of co-trimoxazole.
  • Candida — using fluconazole, echinocandins, or an amphotericin.
  • Aspergillus — using voriconazole or an amphotericin.

Anti-candidal prophylaxis with fluconazole may be used after upper and lower gastrointestinal perforation, but only limited evidence exists to support this.

Empirical therapy

Empirical treatment is required when high-risk patients develop evidence of severe sepsis or severe respiratory compromise. Broad-spectrum antifungals such as an echinocandin are advised, and amphotericin may be required if a resistant species or pulmonary aspergillosis is possible.

14 · Anaesthetic & ICU practical points

Always check the BNF / local formulary and microbiology advice before prescribing. The points below are the ones that most often change what you do at the bedside.

Before you start

  • Take cultures first — blood (multiple sets), urine, sputum/ETA/BAL, CSF, wound/pus, and remove/change lines where possible. But do not delay therapy in the unstable patient.
  • Ask: colonisation or invasion? A positive culture from a non-sterile site (sputum, urine, wound swab) in a stable patient is often colonisation.
  • Document the risk profile (neutropenia, TPN, steroids, transplant, HIV, DKA, prolonged broad-spectrum antibacterials, burns, prostheses).

Drug-specific issues relevant to the anaesthetist/intensivist

DrugWatch out for
Amphotericin B (conventional)Nephrotoxicity, anaphylactoid reactions, electrolyte disturbance (K⁺, Mg²⁺), bone-marrow suppression. Pre-load with saline; monitor renal function and electrolytes. Never interchange conventional and liposomal dosing.
Liposomal amphotericinLess nephrotoxic than conventional, but far more expensive — and the dose is much higher. Storage mix-ups are a real hazard.
VoriconazoleVisual disturbance and hallucinations (often transient); hepatotoxicity; QT prolongation; major CYP450 interactions — tacrolimus, ciclosporin, rifampicin, warfarin. The IV vehicle accumulates in renal impairment.

EchinocandinsWell tolerated; generally minimal CYP interaction; hepatic dysfunction possible. Useful when the patient is already on multiple CYP-metabolised drugs. No activity against P. jirovecii or Cryptococcus — and not the drug for CNS cryptococcosis.

Don't forget the non-drug bits

  • Remove infected lines and devices — especially in candidaemia and where C. parapsilosis is isolated.
  • Source control: surgical debridement for zygomycosis; drainage of infected collections.
  • Echocardiography + dilated retinal examination in candidaemia — early ophthalmology even if asymptomatic.
  • Isolate suspected PCP — person-to-person spread is suspected.
  • Reduce immunosuppression where feasible (transplant patients, steroids) in discussion with the relevant team.
  • Label dimorphic fungus samples as high risk and speak to a medical microbiologist.
15 · Ten high-yield case examples

Click each case to expand. These are the scenarios that catch people out in ICU and on the wards.

Case 1 — Day 10 after laparotomy for faecal peritonitis: fever, TPN, CVVH, new candidaemia

Presentation A 58-year-old man is on ICU day 10 after laparotomy for faecal peritonitis. He is on parenteral nutrition via a central line, CVVH, and has had 12 days of broad-spectrum antibacterials. He spikes a temperature of 39°C with rising vasopressor requirement. Blood cultures grow Candida spp.

Do Take repeat blood cultures and remove/change the central line early. Start an echinocandin (micafungin, caspofungin or anidulafungin) empirically if he is unstable or a non-albicans species is likely; if he is stable and C. albicans is confirmed, fluconazole is reasonable. Arrange echocardiography and dilated retinal examination. Review TPN and consider stopping it if not essential. Review all other devices (urinary catheter, PD catheter).

Key learning Gut perforation + TPN + broad-spectrum antibacterials + a line is the classic ICU candidaemia triad. Early line removal improves outcomes regardless of the putative source. Mortality may be as high as 40%.

Case 2 — Neutropenic AML patient with pleuritic pain and haemoptysis

Presentation A 34-year-old with AML is day 14 post-chemotherapy, profoundly neutropenic, febrile despite meropenem. He develops pleuritic chest pain and haemoptysis. CT chest shows a nodule with a surrounding ground-glass halo.

Do Send serum galactomannan and 1–3 beta-D-glucan, and take BAL if safe and appropriate. Start voriconazole — the first-line agent for invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. Do not wait for culture; diagnostic yield in neutropenic patients is low.

Key learning The halo sign in a neutropenic febrile patient is invasive aspergillosis until proven otherwise. Delay in therapy worsens outcome — empirical treatment in high-risk patients is standard. Watch for CYP450 interactions with the patient's other drugs.

Case 3 — DKA with periorbital swelling and a black eschar on the palate

Presentation A 46-year-old with poorly controlled type 1 diabetes presents in DKA with facial pain, periorbital swelling, and a black necrotic eschar on the hard palate. He is confused.

Do Call ENT/maxillofacial and ophthalmology immediately — this is rhinocerebral mucormycosis. Start liposomal amphotericin B (± posaconazole). Arrange urgent CT/MRI of sinuses and brain. Surgical debridement is the definitive treatment and improves survival; antifungal therapy alone is not enough.

Key learning Zygomycosis has a specific association with diabetic ketoacidosis. Angio-invasion and necrosis are hallmarks. Mortality is high even with optimal treatment — speed of debridement matters more than the drug choice.

Case 4 — HIV with CD4 40, headache, fever and confusion

Presentation A 39-year-old with HIV (CD4 count 40) presents with two weeks of worsening headache, low-grade fever, cranial nerve palsy and reduced consciousness. CT head is unremarkable; LP shows raised opening pressure.

Do Send CSF cryptococcal antigen and India ink/culture. If positive, start liposomal amphotericin B + flucytosine for induction (typically 2 weeks), followed by fluconazole consolidation. Manage raised intracranial pressure with serial therapeutic LPs and appropriate CSF diversion. Monitor FBC for flucytosine marrow suppression.

Key learning Cryptococcal meningoencephalitis develops over weeks, and mortality is up to 20% even with optimal treatment. Raised ICP from "sludging", cryptococcomas or hydrocephalus is what kills these patients — treat the pressure, not just the fungus.

Case 5 — Renal transplant on tacrolimus with worsening hypoxia and a dry cough

Presentation A 55-year-old renal transplant recipient on tacrolimus and prednisolone develops progressive dyspnoea, a dry cough and low-grade fever over 10 days. SpO₂ is 88% on air. CXR shows bilateral proximal interstitial infiltrates; LDH is high.

Do Suspect Pneumocystis pneumonia. Send sputum/BAL for immunofluorescence and PCR, and serum 1–3 beta-D-glucan. Start co-trimoxazole (high dose, adjusted for renal function) and add steroids if hypoxic. Do not reach for an azole — P. jirovecii lacks ergosterol, so most antifungals are ineffective. Reduce immunosuppression in discussion with the transplant team, and beware the tacrolimus interaction if any azole is added for another indication.

Key learning PCP mortality is 20–60%. Pneumatocoeles and pneumothoraces are common — watch for barotrauma on the ventilator. Isolation is advisable as person-to-person spread is suspected.

Case 6 — Candida in the tracheal aspirate of a long-stay ventilated patient

Presentation A 70-year-old with COPD is on day 14 of mechanical ventilation. The tracheal aspirate grows Candida albicans. He is afebrile with stable white cell count and no new infiltrate.

Do Nothing antifungal. This is almost certainly colonisation. Look for another explanation for any clinical deterioration. Do not start fluconazole "just in case".

Key learning Candida is frequently isolated from respiratory secretions in the critically ill, but true lower respiratory tract infection is rare. Growth from respiratory specimens alone should not prompt antifungal therapy in most patients.

Case 7 — Candiduria in a catheterised patient with pyuria

Presentation A 72-year-old with a long-term urinary catheter has Candida grown on urine culture. Urinalysis shows pyuria. She is haemodynamically stable and apyrexial.

Do Remove or change the catheter first. Remember pyuria is unreliable — up to 25% have bacterial co-infection. Treat systemically only if she is severely immunocompromised, septic with no other source, symptomatic, or undergoing a urological procedure. Otherwise removal of the predisposing factor may be sufficient.

Key learning In the severely immunocompromised, fever + candiduria may be a surrogate marker of disseminated disease and needs treatment. In everyone else — treat the catheter, not the culture.

Case 8 — Breakthrough candidaemia on fluconazole prophylaxis

Presentation A surgical ICU patient has been on fluconazole prophylaxis for 3 weeks. He develops a new fever and a blood culture grows Candida glabrata.

Do Switch to an echinocandin. Fluconazole use selects out resistant non-albicans species such as C. glabrata and C. krusei. Remove lines, repeat cultures, and arrange echo and retinal examination.

Key learning Non-albicans candidaemia is increasing, is associated with worse outcomes, and should be assumed when a patient breaks through azole prophylaxis. Previous fluconazole exposure is a strong hint.

Case 9 — Prosthetic valve and persistently positive Candida blood cultures

Presentation A 66-year-old man with a mechanical aortic valve has persistent Candida bacteraemia despite appropriate antifungal therapy and removal of his central line. He has a new early diastolic murmur.

Do Urgent echocardiography (TTE, and TOE if TTE is non-diagnostic) for infective endocarditis. Involve cardiology/cardiac surgery early. Persistently positive blood cultures despite treatment and removal of an infected focus indicate the need to investigate for endovascular involvement.

Key learning The two classic complications of disseminated candidiasis are endocarditis and ocular involvement — which is why every candidaemia patient should get an echo and a dilated retinal examination. Prosthetic material makes eradication very hard without surgery.

Case 10 — Severe ARDS with an air crescent on CT

Presentation A 60-year-old with severe viral pneumonitis (influenza/COVID) has been on high-dose steroids and 10 days of broad-spectrum antibacterials. He deteriorates with worsening hypoxia and haemoptysis. CT chest shows a cavity with an air crescent and a pleural wedge of consolidation.

Do Think invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in a non-neutropenic ICU patient. Send serum and BAL galactomannan and 1–3 beta-D-glucan, and blood PCR. Start voriconazole (or an amphotericin if azole interactions are prohibitive, remembering A. terreus is amphotericin-resistant). Minimise steroids where possible. Beware massive haemoptysis from angio-invasion and pseudoaneurysm rupture.

Key learning Aspergillosis is not just a neutropenic disease. Severe viral pneumonia, high-dose steroids, COPD and prolonged ICU stay are increasingly recognised risk factors. Air crescent and halo sign on CT are the classic findings.

16 · Memory palace (Anthony Metivier technique) — drug & organism mnemonics
How to build this palace (Metivier's method):
  1. Location — choose a place you know intimately. Use your own ICU as the route (ambulance bay → reception → corridor → bed spaces → store cupboard → sluice → coffee room).
  2. Image — put one exaggerated, multi-sensory image at each station. Make it huge, colourful, smelly, noisy.
  3. Action / interaction — make the image move and do something to you. Static images fade; moving ones stick.
  4. Recall — mentally walk the route in order. Revisit at day 1, day 3, day 7, day 30 (spaced repetition).
