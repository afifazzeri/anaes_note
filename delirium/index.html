1. At a glance — the numbers that matter Epidemiology

Definition. Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness (DSM-5). Postoperative delirium typically presents on postoperative days 2–5 and may occur up to 30 days after surgery.

Incidence by population

Consequences

  • Length of stay: +2–3 days in hospital; +2 days in ITU.
  • Mortality: 30-day mortality 7–10% vs ~1% without delirium.
  • Discharge destination: 2–3× higher risk of needing a care facility.
  • Function: significant functional decline; increased dependency in basic ADLs; falls and pressure injuries.
  • Cost: estimated additional USD 2,000–8,000 per case.
  • Subtypes: hypoactive ~71% (easily missed), hyperactive, and mixed.

Hypoactive delirium is the trap. Anhedonia, decreased alertness, and quiet withdrawal are more common than agitation. Screen actively — do not wait for the patient to become combative.

2. Recognition & screening Diagnosis

DSM-5 core criteria

  1. Disturbance in attention and awareness.
  2. Develops over a short period and tends to fluctuate over the day.
  3. Additional cognitive disturbance (memory deficit, disorientation, language, visuospatial).
  4. Not better explained by another neurocognitive disorder; not in coma.

Bedside symptoms to look for

  • Change in arousal — drowsiness, reduced arousal, or hypervigilance.
  • Delayed awakening from anaesthesia.
  • Abrupt worsening confusion over hours to days; difficulty tracking conversation or following instructions.
  • Disorganised, slow, or rapid speech; quick-changing emotions; irritability; refusal to engage with care.
  • New paranoid thoughts or delusions; illusions or hallucinations.
  • Motor changes — slowed/decreased movement, purposeless fidgeting, inability to maintain posture.
  • Sleep–wake cycle reversal; decreased appetite; new urinary or faecal incontinence.
  • Fluctuation of symptoms over minutes to hours.

Validated screening tools

ToolSettingNotes

Caveats. Neither 3D-CAM nor CAM-ICU reliably distinguishes syndromal from subsyndromal delirium. Delirium is frequently misdiagnosed as depression or dementia. A focused staff education programme improves recognition.

4. Pathophysiology — the working hypotheses Mechanism

Neuroinflammation

  • Surgery raises systemic inflammatory mediators (CRP, IL-6) that remain elevated postoperatively.
  • Elevated preoperative CRP and IL-6 also predict delirium — pre-existing pathology contributes.
  • Peripheral inflammation → loss of blood–brain barrier integrity → translocation of inflammatory cells and mediators into the CNS.
  • Consequences: loss of synaptic plasticity, neuro-apoptosis, impaired neurogenesis.
  • Amyloid-β deposition has been proposed, but multiple surgeries under GA were not associated with increased brain amyloid in older adults; abnormal cortical thickening was reported.

Neurotransmitter disturbance

  • Acetylcholine: low preoperative and postoperative acetylcholinesterase activity is an independent risk factor; centrally acting anticholinergics (e.g. amitriptyline) increase risk.
  • Dopamine: receptor and transporter gene polymorphisms alter delirium risk (confirmed in meta-analysis).
  • Monoamines: altered metabolism reported, but not confirmed as independent risk factor on multivariable analysis.

Subclinical cerebral vascular events

  • Hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and previous stroke are risk factors.
  • Radiological cerebral ischaemia in 7–10% of older surgical patients → more than double the risk of delirium (covert stroke).
  • Cerebral perfusion pressure: every 10 mmHg reduction was associated with double the delirium risk in lung transplant patients; pressure above the autoregulatory limit is also an independent risk factor.
5. Prevention — preoperative Interventions

Avoid perioperative polypharmacy

  • Polypharmacy is an independent risk factor in older patients; drug–drug interactions compound risk.
  • Review and deprescribe agents with direct deliriogenic potential before surgery.

Avoid prolonged fluid fasting (>6 h)

  • 2 h clear fluid fasting is recommended to allow gastric emptying, but practice often exceeds this.
  • Prolonged fasting causes dehydration and unnecessary IV fluids.
  • Fluid fasting >6 h is an independent risk factor for delirium — odds ratio 10.6.

Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA)

  • Multidisciplinary evaluation of medical, functional, psychological, and social needs before surgery.
  • Individualised optimisation plan made in advance.
  • Clinical trial: preoperative CGA and optimisation reduced delirium incidence from 24% → 11%.

Preoperative pain management

  • Preoperative pain is associated with 1.5–3× higher risk of postoperative delirium.
  • Pain imposes direct cognitive burden, triggers acute stress response, and increases complications (e.g. atelectasis).
  • Fascia iliaca block for neck of femur fracture: pooled data suggest reduced delirium risk.
  • Early surgical repair of femur fractures reduces pain and may reduce delirium, though evidence is limited.
6. Prevention — intraoperative Interventions
InterventionEvidenceEffect / numbers
Dexmedetomidine Effective Meta-analysis Intraoperative + postoperative infusion: OR 0.35 for delirium. Noncardiac surgery: RR 0.61, NNT 10. Prophylactic 0.1 µg/kg/h in ICU patients >65 yr after noncardiac surgery reduced delirium.
Paracetamol Effective Clinical trial 1 g every 6 h for 2 days: delirium 28% → 10%, NNT 5.6 (cardiac surgery).
NSAIDs (parecoxib) Effective Clinical trial Parecoxib 40 mg every 12 h for 3 days: delirium 11% → 6%, NNT 20 (hip/knee). Observational cohort >1 million patients: OR 0.85. Note: renal safety not reported in the trial.
Neuraxial / regional anaesthesia Cohort data Two large observational studies (40,000 and 1 million patients) report regional anaesthesia independently associated with 20–40% lower delirium incidence. Meta-analysis of hip fracture trials found mostly no difference but was underpowered.
Multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia Recommended Higher postoperative pain scores and longer-acting opioids both increase delirium risk. Multimodal analgesia is likely the optimal strategy.
Avoid benzodiazepines Avoid Cohort Perioperative benzodiazepine administration: 2–2.5× higher delirium risk. Midazolam identified as a risk factor in surgical and ICU patients. Discourage routine premedication — except pre-existing anxiety or benzodiazepine/alcohol withdrawal.
Avoid gabapentinoids Balance Cohort Perioperative gabapentinoids: OR 1.26 for delirium. Must be balanced against opioid-sparing benefit. A randomised trial found gabapentin did not reduce delirium in older surgical patients.
Avoid scopolamine Avoid Guidelines Anticholinergic antiemetic; case reports of scopolamine-induced delirium; expert guidelines recommend avoiding in older patients.
Depth of anaesthesia monitoring (BIS) Equivocal Meta-analyses MacKenzie 2018 and Bocskai 2020: monitoring reduced delirium. Miao 2019 (BIS only, >60 yr): OR 0.69, P = 0.05 — not statistically significant. See Section 10.
TIVA vs volatile agent No difference Cochrane meta-analysis of 5 trials: no significant difference in delirium. Overall quality of evidence very low (bias, inconsistency). Volatile agents may induce/exacerbate neuroinflammation in laboratory models.
Ketamine (sub-hypnotic) No difference 0.2–0.5 mg/kg at induction: no increased delirium risk. Quality of evidence low. Large multicentre trial (0.5 vs 1.0 mg/kg vs saline): no difference in delirium, but higher rates of hallucinations and nightmares with each dose increment.
Xenon No difference One small off-pump CABG trial favoured xenon over sevoflurane (HR 4.2 for sevoflurane), but larger trials in cardiac surgery and hip fracture found no difference.
Minimally invasive surgery Conflicting Reduces pain, stress, and inflammatory response, but cohort evidence on delirium is conflicting. Requires more robust study.
Strict BP control / haemodynamics Equivocal No consistent association between intraoperative hypotension and delirium. Higher vasopressor requirement is associated with delirium in high-risk surgery (may reflect cardiovascular compromise). Pilot trial targeting MAP >90% of baseline: no significant difference.
Goal-directed fluid therapy No difference Earlier observational study suggested 75% reduction in spinal surgery; recent RCT (n=180) found no significant difference. Small studies, likely underpowered.
Transfusion strategy No difference Small RCT of liberal vs restrictive thresholds: no difference. Observational studies show a dose-dependent association between transfusion volume and delirium — likely confounded by blood loss and haemodynamic instability.
Avoid hypothermia Association Cohort Cardiac surgery patients who developed delirium had lower minimum intraoperative temperature (34.5 °C vs 35 °C, P = 0.035). Whether milder hypothermia matters is unclear.

Intraoperative bottom line: the strongest evidence-based levers are dexmedetomidine, paracetamol, NSAIDs, and multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia — plus avoiding benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, and scopolamine.

7. Prevention — postoperative Interventions
InterventionEvidenceEffect / numbers
Multicomponent non-pharmacological care Effective Meta-analysis (14 studies) OR 0.46, NNT 14.3. Variable risk of bias across included studies.
Reorientation alone Effective Clinical trial Reduced incidence of overt delirium by 40%.
Melatonin Effective Meta-analysis Perioperative administration associated with ~40–45% lower risk of postoperative delirium.
Ramelteon Effective Network meta-analysis Pooled finding favoured ramelteon, OR 0.07.
Antipsychotic prophylaxis Benefit vs harm unclear Network meta-analysis: olanzapine OR 0.25, risperidone OR 0.27; haloperidol no benefit. Safety concerns limit clinical value.
High-dose dexamethasone Cardiac surgery only Meta-analysis of 3 cardiac surgery RCTs: up to 100 mg associated with ~20% reduction in delirium. Safety profile in noncardiac patients unclear.

The multicomponent delirium prevention bundle

#Strategy
1Sensory enhancement — ensure glasses, hearing aids, listening amplifiers
2Mobility enhancement — ambulate at least twice daily if possible
3Cognitive orientation and therapeutic activities tailored to the individual
4Pain control with scheduled paracetamol if appropriate
5Cognitive stimulation tailored to interests and mental status
6Simple communication standards to prevent escalation of behaviours
7Nutritional and fluid repletion enhancement
8Sleep enhancement — daytime sleep hygiene, relaxation, night-time routine
9Medication review and appropriate medication management
10Daily interdisciplinary team rounding to reinforce interventions

Framework. Prevention is best delivered as a protocolised pathway (see Section 9) with multidisciplinary ownership — enthusiastic leadership, stakeholder participation, and efficient pathway management are prerequisites for success.

8. Treatment of established delirium Management

There is no effective pharmacotherapy that shortens delirium or improves its prognosis. Risk reduction is the most important part of management.

Step 1 — Find and treat the underlying cause

First-line treatment is assessment and management of underlying causes:

  • Infection
  • Pain
  • Dehydration
  • Metabolic derangement
  • Constipation
  • Urinary retention

Step 2 — Non-pharmacological measures

  • Reorientation strategies; involve family members where available.
  • Encourage mobility; avoid strict bed rest.
  • Optimise nutrition and fluid therapy.
  • Sleep hygiene and daytime/night-time routines.
  • Medication review.

Step 3 — Pharmacological, with caution

AgentRoleEvidence & cautions
Antipsychotics First-line for agitation symptoms only Do not shorten delirium duration or improve adverse outcomes (multiple meta-analyses). Safety signal: in >350,000 patients with neurocognitive disorders, antipsychotics doubled the risk of death. In palliative care, neither haloperidol nor risperidone reduced delirium severity, and antipsychotics were associated with significantly shorter survival. Short-term risk profile in surgical patients is unclear — weigh potential harm.
Benzodiazepines Avoid Previously used for hyperactive agitation; increasingly recognised as worsening delirium symptoms. Avoid except for withdrawal states.

10. Depth of anaesthesia & BIS — the controversy Evidence

Question: does deliberately targeting deep anaesthesia cause delirium? Current evidence is contradictory, and whether — and for whom — deep anaesthesia is deliriogenic remains unanswered.

The Balanced Anaesthesia Delirium substudy

  • 515-patient substudy of the 6,644-patient Balanced Anaesthesia trial.
  • Adults >60 yr, major noncardiac surgery, general anaesthesia with a volatile agent, nitrous oxide excluded.
  • Compared targeting BIS 35 (deep) vs BIS 50 (light).
OutcomeBIS 35 (deep)BIS 50 (light)P value
Syndromal delirium28%19%P = 0.010
Subsyndromal delirium45%49%P = 0.278 (NS)

But the effect was driven by East Asian centres

Site groupBIS 35BIS 50Difference (95% CI)P
Asia29.4%17.7%+11.7% (3.0–20.1)0.006
Outside Asia24.6%21.8%+2.8% (−13.9–18.9)0.724

BIS–MAC relationship by site

Possible explanations for the site difference

  • Pharmacogenetic variation. CYP2E1*7 — an important P450 enzyme in volatile anaesthetic metabolism — is carried by nearly 34% of the East Asian population vs <2% in other populations.
  • Genome-wide association studies in medicine are overwhelmingly performed in populations of European descent; Asian populations (50% of the world, 4.3 billion people) are under-represented.
  • Differences in medical care, patient status, or other genetic factors cannot be excluded.

Why BIS is an imperfect depth monitor

  • Proprietary processed EEG indices lack a neurobiological foundation — a single number cannot represent a precise brain state for every patient, drug combination, age, sex, ethnicity, and comorbidity profile.
  • BIS readings decrease below 50 with succinylcholine or rocuronium in awake volunteers.
  • Falsely high BIS readings occur: older adults have higher BIS than younger adults at equivalent age-adjusted MAC. This would theoretically promote excessive anaesthetic administration to vulnerable older adults.
  • Time delay in EEG index calculation, effects of noxious stimulation, and transient alpha power loss all degrade reliability — especially when reliability is most needed.

Reconciling the substudy with the parent trial

  • In the substudy, delirium was strongly associated with adverse outcomes: 3× unplanned ICU admission, median 2 extra hospital days (8 vs 6), 6× myocardial infarction (6% vs 1%), 2× 1-year mortality (12% vs 6%).
  • If this held in the parent trial, delirium should have mediated worse outcomes in the deep anaesthesia group — but the larger parent trial found no significant difference in death, major morbidity, quality of recovery (days 1, 2, 3, 30), or global health/disability at 30 days and 1 year.

Three possible interpretations:

  1. Unrecognised bias or noise at some substudy sites — the delirium finding may not be true.
  2. True heterogeneity across sites (e.g. subpopulation genetic susceptibility) — the finding is correct but does not generalise.
  3. The delirium-reducing effect is real and generalised, but anaesthetic-related delirium does not causally mediate other adverse outcomes.

Other trials

  • ENGAGES: randomised to usual care vs avoidance of EEG suppression — no significant difference in postoperative delirium. Limitation: small difference in median EEG suppression duration between groups (7 vs 13 min).
  • The Balanced Anaesthesia Delirium substudy achieved an even smaller absolute difference in EEG suppression (2 vs 6 min), so EEG suppression duration does not explain the contrasting findings.
  • Studies comparing regional anaesthesia (possibly with sedation) vs general anaesthesia, mostly orthopaedic, generally found no significant difference in delirium — making a profound effect of moderate volatile exposure reduction biologically surprising.

Limitations to consider before changing practice

  • In the low BIS group, patients were ~50% more likely to have a cardiovascular disease history (29% vs 20%) — an imbalanced vulnerability, since cardiovascular disease is a delirium risk factor. No baseline-adjusted analysis was performed.
  • Delirium has no gold standard diagnosis; marked variation in incidence across sites, exclusion of some patients with preoperative delirium, and a high combined syndromal + subsyndromal incidence (70.5%).
  • Neither 3D-CAM nor CAM-ICU was designed to distinguish syndromal from subsyndromal delirium.
  • Type 1 errors / false positives occur commonly even in well-conducted trials — replication is needed before clinical implementation.

Practical position: current routine anaesthetic practice is probably not associated with increased delirium risk, whereas deliberately targeting deep anaesthesia for a prolonged duration might be — possibly especially in particular patient populations who may be vulnerable due to genetic susceptibility or comorbidities.

11. Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) Separate entity

POCD is not delirium. It refers to a decline in cognitive function following surgery as measured by neuropsychological testing — ideally with preoperative baseline testing for reference. It does not yet correspond to a specific clinical diagnosis.

Incidence

  • Patients older than 65 years: approximately 25.8% at 1 week and 9.9% at 3 months after surgery.

Risk factors

  • Increasing age
  • Pre-existing cognitive impairment
  • Lower education level

Outcomes

  • Reduced quality of life.
  • Increased mortality.

Nomenclature — current terminology

TermSource / contextMeaning
Mild cognitive impairment (amnesic subtype)National Institute on Aging / Alzheimer's AssociationInterim period between normal cognition and dementia
Mild neurocognitive disorderDSM-5Community terminology — not used for perioperative impairment
Major neurocognitive disorderDSM-5Equivalent to dementia
Perioperative cognitive disordersInternational Perioperative Cognition Nomenclature Working Group (Br J Anaesth)New consensus definition for the anaesthesia/surgery context

Aetiology

  • POCD appears to be independent of the type of surgery and anaesthetic.
  • Proposed mechanisms include inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and cerebral protection failure.
  • Cognitive decline also occurs with age and in the community, independent of surgery — which complicates attribution.
12. One-page checklist Summary

Preoperative

  • ☐ Quantify delirium risk (age, cognition, comorbidity, function, sensory deficits)
  • ☐ Comprehensive geriatric assessment if high risk
  • ☐ Review and stop deliriogenic drugs (benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, scopolamine)
  • ☐ Avoid fluid fasting >6 h
  • ☐ Treat preoperative pain (consider fascia iliaca block for NOF fracture)
  • ☐ Consider early surgery for hip fracture

Intraoperative

  • ☐ Multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia
  • ☐ Consider dexmedetomidine infusion
  • ☐ Consider paracetamol and/or NSAID unless contraindicated
  • ☐ Avoid benzodiazepines and gabapentinoids
  • ☐ Maintain normothermia
  • ☐ Consider depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring — evidence is equivocal
  • ☐ No clear evidence favouring TIVA over volatile, or a specific BP/fluid/transfusion target

Postoperative

  • ☐ Screen actively for delirium — remember hypoactive subtype (71%)
  • ☐ Multicomponent non-pharmacological bundle (NNT 14.3)
  • ☐ Reorientation, mobility, sensory aids, sleep hygiene, hydration, nutrition
  • ☐ Consider melatonin or ramelteon
  • ☐ Treat underlying causes: infection, pain, dehydration, metabolic, constipation, retention
  • ☐ Antipsychotics for distressing agitation only — do not shorten delirium, safety concerns
  • ☐ Avoid benzodiazepines for delirium symptoms
