Levels of recommendation (4th Edition)

LevelBasis
Level IHigh-quality body of evidence
Level II AModerate-quality body of evidence
Level II BClass 2 studies, direct evidence, overall low quality
Level IIIClass 3 studies, or Class 2 studies providing only indirect evidence
InsufficientNo studies, or quality limitations preclude a conclusion → no recommendation made

The level of recommendation is driven by the quality of the body of evidence, not by the class of the individual studies. A single Class 1 study may support a Level I, a Level II, or no recommendation at all depending on applicability, consistency and precision.

Scope

  • Adult, severe TBI (GCS 3–8) — in-hospital management only.
  • Topics are limited to those that are TBI-specific; general trauma/ICU good practice is excluded.
  • Recommendations are made only where evidence exists — this is not a complete clinical protocol.
  • This edition is transitional: the BTF is moving to a “Living Guidelines” model of continuous literature monitoring.

Part I · Treatments

1Decompressive craniectomyLevel II A
Level IInsufficient evidence to support a Level I recommendation.
Level II A
Bifrontal DC is NOT recommended to improve outcomes (GOS-E at 6 months) in severe TBI with diffuse injury without mass lesions and ICP elevation >20 mm Hg for >15 minutes within a 1-hour period refractory to first-tier therapies. However, the procedure reduces ICP and minimises ICU days.
Level II A
A large frontotemporoparietal DC (not less than 12 × 15 cm, or 15 cm diameter) is recommended over a small frontotemporoparietal DC for reduced mortality and improved neurological outcomes.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Cooper 2011 (DECRA) Class 1 RCT, N=155
DC 73 vs no DC 82		 Worse GOS-E at 6 months with DC — OR 1.84 (95% CI 1.05–3.24), p=0.03.
Mean ICP after randomisation 14.4 vs 19.1 mm Hg, p<0.001.
ICU days 13 vs 18, p<0.001.
Mortality at 6 months 19% vs 18%.
Post hoc adjustment for baseline pupil reactivity → differences no longer significant.
Jiang 2005 Class 2 RCT, N=486
Standard (12×15 cm) vs limited (6×8 cm)		 GOS 4–5: 39.8% vs 28.6%, p=0.05. Mortality 26.2% vs 35.1%, p<0.05 (favouring larger flap).
Qiu 2009 Class 2 RCT, N=74
Large (15 cm) vs routine temporoparietal		 Mortality at 1 month 27% vs 57%, p=0.010. Good outcome (GOS 4–5) at 1 year 56.8% vs 32.4%, p=0.035. More delayed haematoma and subdural effusion with larger DC.

Committee caveat: the RESCUEicp results may alter these recommendations — check https://braintrauma.org/coma/guidelines for updates before applying.

2Prophylactic hypothermiaLevel II B
Level ILevel II AInsufficient evidence to support either.
Level II B
Early (within 2.5 hours), short-term (48 hours post-injury) prophylactic hypothermia is NOT recommended to improve outcomes in patients with diffuse injury. There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation outside these conditions.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Clifton 2011 (NABIS:H II) Class 1 RCT, N=97
Hypothermia 52 vs normothermia 45		 Mortality 12/52 vs 8/45 — RR 1.30 (95% CI 0.58–2.52), p=0.52.
Poor outcome RR 1.08 (95% CI 0.76–1.53), p=0.67.
Subgroups: diffuse injury 70% vs 50% poor outcome (p=0.09); surgically evacuated haematoma 33% vs 69% (p=0.02, favouring hypothermia).
Study stopped for futility.
Clifton 2001 Class 2 RCT, N=392 Mortality 28% vs 27%, p=0.79. Severe disability/vegetative/dead 57% in both groups. Trend to harm in patients hypothermic on arrival randomised to normothermia.
Jiang 2000 Class 2 RCT, N=87 (China) Mortality at 1 year 25.6% vs 45.5%; better GOS at 1 year 46.5% vs 27.3%, p<0.05.

Why no meta-analysis this edition: the hypothermia protocols differed in clinically important ways — target temperature (32–33 °C to 33–35 °C), cooling duration (24 h to 14 days), and rewarming rate (spontaneous to 1 °C/hour). Pooling was judged inappropriate. There is insufficient evidence on duration of cooling and on head-only vs systemic cooling.

3Hyperosmolar therapyInsufficient
Level I / II / III
Although hyperosmolar therapy may lower ICP, there was insufficient evidence about effects on clinical outcomes to support a specific recommendation, or to support use of any specific hyperosmolar agent, in severe TBI.

The Committee is universally of the view that hyperosmolar agents are useful — but current literature does not meet contemporary evidence-based criteria. The 3rd Edition recommendations are restated below for recognition only; they are not supported by evidence meeting current standards.

Carried over — not currently evidence-supported
  • Mannitol is effective for control of raised ICP at 0.25–1 g/kg body weight.
  • Avoid arterial hypotension (systolic BP < 90 mm Hg).
  • Restrict mannitol use prior to ICP monitoring to patients with signs of transtentorial herniation, or progressive neurological deterioration not attributable to extracranial causes.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Mangat 2014 Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=73
Hypertonic saline vs mannitol		 2-week mortality not significantly different (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.05–5.51, p=0.56).
Cumulative ICP burden 15.2% vs 36.5%, p=0.003.
Daily ICP burden 0.3 vs 1.3 hours/day, p=0.001.
ICU days 8.5 vs 9.8, p=0.004.
HTS more effective at lowering ICP burden; no mortality effect.
Cottenceau 2011 Class 3 RCT, N=47 No significant difference in time with ICP >20 mm Hg, or GOS at 6 months.
Ichai 2009 Class 3 RCT, N=34
Sodium lactate vs mannitol		 ICP significantly lower with sodium lactate (p=0.016); better 1-year GOS but not powered for this.
4Cerebrospinal fluid (EVD) drainageLevel III
Level I / IIInsufficient evidence.
Level III
  • An EVD system zeroed at the midbrain with continuous drainage of CSF may be considered to lower ICP burden more effectively than intermittent use.
  • Use of CSF drainage to lower ICP in patients with an initial GCS < 6 during the first 12 hours after injury may be considered.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Nwachuku 2013 Class 3 retrospective cohort, N=62
Continuous (open) vs intermittent (closed)		 Area under ICP curve 962.7 vs 608.8, p<0.0001.
Area under ICP curve above 20 mm Hg 59.7 vs 17.2, p=0.0002.
6-month survival 71.0% vs 77.4%, p=0.56; favourable GOS 42% vs 26%, p=0.35.
Closed EVD → significantly higher ICP burden.
Griesdale 2010 Class 3 retrospective cohort, N=171
EVD 93 vs no EVD 73		 In-hospital mortality, all patients OR 2.76 (95% CI 1.07–7.08), p=0.04.
GCS < 6: OR 0.76 (0.18–3.2), p=0.71 (favouring EVD).
GCS ≥ 6: OR 5.6 (1.7–18.4), p<0.01 (harm).

Both studies were single-centre, retrospective, and underpowered to detect infrequent complications. Continuous drainage may be superior for ICP lowering, but this needs multi-institutional verification with complications assessed.

5Ventilation therapies (hyperventilation)Level II B
Level I / II AInsufficient evidence.
Level II B
Prolonged prophylactic hyperventilation with PaCO₂ ≤ 25 mm Hg is NOT recommended.
Carried over from 3rd Edition — not currently evidence-supported
  • Hyperventilation is recommended as a temporising measure for reduction of elevated ICP.
  • Hyperventilation should be avoided during the first 24 hours after injury, when CBF is often critically reduced.
  • If hyperventilation is used, monitor SjO₂ or BtpO₂ to monitor oxygen delivery.

Key evidence — Muizelaar 1991

DesignGroupsResult
Class 2 RCT, N=113 Control PaCO₂ 35 ± 2 mm Hg (n=41)
HV PaCO₂ 25 ± 2 mm Hg (n=36)
HV + tromethamine (n=36)		 3 months: HV worse, p<0.03. 6 months: HV worse, p<0.05. 12 months: not statistically significant.
Effect driven by patients with motor GCS 4–5; no difference in motor score 1–3.

Normal ventilation is the goal in the absence of cerebral herniation; normal PaCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg. CBF is linearly responsive to PaCO₂ between 20 and 80 mm Hg.

6Anaesthetics, analgesics & sedativesLevel II B
Level I / II AInsufficient evidence.
Level II B
  • Administration of barbiturates to induce burst suppression on EEG as prophylaxis against intracranial hypertension is not recommended.
  • High-dose barbiturates are recommended to control elevated ICP refractory to maximum standard medical and surgical treatment. Haemodynamic stability is essential before and during barbiturate therapy.
  • Propofol is recommended for control of ICP, but not for improvement in mortality or 6-month outcomes. Caution is required — high-dose propofol can produce significant morbidity.

Key evidence

Drug / questionStudyResult
Prophylactic barbiturates Ward 1985
Class 2 RCT, N=53		 No significant difference in mortality or 1-year GOS. Hypotension (SBP < 80 mm Hg) in 54% vs 7%, p<0.001.
Barbiturates for refractory ICP Eisenberg 1988
Class 2 RCT, N=73		 ICP more likely to be controlled on barbiturates (odds ~2×). Survival in responders 92% vs 17% in non-responders. No benefit if hypotensive prior to randomisation.
Propofol vs morphine Kelly 1999
Class 2 RCT, N=42		 Favourable 6-month outcome 52.5% vs 47.4%. ICP and therapy intensity level lower on day 3 with propofol, p<0.05. No mortality effect. Post hoc: high-dose propofol favourable outcome 70% vs 38.5% low-dose, p<0.05.

Propofol infusion syndrome — hyperkalaemia, hepatomegaly, lipaemia, metabolic acidosis, myocardial failure, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure and death. Extreme caution with doses >5 mg/kg/hour or any dose exceeding 48 hours in critically ill adults. The Cochrane review concluded there is no evidence that barbiturates improve outcome, and that the hypotensive effect offsets any ICP-lowering benefit on cerebral perfusion pressure.

7SteroidsLevel I — contraindicated
Level I
The use of steroids is not recommended for improving outcome or reducing ICP. In patients with severe TBI, high-dose methylprednisolone was associated with increased mortality and is contraindicated.

Key evidence — CRASH trial

OutcomeMethylprednisolone vs placebo
DesignClass 1 RCT — 10,008 patients, 239 hospitals, 49 countries. Severe TBI subgroup 3,966. Stopped early for harm.
2-week mortality — severe TBI39.8% vs 34.8% — RR 1.14 (95% CI 1.05–1.23), p=0.0013
2-week mortality — all patients21.1% vs 17.9% — RR 1.18 (95% CI 1.09–1.27), p=0.0001
6-month mortality25.7% vs 22.3% (95% CI 1.07–1.24; p=0.0001)
Unfavourable outcome at 6 months38.1% vs 36.3% — RR 1.05 (95% CI 0.99–1.10), p=0.079

The increased mortality was not due to infection or gastrointestinal bleeding. Earlier smaller RCTs (Saul 1981, Marshall 1998 with tirilazad mesylate, N=957) also found no benefit. Quality of the body of evidence: high.

8NutritionLevel II A
Level IInsufficient evidence.
Level II A
Feeding patients to attain basal caloric replacement at least by the fifth day and, at most, by the seventh day post-injury is recommended to decrease mortality.
Level II B
Transgastric jejunal feeding is recommended to reduce the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia.
Glycaemic controlVitamins/supplements

9Infection prophylaxis (VAP & EVD)Level II A / III
Level IInsufficient evidence.
Level II A
  • Early tracheostomy is recommended to reduce mechanical ventilation days when the overall benefit is felt to outweigh the complications. However, there is no evidence that early tracheostomy reduces mortality or the rate of nosocomial pneumonia.
  • Povidone-iodine oral care is NOT recommended to reduce VAP, and may cause an increased risk of acute respiratory distress syndrome.
Level III
Antimicrobial-impregnated catheters may be considered to prevent catheter-related infections during EVD.

10Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxisLevel III
Level I / IIInsufficient evidence.
Level III
  • LMWH or low-dose unfractionated heparin may be used in combination with mechanical prophylaxis. However, there is an increased risk for expansion of intracranial haemorrhage.
  • In addition to compression stockings, pharmacologic prophylaxis may be considered if the brain injury is stable and the benefit is considered to outweigh the risk of increased intracranial haemorrhage.
  • Insufficient evidence regarding the preferred agent, dose, or timing of pharmacologic prophylaxis.

Key evidence (all Class 3 — indirect, mixed-severity populations)

StudyNResult
Kwiat 20121,215 Progression of bleed 42% vs 24%, p<0.0001. Progression after LMWH 14.5%. Neurosurgical intervention 14.5% vs 4.9%, p<0.001. VTE 9.1% vs 3.1%, p<0.001. Risk may exceed benefit.
Mohseni 201278 Mortality 5% vs 19%, p=0.001. VTE 11% vs 30%; OR for VTE in control 3.5 (95% CI 1.0–12.1), p=0.002. No adverse outcomes reported.
Scudday 2011812 VTE 1% vs 3%, p=0.019. Injury progression 6% vs 3%, p=0.055 (not significant).
Daley 2015271 (craniotomy) No significant difference in VTE, ventilation days or LOS. Significantly higher in-hospital mortality in untreated group.
Farooqui 2013 (protocol)236 DVT 0% vs 5.6%, p=0.0080. PE and increase in ICH not significantly different.

TBI is associated with up to 54% incidence of DVT without prophylaxis, and 25% in isolated TBI treated with sequential compression devices alone. Age, subarachnoid haemorrhage, ISS > 15 and extremity injury are predictors.

11Seizure prophylaxisLevel II A
Level IInsufficient evidence.
Level II A
  • Prophylactic use of phenytoin or valproate is NOT recommended for preventing late post-traumatic seizures.
  • Phenytoin IS recommended to decrease the incidence of early PTS (within 7 days of injury), when the overall benefit is felt to outweigh the complications associated with such treatment. However, early PTS have not been associated with worse outcomes.
  • Insufficient evidence to recommend levetiracetam over phenytoin regarding efficacy in preventing early PTS or toxicity.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Temkin 1990 Class 2 RCT, N=404
Phenytoin vs placebo		 Early PTS reduced from 14.2% to 3.6%, RR 0.27 (95% CI 0.12–0.62), p<0.001.
Late PTS: 21.5% vs 15.7% at day 8–1 year, p>0.2 — no significant effect.
Temkin 1999 Class 2 RCT, N=379
Phenytoin vs valproate		 Early PTS 1.5% vs 4.5%. Late PTS no significant difference. Trend toward higher mortality in valproate groups.
Dikmen 1991 Class 2 RCT, N=244 (severe TBI subgroup 71) At 1 month, phenytoin group performed significantly more poorly on neurobehavioural measures; no significant effect at 12 months.
Inaba 2013 Class 2 prospective observational, N=813
Levetiracetam vs phenytoin		 No difference in seizure rate (1.5% vs 1.5%, p=0.997), adverse drug reactions (7.9% vs 10.3%), or mortality (5.4% vs 3.7%).

Risk factors for early PTS: GCS ≤ 10, immediate seizures, post-traumatic amnesia > 30 min, linear or depressed skull fracture, penetrating head injury, subdural/epidural/intracerebral haematoma, cortical contusion, age ≤ 65, chronic alcoholism.

Part II · Monitoring

12Intracranial pressure monitoringLevel II B
Level I / II AInsufficient evidence.
Level II B
Management of severe TBI patients using information from ICP monitoring is recommended to reduce in-hospital and 2-week post-injury mortality.
Carried over from 3rd Edition — not currently evidence-supported
  • ICP should be monitored in all salvageable patients with severe TBI (GCS 3–8 after resuscitation) and an abnormal CT scan — i.e. haematomas, contusions, swelling, herniation, or compressed basal cisterns.
  • ICP monitoring is indicated in severe TBI with a normal CT scan if two or more of the following: age > 40 years, unilateral or bilateral motor posturing, systolic BP < 90 mm Hg.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Chesnut 2012 (BEST TRIP) Class 1 RCT, N=324
ICP-monitored vs imaging/clinical exam		 6-month mortality 39% vs 41%, p=0.6. Unfavourable outcome 17% vs 17%. Favourable 44% vs 39%, p=0.4. No difference between strategies. Conducted in Bolivia and Ecuador.
Alali 2013 Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=10,628
155 trauma centres		 ICP monitoring associated with significantly lower odds of death — adjusted OR 0.44 (95% CI 0.31–0.63), p<0.0001. Effect more pronounced under age 65.
Farahvar 2012 Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=1,307 Adjusted OR for 2-week mortality 0.64 (95% CI 0.41–1.00), p=0.05 (adults).
Talving 2013 Class 2 prospective cohort, N=216 Adjusted OR mortality 0.15 (95% CI −0.03 to 0.74), p=0.019. Mortality due to brain herniation OR 0.34 (0.10–0.87), p=0.046.

Evidence from the multi-centre Class 1 RCT tempers but does not negate the observational data. Intracranial hypertension is an important secondary insult and its alleviation is pivotal to good care. The RCT was conducted in settings with limited pre-hospital care and where monitors are uncommon — applicability concerns have been raised.

13Cerebral perfusion pressure monitoringLevel II B
Level IInsufficient evidence.
Level II B
Management of severe TBI patients using guidelines-based recommendations for CPP monitoring is recommended to decrease 2-week mortality.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Gerber 2013 Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=2,320
22 New York State trauma centres		 Age-adjusted 2-week mortality fell 22.4% (2001–03) → 19.7% (04–06) → 13.3% (07–09).
CPP monitoring compliance rose from 14.6% → 34.2% → 48.2% — the largest increase of all guideline components analysed.
Huang 2006 Class 3 retrospective cohort, N=213 ICP-targeted therapy had higher mortality than CPP-targeted (p=0.02) and modified-CPP-targeted (p=0.03) groups. No difference between CPP >70 and CPP >60 groups.

CPP is the gradient across the cerebral vascular bed: normally MAP − JVP, but in TBI it is effectively MAP − mean ICP. It can only be calculated when ICP is known.

14Advanced cerebral monitoringLevel III
Level I / IIInsufficient evidence. Although patients with desaturations identified by advanced monitoring have poorer outcomes, Level II evidence showed no improvement in outcomes for monitored patients.
Level III
Jugular bulb monitoring of arteriovenous oxygen content difference (AVDO₂), as a source of information for management decisions, may be considered to reduce mortality and improve outcomes at 3 and 6 months post-injury.

The Level III recommendation for brain tissue oxygen monitoring has been removed because of higher-quality, contradictory evidence acquired since the 3rd Edition. Transcranial Doppler autoregulation monitoring and microdialysis glutamate monitoring each had only a single Class 3 study — insufficient to recommend.

Part III · Thresholds

15Blood pressure thresholdsLevel III
Level I / IIInsufficient evidence.
Level III
Maintaining SBP ≥ 100 mm Hg for patients 50–69 years old, or ≥ 110 mm Hg for patients 15–49 or over 70 years old, may be considered to decrease mortality and improve outcomes.

Key evidence — Berry 2012

Age groupOptimal hypotension thresholdAdjusted OR for death
15–49 years110 mm Hg1.98 (95% CI 1.65–2.39), p<0.0001
50–69 years100 mm Hg2.20 (95% CI 1.46–3.31), p=0.0002
≥ 70 years110 mm Hg1.92 (95% CI 1.35–2.74), p=0.0003

Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=15,733 (26.9% with GCS ≤ 8). This is indirect evidence (mixed moderate/severe TBI, not reported separately), which is why it supports only Level III. The traditional threshold of SBP < 90 mm Hg should be reconsidered. Butcher 2007 (N=6,801, IMPACT database) suggested targeting SBP 120–150 mm Hg and MAP 85–110 mm Hg.

16Intracranial pressure thresholdsLevel II B
Level I / II AInsufficient evidence.
Level II B
Treating ICP above 22 mm Hg is recommended, because values above this level are associated with increased mortality.
Level III
A combination of ICP values and clinical and brain CT findings may be used to make management decisions.

Key evidence — Sorrentino 2012

ParameterThresholdFor
ICP22 mm HgReduced mortality (χ²=58.18, p<0.001) and favourable outcome (χ²=18.15, p<0.001)
ICP (subgroups >55 y and women)18 mm HgFavourable outcome — subgroup analysis, underpowered, not used for recommendation
CPP70 mm HgMortality and favourable outcome
PRx (pressure reactivity index)0.25 / 0.050.25 for reduced mortality; 0.05 for increased favourable outcome

Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=459, single centre (Cambridge, UK), data 1992–2009. Because it is a single study it yields only low-quality body of evidence — hence Level II B rather than Level II A. Patients can herniate at ICP below 20–25 mm Hg; the likelihood depends on the location of an intracranial mass lesion (temporal/temporoparietal lesions and clot size > 30 cc carry higher herniation risk). CT classification may shift the tolerable ICP threshold (reported range 22–36 mm Hg).

Committee caveat: RESCUEicp results may affect this recommendation — check https://braintrauma.org/coma/ for updates.

17Cerebral perfusion pressure thresholdsLevel II B
Level I / II AInsufficient evidence.
Level II B
The recommended target CPP value for survival and favourable outcomes is between 60 and 70 mm Hg. Whether 60 or 70 mm Hg is the minimum optimal threshold is unclear and may depend on the patient's autoregulatory status.
Level III
Avoiding aggressive attempts to maintain CPP above 70 mm Hg with fluids and pressors may be considered, because of the risk of adult respiratory failure.

Key evidence

StudyDesign / NResult
Allen 2014 Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=1,757 Survivors: CPP > 60 → 84.0%; CPP 50–60 → 83.6%; CPP < 50 → 62.3%. RR low vs high 2.35 (95% CI 1.88–2.95), p<0.0001.
Sorrentino 2012 Class 2 retrospective cohort, N=459 70 mm Hg optimal for mortality and neurological outcome; 75 mm Hg in patients > 55 years. In preserved autoregulation (PRx < 0.05), no difference between CPP ≥ 70 and < 70. In impaired autoregulation (PRx ≥ 0.05), CPP < 70 → significantly poorer outcomes.
Robertson 1999 Class 3 RCT, N=189
CPP > 70 vs ICP-targeted (CPP > 50)		 No difference in neurological outcome at 3 or 6 months. ARDS 15% vs 3.3%, p=0.007. Jugular desaturations more frequent in the ICP protocol group (OR 2.367, p=0.012).

Views have evolved: Rosner advocated very high CPP; Robertson found CPP > 70 mm Hg associated with respiratory complications and poorer outcome. Optimal CPP may need to be individualised to the patient's pressure-reactivity status.

Authors: Carney N, Totten AM, O'Reilly C, Ullman JS, Hawryluk GWJ, Bell MJ, Bratton SL, Chesnut R, Harris OA, Kissoon N, Rubiano AM, Shutter L, Tasker RC, Vavilala MS, Wilberger J, Wright DW, Ghajar J.

Produced with the Pacific Northwest Evidence-based Practice Center, Oregon Health & Science University. Reviewed for evidence-based integrity and endorsed by the American Association of Neurological Surgeons (AANS) and the Congress of Neurological Surgeons (CNS).

Funding: U.S. Army Contracting Command, Aberdeen Proving Ground, Natick Contracting Division (Contract W911QY-14-C-0086, awarded to Stanford University, with subcontracts to the Brain Trauma Foundation and Oregon Health & Science University); and the Brain Trauma Foundation.

Living Guidelines updates: https://braintrauma.org/coma/guidelines

Important. This page is a faithful condensation of the source document's recommendations, evidence tables and stated rationale. It is a quick reference only and does not replace the full guideline, local protocols, or clinical judgement. The source explicitly states that adherence to these guidelines does not necessarily assure a successful medical outcome, that they should not be considered inclusive of all proper procedures, and that decisions are appropriately made only by a competent licensed physician in light of each patient's individual circumstances and available resources.
