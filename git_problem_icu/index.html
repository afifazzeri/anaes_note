GI Dysfunction in the Critically Ill — Quick Reference

Anaesthesia & Intensive Care · ESICM MEN Section + Curr Opin Crit Care
1 · Definitions, scoring & why it matters

Gastrointestinal (GI) dysfunction = functional impairment of the GI tract, which may include disturbed motility and/or absorption, breaches in mucosal integrity, changes in the microbiome, raised intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), impaired mesenteric perfusion, and infections of the GI tract.

Why it matters GI dysfunction is frequent in the critically ill, is associated with worse clinical outcomes, may aggravate multi-organ failure, and can deteriorate into life-threatening emergencies (bowel ischaemia, Ogilvie's syndrome, perforation, massive GI bleeding, abdominal compartment syndrome).

GI symptoms used in assessment

  • Core symptoms: vomiting/regurgitation, abdominal distension, GI bleeding, diarrhoea, lower GI paralysis.
  • Expanded (if assessable): nausea, abdominal pain, absent bowel sounds, large gastric residual volume (GRV >500 mL/6 h), radiological bowel dilatation, bowel wall thickening / bowel oedema.
Key limitation There is no gold standard for monitoring GI function, no validated scoring system, and no validated biomarker for clinical use. No single symptom correlates with mortality — but an increasing number of concurrent GI symptoms does.

Scoring concepts

Two generations of GI scoring
ToolStructureStatus / notes
AGI
Acute Gastrointestinal Injury		 Grades I (low risk) → IV (life-threatening) ESICM 2012 terminology; improved communication, but has now been replaced by GIDS.
GIDS
Gastrointestinal Dysfunction Score		 Built on the SOFA model; 0 (no risk) → 4 (life-threatening) Developed in a large prospective multicentre observational study (ISOFAS, n = 540). Now the preferred construct. Still no reliable, reproducible biomarker linked to it.
NRS-2002 Nutrition Risk Screening, scored within 48 h of admission ≥5 points = increased mortality. First step whenever addressing nutrition therapy.

Feeding intolerance — the definition problem

Diagnosed on at least twice-daily clinical examination, based on: repeated or profuse vomiting, regurgitation, abdominal distension, absent/abnormal bowel sounds, abdominal pain, absence/presence of stool, flow from any stoma, severe diarrhoea, raised IAP, and/or GI bleeding.

Consensus needed The large variety of definitions of feeding intolerance confuses interpretation across studies. A consensus definition is a priority to determine clinical importance and refine management. These easily available variables are often not recorded on the chart — and therefore go unrecognised.

Burden of disease — quick numbers

  • Constipation incidence 5–83% (more frequent than diarrhoea, but less often diagnosed).
  • Diarrhoea prevalence 2–68%.
  • Antibiotics prescribed in >70% of ICU patients — the leading cause of diarrhoea.
  • Mortality from antibiotic-associated diarrhoea (including C. difficile) 10–56%.
  • GI tract consumes 30–40% of basal energy expenditure → highly vulnerable during haemodynamic shock.
  • GI failure is not represented in SOFA — only the liver stands in for the whole GI tract.

3 · Monitoring GI function at the bedside

Current techniques are limited. Clinical assessment, often combined with GRV, is widely used but gives an imprecise picture of global GI function.

Monitoring modalities — what they give and what limits them
ModalityWhat it can showLimitations / caveats
Clinical assessment
(symptoms + GRV)		 Number of GI symptoms is associated with increased mortality. No gold standard; single symptoms do not correlate with mortality; symptoms not clearly correlated with objective measures of GI function. GRV-based feeding strategy did not reduce VAP.
GI bleeding as a marker Used in some multi-organ failure scores. Numerous specific causes and therapeutic modalities — not necessarily related to gut dysfunction.
Delayed gastric emptying / ↑GRV Surrogate for gastroparesis. Can occur without intestinal dysfunction. High residuals predict vomiting risk at EN initiation and in symptomatic patients.
Ultrasound Gastric emptying (antral diameter correlates with GRV and CT-based volumes), bowel peristalsis, bowel diameter, wall thickness, tissue perfusion (Doppler); helps feeding-tube placement. Promising but unvalidated for bedside monitoring; needs a protocolised approach and operator reproducibility. Air in the GI tract degrades quality.
Biomarkers Lactate; citrulline (enterocyte mass — levels <10 µmol/L associated with increased mortality); I-FABP; paracetamol/glucose absorption tests. None validated for clinical use. Confounded by sampling timing, surgical damage, coexisting organ dysfunction (esp. renal), prior gut surgery/length of intact bowel, assay precision and thresholds. Most studies target mesenteric ischaemia or organ dysfunction, not GI function per se.
Absorption of nutrients Isotope-labelled or nutrient-analogue tracers (e.g. 3-O-methyl-glucose, 13C-glucose); whole-body balance studies; faecal bomb calorimetry. No routine clinical method. Absorption is markedly attenuated in critical illness. Results differ depending on intragastric vs intraduodenal administration (gastric emptying delay distorts). Bomb calorimetry needs stool passage — infrequent in ICU.
Barrier function Electron microscopy; double/triple sugar absorption tests; bacterial 16S rDNA RT-PCR; endotoxin assays. No valid routine method. Biopsy is invasive and a snapshot. Sugar tests confounded by dysmotility, renal/hepatic impairment and antibiotics. Endotoxin/antibody assays are neither gut- nor species-specific. No single biomarker can summarise the system.
Intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) Easily measured at the bedside (bladder technique: instill 50 mL saline via urinary catheter); numeric index of the abdominal compartment. Association with GI function unclear. IAH without GI symptoms was not associated with mortality in ventilated patients. Grossly elevated or rising IAP may require reducing or stopping EN.
Diarrhoea Suggested marker of malabsorption and of feeding intolerance. Existing evidence is scarce. Can signal non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia (NOMI), particularly with early full EN during acute circulatory failure.
Practical core set Until consensus exists, track daily: vomiting/regurgitation, distension, bowel sounds, stool/stoma output, GRV, IAP, abdominal pain, GI bleeding, and actual EN delivered vs prescribed. Position matters — a bed angle <30° is associated with increased gastric aspirates.
4 · GI dysfunction and outcome
  • GI dysfunction is associated with adverse outcome — but reported outcomes and their definitions are very variable across studies.
  • An increasing number of concurrent GI symptoms is associated with increasing mortality; no single symptom in isolation.
  • Indirect evidence supports a role for GI dysfunction in the development and perpetuation of MODS, through associations between severity of GI dysfunction and organ failures.
  • Agreement on a minimum collection of essential outcomes — a core outcome set (COS) — is needed for the field.
  • Open questions: what is the natural course of GI dysfunction in ICU survivors? What distinguishes primary vs secondary GI injury?
5 · Management of GI dysfunction

Management mainly relies on treating the underlying cause. Specific therapeutic options exist but have substantial limitations.

5.1 Prokinetics

Prokinetic agents at a glance
AgentTypical role / doseEvidence & caveats
Metoclopramide First-line; ~10 mg IV q6–8h Widely used despite relatively low efficacy. D2 antagonist with prokinetic (5-HT4) activity. Extrapyramidal and QT effects.
Erythromycin Motilin-receptor agonist; 100 mg × 3/day vs 200–250 mg — optimal dose and duration uncertain Accelerates gastric emptying and may be superior to metoclopramide. In one meta-analysis it was the only prokinetic to reduce feeding intolerance. Caveats: tachyphylaxis, arrhythmias, antibiotic resistance/ecological harm.
Metoclopramide + erythromycin Combination, usually ~48 h Synergistic; effect is sustained longer than either drug alone. Same toxicity caveats.
Domperidone Oral only Limited use in ICU patients.
Non-antibiotic motilin agonists
(e.g. camicinal)		 Investigational Pre-emptive administration had a negligible effect on nutrition provision in a multicentre RCT.
Neostigmine Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor; ~2–2.5 mg IV (or SC), often with an anticholinergic Effective in colonic paralysis and used for acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie's). Bradycardia and other cholinergic effects — monitor.
Bottom line Prokinetics modestly reduce feeding intolerance (absolute risk reduction 17.3%, 95% CI 5–26.8%) and facilitate post-pyloric tube placement — but have no effect on pneumonia, vomiting, diarrhoea, mortality, or length of stay.

Uncertainties: optimal erythromycin dose and duration, repeated courses, definition of lower GI intolerance/dysmotility, and true effect on morbidity and mortality.

5.2 Laxatives & bowel protocols

  • Possible benefit of prophylactic polyethylene glycol or lactulose on time to defaecation — but not on complications.
  • PEG is probably better than lactulose and may reduce the incidence of Ogilvie's syndrome.
  • In a phase II RCT in mechanically ventilated patients, daily laxative therapy from the start reduced SOFA score.
  • Demonstrated effect of formal "bowel protocols" overall remains limited.
  • Opioid-receptor antagonists and stool softeners have been proposed to prevent GI paralysis — evidence limited (opioid antagonists are a top-ranked research priority).

5.3 Post-pyloric feeding

  • Gastric route is preferred; guidelines include post-pyloric feeding for high aspiration risk or gastric feeding intolerance.
  • May reduce ventilator-associated pneumonia.
  • Most trials were small, in patients without feeding intolerance, and heterogeneous (duodenal and jejunal tubes pooled).
  • Open questions: exact indications; post-pyloric vs gastric + prokinetic on patient-centred outcomes; duodenal vs jejunal.

5.4 Systemic management

Systemic factors that worsen or improve GI function
FactorEffectAction / evidence status
Intravenous fluid Excess fluid is associated with GI dysfunction. A restrictive perioperative strategy showed no GI benefit and more renal complications in a large RCT. Insufficient evidence to restrict fluids purely to reduce GI dysfunction.
Glucose Hyperglycaemia slows gastric emptying; hypoglycaemia accelerates it. No specific threshold validated to improve GI function.
Electrolytes Abnormalities suggested to interfere with bowel motility. Insufficient data for specific targets (high-normal K⁺/Mg²⁺ is an open research question).
Opioids & α₂-agonists Both inhibit GI motility. After colorectal surgery, early feeding + multimodal analgesia + morphine restriction speeds recovery of motility (ERAS).
Intra-abdominal hypertension Attenuates splanchnic blood flow; exacerbates bowel oedema; risk factor for NOMI. No intervention targeting IAH has been shown to improve GI function or outcomes. Effect on outcome depends on severity and dynamics. WSACS 3-step medical management: evacuate intraluminal contents and space-occupying lesions → improve abdominal wall compliance → optimise fluid administration and systemic/regional perfusion.
Positioning Bed angle <30° increases gastric aspirates. Keep head of bed elevated where safe.
Feeding & IAP Do not stop EN for a modest IAP rise. Approaching 20 mmHg is a warning against starting or advancing EN — consider trickle feeding and supplemental PN while treating the cause.
6 · Nutrition therapy & the gut

6.1 Screening & route

  • First step: assess nutritional status within 48 h of admission with NRS-2002 (≥5 = higher mortality).
  • Enteral feeding tolerance and the gut's capacity to cover needs remain the de facto gold standard for monitoring GI function.
  • Try the enteral route first for non-nutritional benefits: supports mucosal health (improves blood flow, releases trophic agents such as cholecystokinin and bile salts), stimulates GALT/MALT, feeds the microbiota, and is cheaper than PN.
  • With similar energy targets and rapid progression, large RCTs show equipoise between EN and PN — even in septic shock (NUTRIREA-2, CALORIES). Route matters little; preventing acquired malnutrition matters a lot.
  • GI dysfunction patients are typical candidates for supplemental PN to avoid an escalating energy/protein deficit.

6.2 Timing — what we know

Feeding timing and the gut
StrategyEffect on the gut
Prolonged fasting (7 vs 3 days)Attenuates nutrient absorption once EN is eventually given.

Open question Should intolerance of EN be accepted as a protective adaptive response to critical illness, or treated to increase nutrient delivery? The quantity of nutrients actually absorbed during EN in critical illness is uncertain.

6.3 Fibre

  • Recommended intake 15–30 g/day — should be part of any enteral feeding solution.
  • Soluble fibre (guar gum, partially hydrolysed guar gum) supplies short-chain fatty acids to the mucosa, promotes electrolyte and water reabsorption in the colon, limits pathogen growth, and normalises transit time.
  • Insoluble fibre increases bulk and improves transit, reducing laxative need.
  • Fibre-free EN reduces the number and diversity of micro-organisms — clearly a bad option. Fibre-containing solutions are safe in the critically ill.

6.4 Micronutrients

  • Absorption is compromised in GI dysfunction → enteral micronutrient bioavailability is uncertain, especially early in acute disease.
  • Rational strategy: IV combination of moderate-dose micronutrients plus thiamine 100–200 mg/day.
  • Most frequent deficiencies: zinc, vitamin B12, vitamin D. In chronic intestinal disorders also check B7, A, E, K, copper and iron with blood levels.
  • After surgical bypass of the duodenum (bariatric or other), vitamin B12 may need lifelong therapeutic administration.
7 · Specific clinical problems

7.1 Constipation

  • Definition: no passing of stools for >4 days. More common in enterally fed patients; frequently under-diagnosed.
  • Consequences: increases risk of abdominal compartment syndrome; adverse effects on other organs, particularly the lung.
  • Risk factors: dehydration, motility-reducing drugs (opioids, myorelaxants, antihypertensives, iron and calcium supplements), bed rest.
  • Treatment: laxatives and enema; neostigmine IV or SC for the most persistent paralytic ileus; dietary fibre for prevention.

7.2 Diarrhoea

  • A burden on nursing workload and a risk to the patient (skin integrity, malabsorption).
  • Main cause: antibiotics (>70% of ICU patients).
  • Prevention: fibre-containing enteral solutions.
  • Mechanisms across inflammatory, infectious and secretory forms: altered ion/solute transporters and activation of cyclic nucleotide and Ca²⁺ signalling pathways.
  • Management has changed little in 30 years: replace fluid and electrolyte losses (oral or IV); cautious use of motility-reducing drugs (µ-opioid agonists) or antisecretory agents; targeted antibiotics for enteric infection; probiotics prevent or shorten diarrhoea.
  • Antimotility drugs are limited by concerns of ileus, ischaemic colitis and bacterial overgrowth.

7.3 Short bowel syndrome & high-output stoma

  • HOS = stoma output >2000 mL/day. Complications: dehydration, electrolyte loss (sodium, magnesium), undernutrition.
  • Absorption of most nutrients occurs in the first 100 cm of jejunum; B12 and bile salts in the last 100 cm of ileum; magnesium in terminal ileum and proximal colon.
  • Treatment: oral fluid restriction (isotonic drinks 500–1000 mL/day) + IV hydration (0.9% NaCl, 1:1 replacement above 1500 mL/24 h) and KCl; antimotility drugs (loperamide, codeine phosphate); antisecretory drugs (PPI, octreotide); correct magnesium, vitamins B12/A/D/E/K and zinc; cover needs with combined EN + PN.
  • Add cholestyramine if fat malabsorption, steatorrhoea or pruritic bilious output.
  • Prevent calcium bilirubinate gallstones: maintain enteral feeding, limit fasting, limit narcotics and anticholinergics.
  • Multidisciplinary approach including psychological support is essential.

7.4 Chylous leaks

  • Definition: triglyceride concentration >120 mg/dL (1.35 mmol/L) in pleural fluid or ascites; cholesterol <200 mg/dL (5.2 mmol/L) in pleural fluid also indicative.
  • Causes: traumatic (post-oesophagectomy, major abdominal surgery) or obstructive (tuberculosis, malignancy, cirrhosis) disruption of the lymphatic system.
  • Nutritional therapy: high-protein, low-fat with medium-chain triglycerides (MCT) to reduce chyle production; total PN with MCT may be required. Progressive reintroduction with a semi-elemental MCT diet, then oral diet once chyle production is markedly reduced.

7.5 When to rest the bowel, when to feed

Bowel rest versus early feeding
SituationApproachNotes
Obstruction, perforation, bowel ischaemia Nil per intestine — absolute Start PN until the problem is resolved.
Acute mesenteric ischaemia Stop EN during the episode and until vessels are recanalised After revascularisation, a RCT in 183 patients found EN within the first week was advantageous: less prolonged PN, fewer infections, fewer respiratory complications, earlier restoration of bowel continuity after resection.
Bowel necrosis during jejunal feeding Prompt resection + bowel rest for a few days Can occur without classic risk factors for enteric ischaemia — presents as nonspecific symptoms, severe shock, then multiorgan failure.
Severe diarrhoea / C. difficile, active inflammatory bowel disease Bowel rest — relative Combine PN + EN as needed. Evidence for bowel rest in severe acute colitis is weak (reduced stool weight only; no effect on surgery or other outcomes).
Chylous leak Bowel rest of fat — relative Temporarily suppress enteral fat; use MCT with amino acids and glucose, or PN.
Surgical / ERAS patients No restriction — feed early Bowel rest has a very limited place; use the gut as early as possible, with oral nutrition supplements if needed. Same applies to intubated medical or surgical patients.
8 · Pathophysiological mechanisms

8.1 The gut in multiple organ dysfunction

  • Animal models: altered microbiome during critical illness is associated with loss of intestinal barrier → translocation of bacterial products across the mucosa → further inflammation → remote organ dysfunction.
  • Acute lung injury can follow release of gut-derived products into lymphatics and/or directly into the lungs — shown in murine models and in humans. Ligation of the mesenteric lymph duct prevents lung injury in animals.
  • Intestinal dysbiosis may also lead to hepatic impairment.
  • Clinically, translocation-induced inflammation triggers fluids and vasopressors → fluid-induced tissue oedema and mesenteric vasoconstriction amplify gut injury and may lead to NOMI.

8.2 Microbiome

  • Critical illness causes a rapid dysbiosis: loss of diversity, loss of site specificity, and dominance of pathogens compared with healthy controls.
  • Not only antibiotics — many other common ICU drugs interfere with the gut microbiome.
  • Presence of specific GI pathogens at ICU admission is associated with increased risk of death or all-cause infection; rectal carriage of common ICU pathogens may predict specific infections.
  • Selective decontamination of the digestive tract (SDD) causes a strong deviation from healthy microbiota; recolonisation with resistant bacteria may occur after discharge and cessation.
  • In ARDS, the lung microbiome is enriched with gut bacteria.
  • Endogenous bacteria may play a beneficial role in acute illness. The shift to a "disease-promoting microbiome" / "pathobiome" drives pro-inflammatory downstream events, increased tight-junction permeability and mucus disintegration — both cause and consequence of GI injury and MODS.

8.3 Mucosal integrity

  • Intestinal epithelial cells (enterocytes, goblet cells, Paneth cells, enteroendocrine cells, M cells, epithelial stem cells) are the central regulators of barrier function and immune homeostasis.
  • First line of defence: epithelial-derived mucins, antimicrobial peptides and IgA. Physical border: the tight epithelial lining.
  • Loss of integrity → translocation of (patho)antigens → activation of intestinal macrophages and recruitment of intestinal T lymphocytes (CD4⁺ α4β7⁺ CCR9⁺) → release of TNF, IL-1β, IL-10, reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide → further barrier failure and impaired motility via disruption of tight junctions and smooth muscle contractile elements.

8.4 GI hormones & bile acids

  • Hormones: early phase shows decreased orexigenic hormones (e.g. ghrelin) and increased anorexigenic hormones (e.g. PYY). No direct correlation with GI function has been reported; none is used clinically as a monitor.
  • Bile acids: intrahepatic cholestasis of the critically ill reflects altered hepatocellular bile acid signalling and transport, occurring early after ICU admission. In malabsorption, reduced bile acid reabsorption removes negative feedback on hepatic synthesis. Increased circulating bile acids are associated with adverse outcome and are a candidate marker of malabsorption (gut–liver axis).

8.5 Bowel oedema & distension

  • Oedema arises from inflammation and capillary leak, fluid resuscitation and increased venous pressure. It can cause endotoxaemia, impair intestinal motility and impair healing of anastomoses. Oedema-induced cell stretch and cytoskeletal change may explain the dysmotility.
  • With raised mesenteric venous pressure (right heart failure, mesenteric hypervolaemia, raised IAP), increased lymphatic drainage is required; when proteins are washed out, interstitial oncotic pressure rises, lymphatic flow is impaired, and oedema worsens.
  • Distension = expansion through increased intraluminal pressure (bloating, pain). Can lead to perforation, increased bacterial translocation and stimulation of MODS.
9 · Remaining uncertainties & research priorities

9.1 Six topics prioritised for a formal consensus process

  1. Feeding intolerance — a single agreed definition.
  2. Core set of daily monitoring of GI function (harmonised symptom definitions).
  3. Core outcome set (COS) for studies of GI (dys)function.
  4. Protocol for abdominal ultrasound to assess GI function (with radiologists and gastroenterologists).
  5. Descriptive definition of NOMI (with radiologists, gastroenterologists and surgeons).
  6. Reference methods to measure gastric emptying, nutrient absorption and barrier dysfunction in ICU studies.
Caveat In the light of current poor evidence, these consensus definitions will likely need to be adapted as new evidence emerges — but they are crucial to produce that evidence.

9.3 Other open questions worth knowing

  • Can measurement of GRV identify delayed gastric emptying, and does monitoring it avoid complications?
  • Which biomarker(s) could guide decisions on EN, or on NOMI?
  • How to quantify bowel oedema; how to distinguish pathological from physiological mucosal permeability.
  • Which GI symptoms identify patients who would benefit from IAP monitoring?
  • Could a specific fluid-resuscitation strategy reduce the prevalence and severity of GI dysfunction?
  • Could high-normal potassium and magnesium improve GI motility?
  • Could early mobilisation, or a particular sedation strategy, improve GI function?
  • Optimal timing/duration of trophic EN (to feed mucosa and microbiome) versus full feeding (to match energy expenditure).
  • Best sampling method for microbiome studies (stool vs rectal swab).
  • Does bowel oedema drive dysmotility, or dysmotility drive oedema?

9.4 Limitations of the evidence base

  • Searches limited to English; interpretation is a consensus summary.
  • The list of study proposals is not exhaustive; individual academic or industry bias may persist despite consensus methods.
  • Voting emphasised feasibility — which may change with methodological advances.
  • All voting members were clinicians/researchers; patients may prioritise differently.
