1 · Foundations

The three pillars of a valid consent

Consent may be implied, verbal, non-verbal or written. A signature on a form is not the same as valid consent — all three pillars below must be satisfied.

Pillar 1Has mental capacity for this decision
+
Pillar 2Is informed (options, benefits, material risks)
+
Pillar 3Consents freely & voluntarily
=
ResultValid consent
Consequence: Failure to obtain valid consent can result in disciplinary proceedings by the MMC against the registered medical practitioner (RMP).
Guideline §3.1 · §3.1.3 · Preamble
What counts as "treatment"?
  • Assessment of history, symptoms and signs.
  • Examination and, where necessary, diagnostic investigations.
  • Management of the patient.
  • Action upon evidence suggesting a condition requiring urgent medical intervention.
  • Referral to appropriate professional colleagues where circumstances warrant.
Anaesthesia note: Anaesthesia, sedation, regional blocks, blood products and pain procedures all fall under "management" — each requires its own valid consent.
Guideline §2 (Treatment), referencing MMC Code of Professional Conduct 2019, §1.1

2 · Mental Capacity

The four abilities — assess all four
Step 1Receive the information
→
Step 2Understand and weigh it
→
Step 3Make a decision from it
→
Step 4Communicate the decision

All four must be present for the patient to be deemed to possess mental capacity.

Guideline §3.1.1
Key capacity rules
  • Decision-specific and time-specific. Capacity is not global — a patient may be able to consent to a cannula but not to a complex high-risk operation, and may fluctuate.
  • All RMPs must consider capacity before taking consent.
  • If in doubt, refer for a formal mental capacity assessment for that particular decision — performed by an RMP suitably trained in capacity assessment.
  • Where capacity appears impaired, first attempt supported decision-making (trusted friends, family, professionals chosen by the patient) to help the patient make an autonomous decision.
Anaesthesia note: Post-op delirium, sedation hangover, head injury, hypoxia and hypothermia all impair capacity. Re-assess before accepting a refusal or a consent given in these states.
Guideline §3.1.1.1–3.1.1.3

3 · Information & Material Risk

What must be disclosed
  • The patient's diagnosis and current condition.
  • Investigation and/or treatment options, including alternatives (including the option of doing nothing).
  • Benefits and risks of each option — including material risks, possible adverse effects or complications.
  • Per the FAQs: also the consequences of not proceeding with treatment, and alternative treatment options available.
Guideline §3.1.2.1 · FAQ 1
The material risk test (two limbs)

A risk is material if either limb applies:

Limb 1 · ObjectiveA reasonable person in the patient's position, if warned of the risk, would be likely to attach significance to it in the circumstances of the particular case.
OR
Limb 2 · SubjectiveThe RMP is, or should reasonably be aware, that this particular patient, if warned, would be likely to attach significance to it.
Anaesthesia note: For a professional singer, hoarseness or vocal cord injury after intubation is likely material even if statistically rare. Ask about occupation, hobbies, anxieties and prior anaesthetic experiences.
Guideline §2 (Material Risk) · FAQ 1
How to communicate the information
  • Communicate in a manner that is understandable to that person.
  • Verify understanding and allow the opportunity to seek further explanation and clarification.
  • Use visual aids, charts, models or other accessories — especially for complicated procedures.
  • Prepared brochures/information sheets may stimulate discussion, but must not substitute for informing the patient and ensuring understanding. They must be current, accurate and relevant.
  • Additional risk information may be provided in a written explanatory document appended to the consent form.
  • Where necessary, engage a translator to facilitate communication.
  • Therapeutic privilege: an RMP may ethically withhold information if non-disclosure is believed to be in the patient's best interests — the reasons must be documented in the medical records.
Guideline §3.1.2.2–3.1.2.7

4 · The Consent Process & Documentation

Consent is a process, not an event

Consent is an ongoing process throughout the course of treatment — not a one-off event.

Document in the medical records:

  • Who was involved in the process.
  • The information provided.
  • Questions asked (and answers given).
  • Any additional information or explanatory notes.

It is advisable to have a witness attest to the process.

Witness role (FAQ 7): should be another healthcare professional (RMP, nurse, allied health professional) — preferably not directly involved in the patient's management, and not related to the patient or the RMP. The witness must be capable and available to attest to the consent process should the need arise.
Guideline §3.2 · FAQ 7
Re-consent triggers

Re-obtain consent where there is a change in the nature, clinical course or presentation of the illness for which consent was originally obtained — these changes must be discussed with the patient.

5 · Exceptions & Emergency

The only two exceptions to prior consent
  • A medical emergency — an injury or illness that is acute and poses an immediate risk to a person's life or long-term health.
  • Treatment required by law.
Use sparingly. The emergency exception undermines patient autonomy. Any non-consensual treatment must be limited to the procedures required to address the immediate necessity — nothing more.

6 · Minors

Assessing a minor's capacity
  • Younger children are generally not competent to provide consent.
  • Whether a minor has the mental capacity to give valid consent depends on whether he/she has sufficient maturity and intelligence to understand the nature and implications of the proposed treatment.
  • This is decided case-by-case by the treating RMP.
  • RMPs should, as far as possible, respect the decisions of mature minors, while being mindful of legal provisions requiring parental consent.
Anaesthesia note: A 15-year-old who understands and can weigh the risks of a general anaesthetic may be able to assent/consent — but local law may still require parental consent. When in doubt, seek ethics or legal advice and document.
Guideline §3.6 · §3.7
Parental consent — one parent usually enough
  • In general, consent of either parent is sufficient — each parent has full responsibility for their children under 18, unaffected by changes to spousal relationships (e.g. separation).
  • Exceptions: a court order to the contrary, or an apparent dispute between the parents.
  • Handle disputes by counselling the parents towards consensus on the child's best interests, or seeking an ethics consult where available.
  • If there is any doubt about who may provide consent, obtain legal advice.
FAQ 3
Accompanying adult who is not a parent
  • Emergency: follow the emergency pathway (FAQ 2).
  • Non-emergency: ascertain the adult's relationship to the patient and their authority to provide consent as a surrogate decision-maker.
Invalid: A blanket consent form signed by parents allowing any third party to authorise treatment on their behalf is not valid consent.
FAQ 4 · Guideline §3.6–3.7
Patients with mental disorders
  • Patients with mental disorders who have mental capacity must provide consent for any treatment themselves.
  • For mentally disordered patients without capacity requiring surgery, electroconvulsive therapy, or recruitment into clinical trials, consent is governed by Section 77 of the Mental Health Act 2001.
FAQ 5 · Mental Health Act 2001 §77

7 · Adults Without Mental Capacity

Finding a surrogate decision-maker (in order)

Impairments to reasoning and judgment from medical conditions may be permanent or transient. Where the patient cannot give informed consent, identify a surrogate:

1stA person with legal authority to make decisions for the patient
→
2ndA person the patient identified or nominated as surrogate
→
3rdAn appropriate surrogate who knows the patient's values and preferences
→
Last resortRMPs decide in the best interests of the patient
Before deciding alone: steps must first be taken to attempt to identify a suitable surrogate, and these efforts must be documented in the medical records.
Guideline §3.8 · §3.8.1–3.8.3
Best interests — how to assess

All treatment decisions for a patient without mental capacity must be made in the best interests of that particular patient.

An assessment of best interests includes what is clinically indicated, plus:

  • The views of the patient, so far as they can express them — including previously expressed preferences.
  • The cultural, religious or other beliefs and values of the patient.
  • The views of others involved in providing care to the patient.

The list is not exhaustive; weight depends on circumstances. Do not make unjustified assumptions based on irrelevant or discriminatory factors — behaviour, appearance or disability.

Guideline §3.9 · FAQ 6

8 · Advance Decisions & Refusal

Advance decisions — four validity checks

Where a patient has made advance decisions or expressed wishes while they had mental capacity, consider:

Check 1Are they sufficiently clear and specific to apply to the clinical circumstances now arisen?
Check 2Does the decision remain current?
Check 3Any reason to doubt the patient's capacity at the time the decision was made?
Check 4Was there any undue pressure on the patient to make the decision?
Doubts, especially in an emergency: proceed in the patient's best interests while attempting to determine the validity of the advance decision. Such decisions must be clearly documented in the medical records.
Unequivocal written directive: an RMP should refrain from providing treatment where there is an unequivocal written directive by the patient that such treatment is not to be provided in the circumstances that now apply.
Guideline §3.10 · §3.11 · §3.12
Refusal of treatment
  • Generally, any patient with mental capacity can refuse consent for any treatment without giving reasons.
  • The refusal, and where possible the specific details discussed, should be recorded in the medical record or by signing a refusal of treatment form.
  • A patient also has the right to withdraw consent at any time prior to treatment, without assigning any reasons.
Anaesthesia note: A patient may withdraw consent on the operating table. Once withdrawn, stop and re-discuss — do not proceed on the original consent.
Guideline §3.13 · §3.14 · §3.1.3.1
When a surrogate refuses against best interests

If the surrogate decision-maker of an incapacitated patient refuses consent for treatment, and the decision is deemed not to be in the best interests of the patient, the RMP should:

  • Seek an ethical consult, and/or
  • Seek legal support — which may include a court decision.
Guideline §3.15

9 · Practical Rules & Validity

Who is responsible for taking consent?
  • The RMP providing the treatment is ultimately responsible for ensuring prior valid consent was obtained.
  • Where possible, consent should be obtained personally by the treating RMP.
  • All RMPs who take consent must possess the necessary competence, communication skills, experience and knowledge.
  • This applies regardless of type of registration — including provisionally registered practitioners.
Delegation: If the treating RMP delegates consent-taking to another RMP (practical necessity or training), the treating RMP must be willing to take full responsibility for their actions. If the RMP taking/signing the form and the RMP providing treatment differ, final responsibility still rests with the treating RMP.
Guideline §3.16 · §3.17 · FAQ 8 · FAQ 9

FAQ 10 · Guideline §3.3
Periodic & serial treatment

Predetermined series (e.g. chemotherapy, radiotherapy regimen, course of psychotherapy):

  • Specific details (duration, interval, total number of episodes) must be explained before consent is taken for the whole series.
  • Consent remains valid as long as the patient's condition and the risks remain the same.
  • The patient's clinical condition should be evaluated prior to each episode.
  • Any change → discuss and re-obtain consent.

Chronic periodic treatment (e.g. regular haemodialysis, repeated de-sloughing of a wound, periodic blood transfusion):

  • Consent should be re-obtained prior to each treatment, as the patient's condition — and therefore the associated risks — may have changed.
FAQ 11
High-risk procedures and next-of-kin
  • There is no legal distinction between a "normal" consent and a "high-risk" consent, nor any additional ethical or legal requirement for another individual to sign.
  • For high-risk treatment, encourage the patient to discuss options with family, close friends or others they wish, to support their decision-making.
  • Document these conversations and the explanation of risks in the medical records.
Do not: require another individual to provide consent after a patient with mental capacity has given their own consent — this violates respect for the patient's autonomy.
FAQ 12
Additional purposes requiring separate consent

Consent is only applicable for the specific purposes for which it was obtained. Additional consent is needed for:

  • Photographs and audio-visual recordings taken during treatment.
  • Releasing patient data to a third party.
  • Keeping organs or tissues removed at surgery for purposes other than the original procedure.
Guideline §3.18
Sterilisation, hysterectomy & orchidectomy
  • Consent must be given by the patient concerned.
  • Discussions between spouses should be encouraged, but this does not and should not deny the patient's right to make the final decision and give consent.
Guideline §3.19
Artificial intelligence (AI) tools in treatment

If an RMP plans to use and rely on an AI tool and/or technology in treatment, the patient must be informed before treatment of:

  • How the AI tools/technology are being used in their clinical care.
  • The basis of the results or recommendations generated by the AI.
  • Any known risks associated with the AI tool/technology.
  • Safeguards in place to prevent errors and/or data breaches.
  • Any conflicts of interest the RMP has or may have in relation to the AI tool/technology.
  • That the RMP remains responsible and accountable for the patient's clinical outcomes.

The patient also retains the right to know when an AI tool/technology has failed and/or led to an adverse outcome.

Guideline §3.20 · FAQ 13
Blanket consent is invalid

Consent given in broad terms without relevant information and advice does not amount to an exercise of choice.

A "blanket" or universal consent for treatment obtained on admission — whether as an out-patient or in-patient — is not considered valid.
Guideline §3.21

10 · Anaesthesia-Specific Situations

Pre-op clinic → theatre: the re-confirmation rule
  • Consent taken in the anaesthetic or surgical clinic before scheduling must be re-confirmed with the patient on admission — both the consent itself and the details on the form.
  • If the patient was discharged home in the interim, or the list was postponed, treat the old consent as no longer valid.
  • Any new symptom, new investigation result or change in planned procedure requires a fresh discussion and re-consent.
Practical tip: Build a "consent reconfirmation" step into your WHO-style sign-in — verify consent, procedure, site and any change since the clinic visit.
FAQ 10 · Guideline §3.3
Regional & neuraxial blocks
  • Discuss alternatives — general anaesthesia, local infiltration, sedation, or doing nothing.
  • Discuss material risks relevant to that patient: bleeding/haematoma (especially with anticoagulation), nerve injury, infection, local anaesthetic systemic toxicity, inadequate block and conversion to GA.
  • Confirm the patient understands that conversion to GA may become necessary and what that entails.
  • Consent to a block is not consent to surgery, and vice versa — separate purposes need separate consent.
Guideline §3.1.2.1 · §3.18 · §2 (Material Risk)
Sedation
  • Sedation is a treatment in its own right — it requires its own informed consent, including risks of over-sedation, aspiration, respiratory depression and the possibility of conversion to GA.
  • Document the discussion separately from the procedural consent where possible.
Guideline §2 (Treatment) · §3.18
Obstetric anaesthesia
  • A labouring woman with mental capacity consents for herself — including for epidural analgesia and caesarean section anaesthesia.
  • Consent for epidural analgesia covers the specific episode and plan; if the clinical situation or risks change (e.g. prolonged labour, new thrombocytopenia, need for caesarean), re-discuss and re-consent.
  • She may withdraw consent at any time prior to treatment.
  • If she lacks capacity in an emergency, follow the emergency pathway — best interests, no unequivocal written refusal, and consensus of attending RMPs where practicable.
Guideline §3.1.3.1 · §3.3 · §3.8 · FAQ 2 · FAQ 11
Emergency surgery, trauma & the unconscious patient
  • Limit non-consensual treatment to what is required to address the immediate necessity — no elective extras.
  • Attempt to contact a surrogate where time permits.
  • Check for any unequivocal written directive refusing the proposed treatment before proceeding.
  • Document: the emergency, why consent could not be obtained, the attempts made to find a surrogate, and the best-interests reasoning.
Guideline §3.4 · §3.5 · §3.8.3 · §3.10 · FAQ 2
Blood products & transfusion
  • Transfusion is treatment — it requires informed consent, including material risks and alternatives.
  • A valid advance decision refusing blood must be respected where it is clear, specific, current, made with capacity and free of undue pressure.
  • If validity is in doubt, especially in an emergency, proceed in the patient's best interests while attempting to determine validity — and document clearly.
  • Refer also to other MMC guidelines and relevant laws for consent in this area.
Guideline §3.10–3.12 · §3.22–3.23
Withdrawal of consent in theatre
  • A patient may withdraw consent at any time prior to the treatment, without giving reasons.
  • Stop, re-discuss, and document the withdrawal — including the specific details discussed where possible.
  • A refusal should be recorded in the medical record or on a refusal of treatment form.
Guideline §3.1.3.1 · §3.13 · §3.14
Teaching, photography, recordings & data
  • Consent for anaesthesia does not cover photographs, audio-visual recordings, release of data to third parties, or retention of tissues/organs for other purposes.
  • Each of these requires additional consent.
Guideline §3.18
Other guidelines and laws still apply
  • Consent in other aspects of healthcare may be covered by other MMC guidelines — the RMP has a duty to refer to these when a relevant situation arises.
  • RMPs must also be cognisant of the relevant laws pertaining to consent for treatment.
Guideline §3.22 · §3.23

11 · Ten High-Yield Cases

Case 1 — Consent taken in clinic 6 weeks ago, now new symptoms

Scenario: A patient consented for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy in the surgical clinic. The list was postponed twice and the patient now reports new-onset chest pain and a productive cough.

Action: The original consent is no longer valid — the condition has changed and the procedure was postponed. Re-discuss the new findings and the anaesthetic risks, then re-obtain consent on admission.

FAQ 10 · Guideline §3.3
Case 2 — Unconscious trauma patient, no next-of-kin reachable

Scenario: A road-traffic victim with a ruptured spleen and head injury arrives intubated and haemodynamically unstable. No family can be contacted.

Action: Invoke the medical emergency exception. Check for any unequivocal written directive refusing treatment. Proceed in the patient's best interests, limited to what addresses the immediate threat to life. Seek consensus of attending RMPs where practicable, and document the attempts to identify a surrogate.

Guideline §3.4 · §3.5 · §3.8.3 · §3.10 · FAQ 2
Case 3 — Mature 15-year-old, parents unreachable, urgent appendicectomy

Scenario: A 15-year-old with a history and examination consistent with acute appendicitis is for urgent surgery. He understands the procedure and risks. Parents are overseas and unreachable for several hours.

Action: The minor's capacity depends on his maturity and intelligence to understand the nature and implications — assess and document this. Respect a mature minor's decision as far as possible, while being mindful of legal provisions requiring parental consent. If this becomes a medical emergency before parents are reachable, follow the emergency pathway and act in his best interests.

Guideline §3.6 · §3.7 · FAQ 2 · FAQ 3
Case 4 — Jehovah's Witness with a signed advance decision refusing blood

Scenario: A patient for major elective surgery produces a signed, dated advance decision refusing blood transfusion.

Action: Run the four validity checks — is it clear and specific to the current circumstances, still current, made with capacity, and free of undue pressure? If valid and unequivocal in writing, refrain from giving the refused treatment. Plan alternatives in advance, and document the discussion.

Guideline §3.10 · §3.11
Case 5 — Parents in dispute over elective paediatric surgery

Scenario: Mother consents to a tonsillectomy for her 7-year-old; the estranged father objects in writing. The surgery is elective and non-urgent.

Action: Consent from either parent is usually sufficient, but this is an exception — there is an apparent dispute. Counsel the parents towards consensus in the child's best interests, or seek an ethics consult. If doubt remains about who may consent, obtain legal advice. Do not proceed on the mother's consent alone until resolved.

FAQ 3
Case 6 — Patient withdraws consent on the operating table

Scenario: A patient consented to spinal anaesthesia for a procedure. After positioning, she becomes distressed and says she no longer wants the spinal.

Action: She may withdraw consent at any time prior to treatment, without giving reasons. Stop. Re-discuss alternatives (e.g. GA) and obtain fresh consent for the new plan. Record the withdrawal and the discussion.

Guideline §3.1.3.1 · §3.13 · §3.14
Case 7 — Dementia patient with a fractured neck of femur

Scenario: An 84-year-old with moderate dementia is confused and cannot weigh the risks of spinal versus GA. Her daughter, who holds legal authority, is present.

Action: Identify the daughter as the surrogate with legal authority and seek consent from her. Check whether the patient made any advance decision or expressed wishes while she had capacity. All decisions must be made in the patient's best interests, considering her views, beliefs and values.

Guideline §3.8 · §3.8.1 · §3.9 · §3.11 · FAQ 6
Case 8 — Next-of-kin demands to co-sign a high-risk consent

Scenario: A patient with severe aortic stenosis consents to a major operation. His wife insists on signing the consent form as well, believing it is required.

Action: There is no legal distinction between normal and high-risk consent, and no requirement for another individual to sign. Encourage the patient to discuss the options with his wife to support his decision-making, and document the risk explanation — but requiring her signature after he has consented violates his autonomy.

FAQ 12
Case 9 — Repeated sessions under GA (radiotherapy / ECT)

Scenario: A patient requires 20 fractions of radiotherapy under general anaesthesia.

Action: Consent may be taken for the whole predetermined series, provided the duration, interval and total number of episodes are explained beforehand. The consent remains valid as long as the condition and risks remain the same — evaluate the patient's clinical condition before each episode. Any change in the nature, clinical course or presentation requires a fresh discussion and re-consent.

FAQ 11 · Guideline §3.3
Case 10 — Provisionally registered MO asked to take anaesthetic consent

Scenario: A busy consultant asks a provisionally registered medical officer to obtain consent for anaesthesia for a complex case.

Action: Any RMP taking consent must have the necessary competence, communication skills, experience and knowledge. The treating RMP remains ultimately responsible for ensuring valid consent was obtained, and must be willing to take full responsibility for the delegate's actions. If the MO cannot adequately explain the material risks, the consultant must take the consent personally.
