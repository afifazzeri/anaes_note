Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS)

Quick reference for anaesthetists & intensivists — mechanism-based assessment and management

Clinical diagnosis · IASP criteria No specific diagnostic test Multidisciplinary care Mechanism-based treatment
1 At a glance
Definition — CRPS is a post-traumatic disorder characterised by nondermatomal, severe, continuous pain in the affected limb, associated with sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/oedema and motor/trophic disturbances. The pain is regionally restricted (cannot be related to a specific dermatome) and disproportionate to the inciting event.
  • Clinical diagnosis. Use the IASP clinical diagnostic criteria. There is no diagnostic test specific for CRPS.
  • Multifactorial pathophysiology. Recent studies point to CRPS being an exaggerated inflammatory response to trauma or surgery, with both peripheral and central mechanisms.
  • Multidisciplinary treatment. Adequate pain management + physiotherapy + psychological evaluation and intervention.
  • Emerging paradigm. Expect a shift from symptomatic to mechanism-based treatment.
  • Incidence 5.5–26.2 per 100 000 person-years. Women affected 3–4× more often than men; highest incidence in women aged 61–70 yr.
  • Triggers. Usually trauma (mostly fractures) or surgery. Upper extremity affected more often than lower. Usually limited to one extremity.
  • Types. Type I = no demonstrable nerve lesion; Type II = demonstrable nerve lesion. They do not differ in clinical presentation or treatment choice — "CRPS" is used as a general term for both.
  • Impact. Can severely reduce quality of life and lead to substantial physical and social disability — early recognition matters.
2 Clinical presentation

Patients typically present with severe, continuous pain in a glove- or stocking-like distribution, usually preceded by injury or surgery. Signs and symptoms can vary over the course of the disease.

Phenotypes

  • Patients are often described as having warm, intermediate or cold CRPS, based on reported and measured skin temperature differences between the affected and contralateral limb.
  • Signs and symptoms may reflect the underlying pathophysiological mechanism (inflammation, pain/sensory disturbance, vasomotor disturbance, motor disturbance, psychological disturbance).
  • It is important to recognise which mechanism is most prominent, so that therapy can be targeted at it (see section 5).
  • Hypothesis of the authors: in the majority of patients — especially those with warm (acute) CRPS — inflammation is the most prominent mechanism; other mechanisms result from ongoing inflammation. Over time, inflammation may disappear in some patients, leaving different forms of rest damage.
3 Diagnosis

There is no gold standard for the diagnosis of CRPS. History and physical examination are the cornerstones of appropriate diagnosis and management.

The most commonly used criteria are the new IASP clinical diagnostic criteria (Budapest criteria), which are based on observed and patient-reported signs and symptoms.

IASP clinical diagnostic criteria

# Criterion
1 Continuing pain, which is disproportionate to any inciting event.
2 Must report at least one symptom in three of the four following categories: sensory · vasomotor · sudomotor/oedema · motor/trophic.
3 Must display at least one sign at time of evaluation in two or more of the following categories: sensory · vasomotor · sudomotor/oedema · motor/trophic.
4 There is no other diagnosis that better explains the signs and symptoms.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis is extensive and can be summarised into five categories. Most have similar presentations, which occasionally makes the diagnosis of CRPS a challenge.

Category Examples
Neuropathic pain-like syndromes Peripheral (poly)neuropathy · nerve entrapment · radiculopathy · post-herpetic neuralgia · de-afferentation pain post-cerebrovascular accident · plexopathy · motor neuron disease
Vascular diseases Thrombosis · acrocyanosis · atherosclerosis · Raynaud's phenomenon · erythromelalgia
Inflammation Erysipelas · inflammation not otherwise specified · bursitis · seronegative arthritis · rheumatological diseases
Myofascial pain syndromes Overuse · disuse · tennis elbow · repetitive strain injury · fibromyalgia
Psychiatric problems Somatoform pain disorders · Münchhausen syndrome

Role of additional tests

  • As the pathophysiology is still not completely understood, there is limited use for additional clinical and laboratory tests in diagnosing CRPS.
  • Tests may be used to exclude other disorders that could explain the signs and symptoms, or to monitor CRPS signs and symptoms.
  • Example: quantitative sensory testing — mostly used in research settings to quantify sensory disturbances found on physical examination.
4 Pathophysiology (brief)

The exact pathophysiology is still unknown. Both peripheral and central mechanisms are thought to play a role in the initiation and maintenance of CRPS. The disease appears to be multifactorial with a multi-mechanism pathophysiology, requiring a multimodal workup and treatment.

Exaggerated inflammation

Most prominent in warm/acute CRPS

  • General markers (CRP, WBC, IL-6, ESR) are usually not elevated in plasma.
  • Yet "classic signs of inflammation" are often displayed: pain, redness, increased temperature, swelling, loss of function.
  • Higher pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) in blister fluid of the affected limb; also elevated in serum, plasma and CSF.
  • Pro-inflammatory cytokines can activate and sensitise peripheral nociceptors → pain and hyperalgesia.

Neurogenic inflammation

Explains oedema, vasodilation, sweating

  • Increased CGRP and substance P in serum vs healthy controls.
  • CGRP → neurogenic dilatation of arterioles.
  • Substance P → plasma protein extravasation.
  • Together these could explain redness and swelling.

Autoimmune hypothesis

Suggestive but unproven

  • Passive transfer of CRPS patient serum IgG induces behavioural changes in mice.
  • CRPS serum stains rodent sympathetic ganglia.
  • Small group experienced pain relief with low-dose IV immunoglobulin.
  • More positive anti-nuclear antibody tests than healthy blood-bank donors.
  • Caveat: Witebsky's criteria are not fulfilled — CRPS may be more an autoimmune condition than an autoimmune disease.

Deep-tissue ischaemia–reperfusion

Tested in a chronic post-ischaemia pain model

  • Proposes deep-tissue ischaemia and inflammation caused by microvascular ischaemia–reperfusion injury.
  • Could explain abnormal pain sensations such as allodynia.

Genetics

Predisposition

  • HLA-DQ1 frequency significantly higher in CRPS patients than controls (Dutch cohort).
  • HLA-B62 and HLA-DQ8 associated with CRPS with fixed dystonia.
  • HLA-DR13 associated with multifocal/generalised tonic dystonia of CRPS.
  • Certain HLA loci may be involved in susceptibility to certain phenotypes.

Cortical reorganisation

Central mechanism

  • Occurs in primary somatosensory cortex (S1) and motor cortex.
  • Changes in S1 correlate with pain intensity and mechanical hyperalgesia.
  • Reversal of S1 reorganisation correlates with pain reduction.
  • Could explain the glove-/stocking-like spread of pain and hyperalgesia rather than nerve-territory distribution.

Small-fibre neuropathy

Similarity to small-fibre-predominant polyneuropathies

  • Decrease in epidermal nerve fibres and in sweat gland and vascular innervation.
  • Could explain neuropathic pain, trophic and vasomotor dysfunction.
  • Antidromic release of CGRP and substance P by these small fibres in response to trauma/inflammation.
  • Unclear whether small-fibre loss is a result or a cause of CRPS.

Psychological factors

Not implicated in onset

  • Most studies show no association between onset of CRPS and depression, anxiety, paranoia, hostility/anger.
  • Some evidence for the influence of stressful life events before onset.
  • These factors may still play a role in maintenance of the disease.
5 Treatment — mechanism-based algorithm

Effective treatment options in CRPS are limited and consist of both non-invasive and invasive therapies. Start with active physical therapy / physiotherapy — it has been shown to reduce pain and improve function. Medication is added to physiotherapy, and the choice of medication should be based on the mechanism deemed most prominent in that specific case.

Step 1 Confirm the diagnosis IASP clinical criteria · exclude the differential diagnosis
▼
Step 2 · in all patients Active physical rehabilitation / physiotherapy Reduces pain and improves function — the foundation of treatment
▼
Step 3 Identify the most prominent pathophysiological mechanism Target therapy at that mechanism

Inflammation

Warm, oedematous, acute CRPS

  • Free-radical scavengers (DMSO, acetylcysteine)*
  • Bisphosphonates — strong evidence
  • Other immunomodulators — conflicting evidence

Pain / sensory disturbance

Allodynia, hyperalgesia, spontaneous pain

  • Nociceptive: WHO analgesic ladder — bar strong opioids
  • Neuropathic: gabapentin
  • Intractable, long-standing: low-dose IV ketamine — monitor LFTs

Vasomotor disturbance

"Cold" CRPS

  • Calcium channel blocker
  • Alpha-sympathetic blocker
  • PDE-5 inhibitor
  • Short-term trial — stop if no effect

Motor disturbance

Tremor, dystonia, weakness

  • Oral muscle relaxants first
  • Intrathecal baclofen for dystonia — likely effective, but invasive with side effects

Psychological disturbance

Chronic pain behaviour, poor coping

  • Refer to a multidisciplinary team including a psychologist
▼ if insufficient, despite adequate treatment of the underlying mechanism
Step 4 · invasive options
  • Sympathetic blocks (stellate ganglion, thoracic sympathetic, lumbar sympathetic): negative recommendation in the EBGD guidelines on CRPS type I (updated 2014).
  • Spinal cord stimulation (SCS): may be considered if patients do not respond to pharmacological treatments or rehabilitation therapies. RCT evidence for effect on (neuropathic) pain and health-related quality of life. Currently the only therapy with a multi-mechanism action in CRPS — positive effect on both the somatosensory system and vasomotor disturbances.

* Free-radical scavengers (dimethyl sulphoxide or acetylcysteine) are advised in the Netherlands for inflammatory symptoms, but have not gained general international acceptance.

6 Prevention

As treatment options for CRPS are limited, prevention would be the best medicine.

  • Supplementation with vitamin C > 500 mg day−1, initiated immediately after injury or surgery and continued for 45–50 days, has been shown to reduce the risk of developing CRPS.
7 Prognosis & social impact
  • The prognosis and outcome of CRPS remain difficult to predict.
  • Resolution rates: ~74% in the 1st year, falling to ~36% after 6 years.
  • Social impact is significant. In one CRPS population study:
    • 31% permanent inability to work
    • 28% partial inability to work (i.e. requiring work adaptations)
8 Anaesthetist / intensivist checklist

Practical points drawn from the clinical content above — for use at the bedside and in the perioperative period.

  • Think of CRPS in any patient with disproportionately severe, continuous, non-dermatomal limb pain after trauma, fracture or surgery — especially if sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/oedema or motor/trophic changes coexist.
  • Document objectively: temperature and colour asymmetry vs the contralateral limb, sweating asymmetry, oedema, range of motion, tremor/dystonia, hair/nail/skin changes, allodynia/hyperalgesia.
  • Use the IASP criteria — ≥1 symptom in 3 of 4 categories and ≥1 sign in ≥2 of 4 categories, with no better alternative diagnosis.
  • Exclude mimics before labelling: neuropathic, vascular, inflammatory, myofascial and psychiatric causes.
  • Do not rely on bloods or imaging to make the diagnosis — general inflammatory markers are usually normal.
  • Start physiotherapy early and refer to a multidisciplinary team (pain physician, physiotherapist, psychologist).
  • Avoid strong opioids for nociceptive pain; use the WHO ladder. Gabapentin for a neuropathic component.
  • Ketamine infusions (low-dose IV, long-standing/intractable CRPS): dose and duration unclear — monitor LFTs frequently and stop immediately if liver enzymes rise.
  • Sympathetic blocks are not recommended by the EBGD guidelines; spinal cord stimulation is the invasive option of choice for refractory patients.
  • Consider vitamin C prophylaxis (>500 mg day−1 for 45–50 days) after relevant injury or surgery.
