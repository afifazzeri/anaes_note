Antimicrobial stewardship essentials

  • Document the indication — specify the clinical diagnosis (pneumonia, UTI, AGE, line-related BSI, meningitis).
  • Cultures before antibiotics — obtain appropriate cultures based on the diagnosis and site.
  • Review at 72 hours and document. Streamline at 48–72 h to narrowest effective agent based on susceptibility.
  • Source control — always consider drainage/debridement/removal of infected devices.
  • Monotherapy preferred unless combination therapy is proven necessary for efficacy, synergy, or resistance suppression.
  • Distinguish infection from colonisation/contamination — critically evaluate microbiology alongside clinical presentation.

IV → PO conversion

Optimal window: 48–96 hours of IV therapy (allows review of microbiology and clinical response).

Criteria — all must be met:

  • Signs of clinical improvement
  • Able to tolerate oral therapy
  • Good compliance to oral therapy
  • No condition requiring higher tissue concentrations of antibiotic

A AmpC β-Lactamase-Producing Enterobacterales Enterobacter cloacae complex · Klebsiella aerogenes · Citrobacter freundii ▶

Key microbiology

  • AmpC = Ambler class C β-lactamase. Hydrolyses multiple β-lactams except cefepime and carbapenems.
  • Inducible chromosomal expression: isolates initially appear susceptible to ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime — but resistance can be induced after a few doses.
  • No national surveillance programme for AmpC in Malaysia.

Risk of clinically significant inducible AmpC

Moderate–high risk (~20%) Enterobacter cloacae complex · Klebsiella aerogenes · Citrobacter freundii

Lower risk (<5%) Serratia marcescens · Providencia spp. · Morganella morganii

E ESBL-Producing Enterobacterales E. coli · Klebsiella pneumoniae · Proteus mirabilis ▶

Key microbiology

  • Plasmid-mediated β-lactamases that hydrolyse penicillin, 3rd- and 4th-generation cephalosporins and monobactams — but not carbapenems or cephamycins.
  • NARSS 2022: almost a quarter of E. coli and K. pneumoniae clinical isolates from Malaysian healthcare settings are resistant to 3rd-generation cephalosporins.
  • High prevalence of ESBL genes reported among Malaysian K. pneumoniae isolates (blaSHV, blaTEM, blaCTX-M-1).

C Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) NDM-dominant in Malaysia · CAZ-AVI ± aztreonam · cefiderocol ▶

Key microbiology

  • CRE = intermediate (I) or resistant (R) to one or more carbapenems.
  • Proteus spp., Morganella spp. and Providencia spp. have intrinsic elevated imipenem MIC — use meropenem, doripenem or ertapenem results to determine CRE status for these organisms.
  • Predominant CRE: Klebsiella pneumoniae > Enterobacter spp. > E. coli. Meropenem resistance in K. pneumoniae rose from 2.3% (2018) to 5.0% (2022).
  • Carbapenemase gene distribution in Malaysia (IMR, 2016–2021, >10,000 isolates):

NDM 83.6% OXA-48 10.8% IMP 1.5% KPC 0.3% VIM 0.3%

CP-CRE vs non-CP-CRE

TypeDefinition / notes
CP-CRE Produces carbapenemase enzyme(s). Detectable by phenotypic tests (mCIM) or molecular tests (PCR). Targeted for intensive IPC efforts.
Non-CP-CRE No carbapenemase production; resistance via porin modification and efflux pumps. Intrinsic resistance to a specific carbapenem is NOT considered carbapenem resistance (e.g. Proteus mirabilis intrinsically resistant to imipenem but susceptible to other carbapenems is not CRE).

Combination therapy — companion drug selection

In moderate-to-severe infection (high-burden), combination therapy is preferred when using tigecycline, polymyxins or cefiderocol. Choose the companion based on susceptibility testing:

  • IV aminoglycosides (once-daily dosing)
  • IV meropenem (if MIC ≤8 mg/L — highest dose with extended infusion over 3–4 hours)
  • IV tigecycline or polymyxins or cefiderocol

Referral to an ID physician / clinical microbiologist is recommended.

Avoid Tigecycline should be avoided in bacteraemia (low blood levels due to extensive tissue distribution).

Clinical pearls

Cefiderocol Most literature recommends monotherapy. However, emerging cefiderocol resistance is associated with NDM-type metallo-β-lactamase producers.
Polymyxins Did not demonstrate better clinical success and were associated with worse side effects (e.g. kidney injury) compared with novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors such as ceftazidime-avibactam. However, polymyxin remains the drug of choice where newer antibiotics are not available.
ESCMID guidance
  • Polymyxins as monotherapy only in non-severe / low-risk CRE infection, when susceptibility is demonstrated.
  • Otherwise, combination therapy with more than one in-vitro active drug (polymyxin, aminoglycoside, tigecycline or fosfomycin) — no specific combination is recommended.
  • May consider combination with meropenem if meropenem MIC ≤8 mg/L (high-dose extended infusion).
Combination data Polymyxin B + tigecycline: rapid and sustained bactericidal killing up to 120 h — promising for carbapenem-resistant E. coli.
Polymyxin B + IV fosfomycin: rapid bacterial killing in KPC-producing CRE.
Tigecycline: combination with polymyxin B assists rapid and sustained bacterial killing.

Paediatric considerations: options similar to adults. Safety and efficacy of IV ceftolozane-tazobactam and IV cefiderocol in children is not established. Tigecycline not recommended in children unless no alternative. Oral fosfomycin not used routinely <12 years. Paediatric ID consult advisable.

B Carbapenem-Resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) High-dose sulbactam backbone · mostly colonisers — don't treat ▶
Critical A. baumannii isolated from clinical specimens are mostly colonisers. Clinical correlation is essential to avoid unnecessary antibiotic therapy. If coloniser — DO NOT treat.

Key microbiology

  • Ubiquitous Gram-negative coccobacillus; significant cause of nosocomial infection. Fimbriae enable attachment and biofilm formation; under dry conditions it develops thicker cell walls, surviving long in the environment.
  • Nationwide carbapenem resistance rate has steadily ranged around 50–60% (2009–2022), mainly due to OXA-23 carbapenemase.
  • International clone II predominates among CRAB isolates in Malaysia.
  • Environmental contamination, healthcare workers' hands and patient isolates show identical resistance patterns/PFGE profiles — indicating a possible ICU transmission route.

MDR A. baumannii definition

Isolates testing I or R to at least 1 drug in at least 3 of these categories:

  • Extended-spectrum cephalosporins (ceftazidime, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, cefepime)
  • Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin)
  • Aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin, tobramycin)
  • Carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, doripenem)
  • β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (piperacillin; piperacillin-tazobactam)
  • Ampicillin/sulbactam

Colistin vs polymyxin B Polymyxin B preferred over colistin for infection sites other than UTI, because:
• Polymyxin B is administered in its active form, whereas colistin is given as the inactive prodrug colistimethate sodium (CMS) → better PK and higher plasma levels with polymyxin B.
• Polymyxin B is predominantly non-renally cleared → potentially lower rates of nephrotoxicity.
Colistin is preferred for UTI — the prodrug CMS is renally cleared, achieving higher urinary drug levels.

Clinical pearls

Sulbactam dose High dose is favoured over standard-dose ampicillin-sulbactam (4 g sulbactam/day). Use 6 g sulbactam/day for mild and 9 g/day for moderate–severe infections. Rationale: high-dose sulbactam saturates the PBPs of A. baumannii more effectively.
Extensively resistant CRAB Cefiderocol may be used (e.g. when susceptible only to cefiderocol), but it is preferable to combine with high-dose ampicillin-sulbactam — even if non-susceptibility to ampicillin-sulbactam is demonstrated. Data are scarce in paediatrics.
Do not use Nebulised polymyxin for HAP/VAP caused by CRAB — not recommended.
Meropenem + colistin combination — based on the AIDA and OVERCOME studies, this was not better than colistin monotherapy.
Meropenem + polymyxin + amp-sulbactam triple therapy — avoid; may lead to more toxicity (e.g. neurotoxicity from dual β-lactam therapy + polymyxin).

Paediatric considerations: options similar to adults. IV minocycline only for children >8 years old. Tigecycline not recommended in children unless no alternative. Paediatric ID consult advisable.

P MDR / DTR Pseudomonas aeruginosa Ceftolozane-tazobactam · ceftazidime-avibactam · cefiderocol ▶

Key microbiology

  • Aerobic Gram-negative bacillus found ubiquitously in soil, plants and hospital water reservoirs (showers, sinks, toilet water). Spreads via contaminated water, hands, equipment or surfaces.
  • NARSS 2022: overall resistance rates to all antibiotics tested were <10%; colistin resistance fell to 1.3% (from 8.2% in 2021).
  • ATLAS programme (Malaysia, 2013–2019): susceptibility to ceftazidime 83.4%, cefepime 87.1%, piperacillin-tazobactam 81.6%, imipenem 78.6%, meropenem 83.4%; ceftazidime-avibactam 93.1%, colistin 95.6%.
  • MDR phenotype in 11.7% of isolates (higher than the Asia-Pacific INFORM rate of 7.1%). These MDR isolates: 100% susceptible to colistin but only 44.7% susceptible to ceftazidime-avibactam.
  • Carbapenem resistance is not primarily carbapenemase-mediated — acquired β-lactamases absent in most isolates (83.7%). Mechanisms: OprD alterations, efflux pump changes, chromosomal AmpC overproduction.

Definitions

MDR P. aeruginosa: non-susceptibility to at least one antibiotic in at least three of the following classes:

  • Traditional extended-spectrum cephalosporins (ceftazidime, cefepime)
  • Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin)
  • Aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin, tobramycin)
  • Carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, doripenem)
  • Traditional β-lactam–β-lactamase inhibitor (piperacillin-tazobactam)

DTR (difficult-to-treat) P. aeruginosa: treatment-limiting resistance to all first-line agents — all β-lactams including carbapenems and β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and fluoroquinolones.

S Stenotrophomonas maltophilia TMP-SMX first line · intrinsically carbapenem-resistant ▶

Key microbiology

  • Ubiquitous non-fermenting Gram-negative rod closely related to Pseudomonas spp. Isolated from sinks, water, fomites, plants and hospital equipment.
  • Emerging cause of healthcare-associated infection. Prevalence in a Malaysian tertiary centre reported at 6.9%.
  • Most isolates from clinical specimens are colonisers — however, isolation in the immunocompromised and patients with chronic illness warrants antibiotic therapy.
  • Intrinsically resistant to penicillins, cephalosporins (excluding ceftazidime), aztreonam and carbapenems.
  • Ceftazidime breakpoints against S. maltophilia were removed from CLSI M100 due to lack of supporting data and poor reproducibility.

Prolonged / extended infusion — the principles

Definition Administration of drugs through prolonged infusion can be achieved by the extended method (infusion over 3–4 hours during a dosing interval) or through continuous infusion (constant IV infusion over 24 hours, or sequential infusions over 6, 8 or 12 hours). Particularly important for time-dependent antibiotics such as β-lactams.

Why: prolonged infusion increases the fraction of time that free drug concentrations remain above the MIC (%fT>MIC), improving efficacy of time-dependent antibiotics.

When to use prolonged infusion

  • Less susceptible pathogens with higher baseline MIC or propensity to develop resistance on therapy — P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, CRE.
  • Altered pharmacokinetics:
    • Critically ill patients
    • Augmented renal clearance (ARC) — CrCl ≥120 mL/min (risk factors: young patients, traumatic brain injury)
    • Obese patients
    • Hypoalbuminaemia (for highly protein-bound drugs)
  • Difficult penetration sites: CNS infection, bone and joint infection, pneumonia.
Loading dose An initial loading dose is essential to rapidly attain therapeutic concentration — typically given over 30–60 minutes. Loading doses are often higher than standard doses and are not affected by renal function. Subsequent doses should commence immediately after the loading dose (not at the next scheduled time).

🛡 Infection Prevention & Control — ICU Essentials Precautions, isolation, screening, un-tagging, sinks & drains ▶

Transmission routes

MDR GN transmission from an infected/colonised patient to another patient occurs via direct contact (body fluid contamination) and indirect contact (contaminated shared equipment, healthcare workers' hands, visitors, contaminated door handles, sinks, toilets, bed rails and other environmental surfaces).

Precautions by organism

OrganismPrecaution
AmpCStandard precautions. Strict adherence to 5 moments of hand hygiene; PPE based on risk assessment.
ESBLStandard + contact precaution (based on risk assessment for onward transmission). Alert/tag in notes or EMR. In limited-resource settings, the IPC team may re-stratify based on local prevalence.
CREContact + standard precaution. More aggressive interventions (e.g. screening contacts) reserved for CP-CRE. Non-CP CRE may still warrant targeted IPC interventions (e.g. contact precautions in acute care).
CRABContact + standard precaution.
MDR / DTR P. aeruginosaContact + standard precaution.
S. maltophiliaStandard precautions. Strict hand hygiene; PPE based on risk assessment; shared equipment disinfected between patients.

Contact precaution bundle

  • Signage clearly displayed outside the room.
  • Yellow clinical waste bin inside the room.
  • Dedicated equipment where possible (BP cuffs, oximeter probes). Shared equipment disinfected with hospital-approved disinfectant between patients.
  • Dedicated nursing staff recommended.
  • PPE cart outside the room containing: disposable gloves · long-sleeved gowns/aprons · hospital-approved disinfectant wipes · alcohol-based hand rub.
  • Strict adherence to the 5 moments of hand hygiene; hand hygiene before and after glove use; change gloves between patients and when moving from dirty to clean areas.
  • Gowns removed before leaving the patient care area/surroundings.

Patient environment: in a single room it is the entire room; in a multi-bed room it is the area behind the patient curtain.

Patient placement

In order of preference:

  1. Single room with attached bathroom (priority)
  2. Single room without attached bathroom
  3. Cohort in a dedicated cubicle/area with attached bathroom — for patients colonised/infected with the same resistant organism
  4. If general ward isolation is the only option: dedicated toilet/commode and an isolation tray/trolley beside the bed strongly recommended

Minimum spatial requirement: 1.5 m between beds in a standard multi-bedded room.

Risk assessment for onward transmission (to prioritise single-room placement):

  • Copious or uncontained drainage from wounds/abscesses
  • Dressings or drains requiring changing
  • Faecal or urinary incontinence
  • Indwelling urinary catheter / intermittent clean catheterisation
  • Abdominal drainage / intestinal stoma / fistula
  • Tracheostomy / ventilated / uncontained sputum
  • ≥2 invasive devices in situ requiring frequent access

Skin disinfectant & environmental cleaning

  • Daily antiseptic bathing with chlorhexidine or octenisan 2% throughout admission — reduces skin MDRO bioburden, lowering hospital-acquired infection risk and environmental contamination. Selection and dilution per hospital committee and manufacturer's recommendation (contact time, amount, frequency).
  • Daily cleaning of patient environment and bathroom, emphasising high-touch surfaces.
  • Enhanced cleaning (increased frequency of high-touch surface cleaning, plus certain low-touch areas) for patients infected/colonised with CRE, CRAB or DTR-Pseudomonas.
  • Terminal cleaning after discharge; fabric or disposable curtains must be changed on discharge.
  • Clean from cleaner areas to dirtier areas.

Equipment disinfection — Spaulding classification

CategoryDescriptionCleaning required
CriticalObjects that enter normally sterile tissue or the vascular systemSterilisation
Semi-criticalObjects that contact mucous membranes or non-intact skinHigh-level disinfection

All staff cleaning critical and semi-critical equipment should be trained and credentialed. Cleaning of non-critical items (infusion pumps, glucometers, BP cuffs, monitors) requires clear protocols/SOPs and designated responsibility.

Active surveillance

  • Entry, weekly and exit screening for colonisation may be considered, especially during an outbreak.
  • Discuss with ID/IPC — for CRE and other carbapenemase-producing organisms, screening should be guided by local epidemiology and risk assessment, considering:
    • History of previous CRE infection/colonisation
    • Contacts of CRE cases
    • Recent hospitalisation in wards/facilities endemic with CRE or with CRE outbreaks
    • Increased risk of acquisition: extended hospitalisation, multiple/recent exposures to broad-spectrum antibiotics, indwelling devices (CVL, CBD, biliary or wound drains), mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, burns, organ/stem-cell transplant, respiratory patients on long-term antibiotics
  • Limited benefit in active surveillance for carbapenemase-producing Acinetobacter (CPA) and Pseudomonas (CPP) outside outbreaks or case follow-up.

Screening samples for CPE — in order of preference

  1. Faeces
  2. Rectal swab + inguinal swab
  3. Rectal swab alone
  4. Peri-anal (e.g. neutropenic patient)

If symptoms are present or clinically relevant, also send appropriate samples from endotracheal tube, enterostomy, urinary catheter or wound.

Pre-emptive isolation and contact precautions should be implemented until screening culture results are available.

Decolonisation

Not recommended There is insufficient evidence to recommend decolonisation in patients with MDR GN infection.

Un-tagging — clearance criteria

Patients may remain colonised for at least 6–12 months, and some considerably longer — particularly with ongoing/repeated healthcare contact and/or antibiotic use.

Risk factors for prolonged carriage (use in risk assessment):

  • Exposure to antibiotics since initial infection/colonisation
  • Number of hospital admissions (e.g. multiple hospitalisations)
  • Presence of an invasive device
  • Higher Charlson's comorbidity score
  • Admission from another facility — especially where CRE is endemic or there is ongoing transmission/outbreak
  • Admission from, or discharge to, a long-term care facility
OrganismIsolation duration & un-tagging criteria
CRE / CPE Isolation for at least 1 year. Un-tagging may be considered after 1 year since the last positive culture, provided the patient:
  • Does not have an active infection
  • Is not on active treatment for CRE infection
  • Has no risk for prolonged CRE carriage
  • Has at least 2 consecutive negative rectal swabs taken ≥1 week apart
Earlier removal from contact precautions may be made after consultation with ID/IPC following a complete risk assessment.
Despite release, an alert (tag) should remain in the medical record that CPE has previously been isolated.
CPA / CPP Exact duration of isolation is not known; contact precautions throughout hospitalisation. On subsequent admissions, risk-assess for ongoing colonisation and onward transmission to decide whether to continue contact precautions and repeat screening — considering ward (e.g. ICU), reason for admission, and individual risk factors for prolonged colonisation (e.g. COPD with history of CPP colonisation).
Alert (tag) should remain in the medical record. Monitor for signs/symptoms of infection and investigate immediately if infection is suspected.
ESBL Protocols differ by hospital policy — from no isolation at all to a tag duration of 3–6 months.
Consider off-tag if negative rectal swabs at D0, D7 and D14 after completion of treatment.
On subsequent admissions, risk-assess as for CPP/CPA.

Decisions for de-isolation and un-tagging of all MDRO cases should be made after consultation with the ID or IPC team, based on institutional risks, priorities and resources.

Communication, training & audit

  • Patient status and required precautions must be clearly communicated to all staff involved in care; documented in clinical notes and referral letters; IT-based hospitals may tag MDRO status in the EMR.
  • All staff involved in patient care should receive training on standard and transmission-based precautions. Cleaning staff require environmental (daily and terminal) cleaning training.
  • Regular audits: hand hygiene compliance, transmission-based precautions practices, environmental cleaning (visual and fluorescent markers). Provide feedback to key stakeholders.

Patients, visitors & movement

  • Provide information leaflets to ward staff, patients and visitors as appropriate.
  • Encourage all visitors to perform hand hygiene before entering and leaving the patient's room; advise patients and visitors to avoid contact with other patients.
  • Staff should remove gloves and gowns before leaving patients' rooms; both staff and patients perform hand hygiene when leaving the room.
  • Minimise risk of environmental contamination during patient transport.
  • Day care / dialysis / outpatient: contact precautions at the bedside, examination chair, or whenever providing direct patient care; dedicated toilet or commode chair if applicable; clean the examination couch/trolley/chair and shared equipment after the patient leaves and before the next patient.

NICU — additional measures

  • Strict expressed breast milk (EBM) and formula milk preparation and protocol
  • Strict milk room, including sink cleaning protocol
  • Incubator change and cleaning protocol
  • Immediate diaper disposal after changing to prevent environmental contamination
  • Weighing scale cleaning and disinfection protocol between patient use
  • Antiseptic bath or wipes for newborns: still lacking evidence and there are concerns about adverse events — may be considered in an outbreak situation. Chlorhexidine concentrations from 0.1% to 2% have been reported.

Surge / outbreak

  • Refer to ID/IPC for outbreak investigation and formation of the outbreak management team.
  • Look for causes of the surge/outbreak and implement control measures.
  • Contaminated environments or equipment may be the cause — enhanced control measures may be needed. Discuss with ID/IPC and microbiologists before environmental sampling.
  • CRE outbreak: investigate milk preparation rooms, especially in ICU.
  • CRAB outbreak: observe and improve environmental and equipment cleaning/disinfection. Radical terminal cleaning including working areas and equipment, plus environmental swabs, may help halt the outbreak.
  • Pseudomonas outbreak: investigate water source contamination — all sinks, IV fluid or heparin saline, medication trolley. In VAP outbreaks, also investigate respiratory/suction tubing.

Sinks, drains & plumbing

Sink drainpipes become colonised with highly consequential MDR bacteria, which then cause hospital-acquired infections. Bacteria initially colonise the elbows of the drainpipe and slowly spread to the strainers. Water hitting the strainer can splash onto surrounding counters and the floor up to three feet away.

General measures

  • Alcohol-based hand rub is the preferred method for cleaning hands when not visibly dirty.
  • Regularly clean and disinfect surfaces near drains — sink basin, faucet, handles, surrounding countertop — at least daily or more frequently.
  • Designated hand-washing sinks are for hand washing only. Do not dispose of clinical waste, patient specimens, liquid nutritional supplements or beverages down the sink.
  • Sinks should be positioned at sufficient distance so they do not contaminate patients, clean supplies or adjacent counters through splashing.
  • Add sink splash guards. Hand-washing sinks should be free-standing or wall-mounted, at least 1 metre from any fixed work surface, or separated by a splash barrier.
  • Avoid placing patient care or personal items on counters next to sinks.
  • Do not place sinks next to medication preparation areas unless splash barriers are in place.

Design

  • Trapless sinks are recommended.
  • Prevent faucets/taps from discharging directly above the drain (angle water away or offset the faucet from the drain).
  • Aerators and strainers are associated with increased contamination risk due to biofilm capture — risk-assess whether they should be removed.
  • Use sinks with adequate depth and regulated maximum water flow to prevent splashing.

Chemical disinfection of sinks

  • May reduce pathogen burden in contaminated drains and reduce contaminated splashing.
  • Hydrogen peroxide vapour, and pouring disinfectants such as bleach, hydrogen peroxide or acetic acid down sink drains, have been shown to decrease bioburden and may be useful in outbreak response — but regrowth occurs within a few days.
  • The most appropriate disinfection method remains unclear; no single agent has been found superior and ideal concentrations are unknown. Some solutions may erode the plumbing — discuss with engineers.
