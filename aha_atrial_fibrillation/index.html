Atrial Fibrillation

This is a condensed aide-mémoire, not a substitute for the full guideline or for clinical judgement. Always confirm doses against local formularies and product labelling.
1At a glance — the essentials Start here
  • Unstable AF with rapid ventricular response → immediate synchronised DC cardioversion. Do not delay for drugs.
  • Stable, no decompensated HF → IV beta-blocker or non-dihydropyridine CCB (diltiazem/verapamil) first line; digoxin or amiodarone as add-on/alternative. Consider IV magnesium as an adjunct.
  • Decompensated HF or LVEF <40% → non-dihydropyridine CCBs are harmful. Use IV amiodarone (or digoxin) for rate control.
  • Warfarin before procedures → stop ~5 days pre-op; do not bridge with LMWH (except mechanical valve or recent stroke/TIA) — bridging increases bleeding without reducing thromboembolism.
  • DOACs before procedures → brief interruption timed to agent + renal function + procedural bleeding risk; no bridging.
  • Life-threatening bleeding on a DOAC → idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa or 4-factor PCC for factor Xa inhibitors; 4-factor PCC + IV vitamin K for warfarin.
  • AF discovered during critical illness or surgery → a marker of risk, not a benign finding. Recurrence 40–68% at 5 years; recurrence carries ↑HF, ↑stroke, ↑mortality. Arrange rhythm monitoring and thromboembolic risk review after discharge.
  • Pre-excited AF (WPW) → AV nodal blockers (verapamil, diltiazem, amiodarone, digoxin, adenosine, beta-blockers) are contraindicated — risk of VF. Use DC cardioversion, or IV ibutilide/procainamide if stable.

Source: 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline, Sections 7.2.1, 6.7, 6.6, 10.10, 10.6.

2Periprocedural anticoagulation — interruption & bridging High yield

Recommendations

  • 1B-RIn patients with AF (excluding recent stroke/TIA or a mechanical valve) on warfarin or a DOAC scheduled for an invasive procedure or surgery, temporary cessation without bridging anticoagulation is recommended.
  • 1AOn warfarin with an annual predicted thromboembolism risk ≥5% undergoing pacemaker/ICD implantation or generator change, continued warfarin is recommended in preference to interruption with heparin bridging, to reduce pocket haematoma.
  • 2aAOn a DOAC with CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥2 (or equivalent) undergoing pacemaker/ICD implantation or generator change, either uninterrupted or interrupted DOAC is reasonable.
  • 1B-NROn a DOAC scheduled for a procedure that cannot safely be performed on uninterrupted anticoagulation, timing of interruption should be guided by the specific agent, renal function and the bleeding risk of the procedure.
  • 2aB-NRAfter interruption, resume the day after low-bleeding-risk surgery and between the evening of day 2 and the evening of day 3 after high-bleeding-risk surgery, provided haemostasis is achieved and further bleeding is not anticipated.
  • 3 HarmB-ROn warfarin and holding for surgery, bridging with low-molecular-weight heparin should not be given (except mechanical valve or recent stroke/TIA).

3Acute rate control in AF with rapid ventricular response High yield

Recommendations

  • 1B-RHaemodynamically stable AF with rapid ventricular response: beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem — provided EF >40%) are recommended for acute rate control.
  • 2aB-RWhere beta-blockers and non-dihydropyridine CCBs are ineffective or contraindicated, digoxin can be considered, alone or in combination.
  • 2aAAddition of intravenous magnesium to standard rate-control measures is reasonable to achieve and maintain rate control.
  • 2bB-NRIn critically ill and/or decompensated HF patients where beta-blockers and CCBs are ineffective or contraindicated, IV amiodarone may be considered for acute rate control.
  • 3 HarmB-NRKnown moderate or severe LV systolic dysfunction, with or without decompensated HF: IV non-dihydropyridine CCBs should not be administered.

4Cardioversion — thromboembolism prevention & technique

Prevention of thromboembolism around cardioversion

  • 1B-RAF duration ≥48 h: 3 weeks of uninterrupted therapeutic anticoagulation or imaging to exclude intracardiac thrombus before elective cardioversion.
  • 1B-NRTherapeutic anticoagulation should be established before cardioversion and continued for at least 4 weeks afterwards without interruption.
  • 1C-LDIf cardioversion is deferred because of LAA thrombus on pre-cardioversion imaging, anticoagulate for at least 3–6 weeks and repeat imaging before cardioversion.
  • 2aB-NRPrevious LAA occlusion and not on anticoagulation: imaging to assess adequacy of occlusion and exclude device-related thrombus before cardioversion may be reasonable.
  • 2bC-LDPrevious LAA occlusion with residual leak: peri-cardioversion anticoagulation may be considered and continued thereafter.
  • 2bC-LDReported AF duration <48 h (not post-cardiac-surgery) and not anticoagulated: pre-cardioversion imaging may be considered if thromboembolic risk is elevated (CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥2 or equivalent).
  • 2bC-LDLow thromboembolic risk (CHA₂DS₂-VASc 0–1 or equivalent) and AF duration <12 h: benefit of pre-cardioversion imaging or peri-cardioversion anticoagulation is uncertain.
AF ≥48 h3 weeks therapeutic anticoagulation or imaging (TEE / CT) to exclude thrombus
Thrombus foundDefer cardioversion · anticoagulate 3–6 weeks · repeat imaging
ProceedTherapeutic anticoagulation before, and continue ≥4 weeks after

DOACs may be preferred over VKAs for planned cardioversion (rapid onset, reliable effect, ease of continuation) unless contraindicated. Cardiac CT with delayed contrast enhancement is an alternative to TEE for excluding intracardiac thrombus.

Electrical cardioversion

  • 1C-LDHaemodynamic instability attributable to AF: immediate electrical cardioversion to restore sinus rhythm.
  • 1B-RHaemodynamically stable: electrical cardioversion can be performed as the initial rhythm-control strategy or after unsuccessful pharmacological cardioversion.
  • 1C-LDSynchronisation to the QRS must be confirmed — unsynchronised or inappropriately synchronised shocks can induce VF. Re-confirm before every shock.
  • 2aB-RElective cardioversion: biphasic energy of at least 200 J as the initial energy can be beneficial to improve first-shock success.
  • 2aB-NRLonger AF duration or unsuccessful initial shock: optimise the electrode vector (anterior–posterior for longer duration), use higher energy, and consider antiarrhythmic pre-treatment.
  • 2bC-LDObesity: manual pressure augmentation and/or escalation of energy may improve success.

Pharmacological cardioversion

  • 2aC-LDReasonable as an alternative to electrical cardioversion in haemodynamically stable patients, or when electrical cardioversion is preferred but cannot be performed.
  • 2aAIbutilide is reasonable in patients without depressed LV function (LVEF <40%).
  • 2aAIV amiodarone is reasonable, though time to conversion is longer (8–12 h).
  • 2aARecurrent AF outside hospital: "pill-in-the-pocket" single oral dose of flecainide or propafenone with a concomitant AV nodal blocking agent — only if previously tested in a monitored setting.
  • 2bB-RIV procainamide may be considered when other IV agents are contraindicated or not preferred.

5Active bleeding & anticoagulant reversal ICU critical

Recommendations

  • 1B-NRAF on dabigatran with life-threatening bleeding: idarucizumab is recommended to rapidly reverse dabigatran's anticoagulant effect.
  • 2aC-LDIf idarucizumab is unavailable: activated prothrombin complex concentrate is reasonable.
  • 1B-NR / C-LDAF on factor Xa inhibitors with life-threatening bleeding: andexanet alfa (apixaban or rivaroxaban; edoxaban C-LD) or 4-factor PCC is recommended.
  • 1AAF on warfarin with life-threatening bleeding: 4-factor PCC (where available) in addition to IV vitamin K is recommended to rapidly achieve INR correction.
  • 2bB-NRAfter a major bleed on a DOAC, resumption of oral anticoagulation may be reasonable after reversal of the reversible cause of bleeding and reassessment of long-term benefits and risks with a multidisciplinary team and shared decision-making.

Supportive measures. Activated charcoal may be given up to 6 hours after the last dose of a DOAC. Haemodialysis may be considered to eliminate dabigatran — challenging and impractical if there is coagulopathy or haemodynamic instability. About 2–4% of patients on oral anticoagulants experience major bleeding requiring intervention.

Restarting anticoagulation after bleeding

Meta-analyses in AF patients after gastrointestinal bleeding: restarting was associated with higher recurrent GI bleeding (10.1% vs 5.3%) but lower thromboembolic events (6.3% vs 10.6%) and lower all-cause mortality (21.3% vs 31%). Optimal timing after upper GI bleeding with a VKA was reported at 3–6 weeks in one study, and 1 week in others. Data on timing of DOAC restart are scarce.

AF and intracranial haemorrhage

  • 2aC-LDConditions with very high thromboembolic risk (≥5%/year), e.g. rheumatic heart disease or a mechanical heart valve: early (1–2 weeks) resumption of anticoagulation after ICH is reasonable.
  • 2bC-LDOther patients with AF and ICH: delayed (4–8 weeks) resumption may be considered to balance thromboembolic and haemorrhagic risk after careful risk–benefit assessment.
  • 2bB-NRHigh risk of recurrent ICH (e.g. cerebral amyloid angiopathy): anticoagulation-sparing strategies (e.g. LAA occlusion) may be considered.

High recurrent-ICH risk features: suspected cerebral amyloid angiopathy, lobar intraparenchymal haemorrhage, older age, >10 cerebral microbleeds on MRI, disseminated cortical superficial siderosis, poorly controlled hypertension, previous spontaneous ICH, coagulopathy, untreated vascular malformation or aneurysm.

Source: 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline, Section 6.6 "Active Bleeding on Anticoagulant Therapy and Reversal Drugs", Section 6.6.1 "Management of Patients With AF and ICH", Tables 15 and 17 (p e44–e49).

7Stroke risk assessment & who gets anticoagulated

Recommendations

  • 1AAF with an estimated annual thromboembolic risk ≥2%/year (e.g. CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥2 in men, ≥3 in women): anticoagulation is recommended.
  • 1AWithout moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis or a mechanical heart valve, and a candidate for anticoagulation: DOACs are recommended over warfarin to reduce mortality, stroke, systemic embolism and intracranial haemorrhage.
  • 2aAEstimated annual thromboembolic risk ≥1% but <2%/year (CHA₂DS₂-VASc 1 in men, 2 in women): anticoagulation is reasonable.
  • 3 HarmB-RCandidates for anticoagulation without an indication for antiplatelet therapy: aspirin alone or with clopidogrel is not recommended as an alternative to anticoagulation.
  • 3 No benefitB-NRAF without risk factors for stroke: aspirin monotherapy is of no benefit.
  • 1B-NREvaluate annual thromboembolic risk using a validated clinical risk score such as CHA₂DS₂-VASc.
  • 3 No benefitB-NRIn patients deemed at high risk for stroke, bleeding risk scores should not be used in isolation to determine eligibility for oral anticoagulation — use them to identify and modify bleeding risk factors.
  • 2aC-LDAt intermediate annual risk who remain uncertain about the benefit of anticoagulation, consider other risk-modifying factors (AF burden, blood pressure control, etc.) to inform the decision.

Risk score caveats. The absolute risk at any given score varies widely between cohorts — among 15 cohort studies, a CHA₂DS₂-VASc of 2 corresponded to an annual stroke rate of <1% in 4 cohorts, 1–2% in 6 cohorts and >2% in 5 cohorts. Bleeding scores (HAS-BLED, HEMORR₂HAGES, ATRIA) discriminate poorly (c-index 0.58–0.59) and include factors that predict both stroke and bleeding. 8AF in critical illness & after cardiac surgery ICU

Acute medical illness or surgery (including AF in critical care)

  • 1B-NRPatients with AF identified during acute medical illness or surgery should be counselled about the significant risk of recurrent AF after the acute illness resolves.
  • 2aB-NROutpatient follow-up for thromboembolic risk stratification, decision-making on OAC initiation or continuation, and AF surveillance can be beneficial given the high risk of recurrence.
  • 2bB-NRIn critical illness due to sepsis, the benefits of anticoagulation during critical illness for stroke prevention are uncertain.
Epidemiology and outcomes. Incidence of AF during acute medical illness 1–46%; severe sepsis 6–22%; after non-cardiac surgery 3–16%. Recurrence at 5 years: 42–68% after medical illness, 39% after non-cardiac surgery. Recurrent AF is associated with HF (HR 2.74, 95% CI 2.39–3.15), stroke (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.30–1.90) and mortality (HR 2.96, 95% CI 2.70–3.24). In a cohort of 38,582 patients with AF and sepsis, parenteral anticoagulation did not reduce stroke (1.3% vs 1.4%) and was associated with increased clinically significant bleeding (8.6% vs 7.2%; RR 1.21).

Prevention of AF after cardiac surgery

  • 2aB-RHigh risk of postoperative AF: short-term prophylactic beta-blockers or amiodarone are reasonable to reduce incidence.
  • 2aB-RCABG, aortic valve or ascending aortic aneurysm operations: concomitant posterior left pericardiotomy is reasonable to reduce postoperative AF (17% vs 32% in the PALACS trial).

Treatment of AF after cardiac surgery

  • 1ABeta-blockers are recommended for rate control unless contraindicated or ineffective, in which case a non-dihydropyridine CCB is recommended.
  • 1B-RHaemodynamically stable postoperative AF: rate control (target heart rate <100 bpm) and/or rhythm-control medication as initial therapy, chosen according to symptoms, haemodynamic consequences and physician preference.
  • 1B-RPoorly tolerated AF: direct current cardioversion combined with antiarrhythmic drug therapy; consider imaging to exclude LAA thrombus before cardioversion if AF has been present >48 h and the patient is not anticoagulated.
  • 2aB-NRAnticoagulation for 60 days after surgery when deemed safe with regard to surgical bleeding, then reassess the need for longer-term anticoagulation.
  • 2aC-LDAt 30–60 day follow-up, perform rhythm assessment and, if AF has not reverted to sinus rhythm spontaneously, consider cardioversion after an adequate duration of anticoagulation.

9AF and heart failure

Recommendations

  • 1B-NRNew diagnosis of HFrEF and AF: suspect arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy and adopt an early, aggressive approach to rhythm control.
  • 1AAF with HFrEF on GDMT with reasonable expectation of procedural benefit: catheter ablation is beneficial to improve symptoms, quality of life, ventricular function and cardiovascular outcomes.
  • 2aB-NRSymptomatic AF with HFpEF: catheter ablation can be useful to improve symptoms and quality of life.
  • 2aB-RAF and HF: digoxin is reasonable for rate control, in combination with other rate-controlling agents or as monotherapy if others are not tolerated.
  • 2aB-NRAF and HF with rapid ventricular rates where beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers are contraindicated or ineffective: IV amiodarone is reasonable for acute rate control.
  • 2aB-RAF, HFrEF (LVEF <50%) and refractory rapid ventricular response not amenable to rhythm control: AV nodal ablation and biventricular pacing can be useful to improve symptoms, quality of life and EF.
  • 2aB-NRAF, HF and biventricular pacing where an effective pacing percentage cannot be achieved pharmacologically: AV nodal ablation can be beneficial — improves functional class, reduces ICD shock risk and improves survival.
  • 2aB-NRAF-induced cardiomyopathy with recovered LV function: long-term surveillance can be beneficial to detect recurrent AF.
  • 2bB-NRSuspected AF-induced cardiomyopathy or refractory HF symptoms on pharmacological rate control: a stricter rate-control strategy (target <80 bpm at rest and <110 bpm during moderate exercise) may be reasonable.
  • 3 HarmB-RKnown LVEF <40%: non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers should not be administered.
  • 3 HarmB-RNYHA class III–IV HF, or decompensated HF within the past 4 weeks: dronedarone should not be administered for maintenance of sinus rhythm (increased early mortality from worsening HF).
Why this matters. AF is the most common cause of arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy. AF and HF are bidirectionally related: among individuals with new AF, 37% had HF; among those with new HF, 57% had AF. AF carries an adjusted mortality OR of 1.40 (randomised trials) and 1.14 (observational studies).

Source: 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline, Sections 9.1 and 9.2, Figure 24 and Table 27 (p e91–e97).

Inpatient initiation recommendations

  • 1AInitiating, increasing the dose of, or reinitiating dofetilide: admit for a minimum of 3 days to a facility with continuous ECG monitoring, CrCl calculation and cardiac resuscitation.
  • 2aB-RInitiating sotalol: reasonable to do so in a facility with continuous ECG monitoring, CrCl calculation and cardiac resuscitation.
  • 2aB-NRInitiating pill-in-the-pocket flecainide or propafenone with a concomitant AV nodal blocking drug: reasonable to give the first dose in a facility with continuous ECG monitoring.

Class IC agents should always be given with a concomitant AV nodal blocking agent to reduce the risk of 1:1 atrial flutter. A newer FDA-approved IV sotalol formulation may allow evaluation of safety and tolerability within 6 hours and obviate a 3-day admission, but data are limited.

11Special populations & situations

Wolff–Parkinson–White and pre-excitation syndromes

  • 1B-NRAF with rapid anterograde conduction (pre-excited AF) and haemodynamic instability: electrical cardioversion.
  • 1B-NRPre-excited AF: catheter ablation of the accessory pathway is recommended.
  • 1C-LDHaemodynamically stable pre-excited AF: IV ibutilide or IV procainamide as an alternative to elective cardioversion.
  • 3 HarmB-NRPre-excited AF: AV nodal blocking agents are contraindicated — verapamil, diltiazem, amiodarone, digoxin, adenosine and beta-blockers — due to the risk of precipitating VF or haemodynamic deterioration.

Chronic kidney disease / kidney failure

  • 1B-RAF at elevated stroke risk with CKD stage 3: warfarin or, preferably, evidence-based doses of direct thrombin or factor Xa inhibitors.
  • 2aB-NRCKD stage 4: warfarin or labelled doses of DOACs is reasonable.
  • 2bB-NREnd-stage CKD (CrCl <15 mL/min) or dialysis: warfarin (INR 2.0–3.0) or an evidence-based dose of apixaban might be reasonable. Pivotal DOAC trials excluded dialysis patients; only dabigatran is substantially removed by dialysis.

Liver disease

  • 2aB-NRIncreased thromboembolic risk with mild or moderate liver disease (Child–Pugh A or B): OAC therapy is reasonable in the absence of clinically significant coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia.
  • 2aB-NRCandidates for anticoagulation with Child–Pugh A or B: DOACs preferred over warfarin (Child–Pugh A: any DOAC; Child–Pugh B: apixaban, dabigatran or edoxaban).
  • 3 HarmC-LDRivaroxaban is contraindicated in Child–Pugh B due to potentially increased bleeding risk (AUC increased 2.27-fold).

Valvular heart disease

  • 1B-RRheumatic mitral stenosis or mitral stenosis of moderate or greater severity with AF: long-term warfarin is recommended over DOACs, independent of CHA₂DS₂-VASc, to prevent cardiovascular events including stroke or death (INVICTUS trial).
  • 1B-NRAF with valve disease other than moderate/greater mitral stenosis or a mechanical valve: DOACs are recommended over VKAs.

AF complicating ACS or PCI

  • 1AAF with increased stroke risk undergoing PCI: DOACs preferred over VKAs in combination with antiplatelet therapy to reduce clinically relevant bleeding.
  • 1AMost patients on OAC undergoing PCI: early discontinuation of aspirin (1–4 weeks) and continuation of dual antithrombotic therapy with OAC + a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor is preferred over triple therapy.

Meta-analysis of PIONEER AF-PCI, RE-DUAL PCI and AUGUSTUS: dual therapy reduced major bleeding by ~40% versus triple therapy, but was associated with increased stent thrombosis (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.02–2.73) — driven largely by the dabigatran 110 mg arm. Number needed to treat for major bleeding = 42; number needed to harm for stent thrombosis = 223. Consider extending triple therapy up to 30 days when the perceived stent thrombosis risk is high (complex revascularisation, multivessel PCI, previous stent thrombosis).

Chronic coronary disease

  • 1B-RAF and chronic coronary disease (beyond 1 year after revascularisation, or CAD not requiring revascularisation) without a history of stent thrombosis: oral anticoagulation monotherapy is recommended over OAC + single antiplatelet therapy, to decrease major bleeding.

Peripheral artery disease

Class of Recommendation 2a (LOE B-NR). Antiplatelet monotherapy remains standard of care for symptomatic PAD. Subgroup analyses of DOAC trials show comparable efficacy and safety of DOACs versus warfarin in patients with AF with and without PAD. Observational data in concomitant AF and PAD found increased bleeding with dual therapy (OAC + antiplatelet) without reduction in major adverse cardiac events. The 2016 AHA/ACC PAD guideline recommends against anticoagulation for ischaemic risk reduction in the general PAD population.

Note: the recommendation table for PAD (Section 6.8.3) is truncated in the source document; the above reflects the accompanying synopsis and supportive text.

Hyperthyroidism

  • 1B-NRAF with hyperthyroidism and elevated stroke risk by a standard clinical risk score: anticoagulation is recommended until thyroid function has returned to normal and sinus rhythm can be maintained.

Pulmonary disease

  • 2aB-RAF and COPD: cardioselective beta-blockers are reasonable for rate control, especially where other indications exist (e.g. MI, HF).
  • 2aB-NRPulmonary hypertension with pulmonary vascular disease and AF: a rhythm-control strategy is reasonable to improve functional status and potentially prolong survival.

Pregnancy (summary)

DCCV of AF in pregnancy is safe for mother and fetus and should be performed within 48 hours of presentation to minimise stroke risk, with appropriate sedation and fetal monitoring. Antiarrhythmic drugs should be avoided if possible during the first trimester. Amiodarone is generally avoided (fetal goitre, neurodevelopmental abnormalities, bradycardia, growth delay, premature birth). Rate control options include propranolol, metoprolol or digoxin; atenolol is generally avoided (intrauterine growth retardation). DOACs are contraindicated during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Warfarin may be used in the first trimester if the dose required is ≤5 mg/day; if >5 mg/day, use LMWH throughout the first trimester then transition to warfarin until 36 hours before planned delivery.

Cardio-oncology

  • 1C-LDAF and cancer: multidisciplinary communication including cardiology, oncology and other clinicians, plus shared decision-making, is recommended to optimise treatment and reduce drug interactions, QTc prolongation, proarrhythmia, bleeding and thromboembolism.
  • 2aC-LDBefore starting cancer therapies associated with increased AF risk: increased vigilance for incident AF and treatment of contributing factors is reasonable.
  • 2aB-NRMost patients with AF and cancer (remote history or active treatment): DOACs are reasonable over VKAs for stroke risk reduction.

Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g. ibrutinib) carry a 4-fold higher risk of AF. Beta-blockers are considered the best rate-control option in BTK-inhibitor-associated AF; CYP3A inhibitors such as amiodarone, dronedarone, verapamil and diltiazem are usually avoided.

Source: 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline, Sections 6.8.1–6.8.5, 10.4, 10.6, 10.11–10.16 (p e52–e58, e97–e110).

12Device-detected AF (atrial high-rate episodes)

Relevant to ICU and perioperative patients with pacemakers, ICDs, implantable loop recorders or wearables. Clinician confirmation of the duration and nature of the longest atrial high-rate episode is recommended before acting.

  • 2aB-NRDevice-detected AHRE lasting ≥24 hours with CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥2 (or equivalent): it is reasonable to initiate oral anticoagulation within a shared decision-making framework that considers episode duration and individual patient risk.
  • 2bB-NRDevice-detected AHRE lasting 5 minutes to 24 hours with CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥3 (or equivalent): anticoagulation may be reasonable within a shared decision-making framework.
  • 3 No benefitB-NRDevice-detected AHRE lasting <5 minutes without another indication: oral anticoagulation should not be given.
Context. The stroke risk of device-detected AF is lower than that of clinical AF and varies with episode duration and CHA₂DS₂-VASc score. Episodes ≥24 hours are associated with a significant risk of stroke or systemic embolism (adjusted HR 3.24, 95% CI 1.51–6.95 in ASSERT) that may be reduced with oral anticoagulation. Episodes ≤5 minutes are not associated with clinical events. The threshold for anticoagulation is therefore higher than for clinical AF.

Source: 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline, Section 6.4.1 and Figure 12 (p e40–e42).

13Definitions & classification (quick reference)
Atrial fibrillation — definitions
TermDefinition
Atrial fibrillationSupraventricular tachyarrhythmia with uncoordinated atrial activation and ineffective atrial contraction. ECG: irregular R–R intervals (when AV conduction present), absence of distinct P waves, irregular atrial activity (fibrillatory waves). Requires visual confirmation of the diagnosis by a clinician.
Paroxysmal AFIntermittent and terminates within ≤7 days of onset.
Persistent AFContinuous and sustained for >7 days and requires intervention. Patients who become paroxysmal with therapy are still defined as persistent.
Long-standing persistent AFContinuous for >12 months.
Permanent AFJoint patient–clinician decision to stop further attempts to restore and/or maintain sinus rhythm. Acceptance of AF is a therapeutic decision, not a pathophysiological attribute.
Subclinical AFAF identified in individuals without symptoms attributable to AF and with no previous ECG documenting AF — includes AF detected by implanted devices or wearable monitors.
Atrial high-rate episodes (AHRE)Atrial events exceeding the programmed detection rate limit set by an implanted device; require visual inspection to confirm AF and exclude other arrhythmias, artefact or oversensing.
AF burdenEncompasses both frequency and duration — the amount of AF an individual has. Defined in this guideline as episode duration, or as a percentage of AF duration during the monitoring period, depending on the study.
Obsolete terms"Chronic AF", "lone AF", and the "valvular"/"non-valvular" distinction should be abandoned.
