Brain Death — Quick Reference for Anaesthetists & Intensivists

Clinical summary of the Malaysian Consensus Statement on Brain Death 2024

1Definition & Key Principle
Definition Brain death is the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. It is a clinical diagnosis.
  • Adults with brain death will eventually develop asystole regardless of treatment given.
  • MRI shows diffuse swelling with tentorial and foraminal herniation; angiographic studies show absent blood flow.
  • Post-mortem: widespread brain necrosis with swelling of the hemispheres and brainstem — incompatible with life.
  • No confirmatory investigation is required in the usual case scenario — the exception is when certain aspects of the clinical tests cannot be reliably performed or evaluated (assessing physician's judgement, case-by-case).
2Preconditions — all must be fulfilled
  • Patient is in deep coma, apnoeic and on mechanical ventilation, for at least 12 hours.
  • The cause of coma is fully established and sufficient to explain the patient's condition. Brain imaging is highly recommended — at least a CT brain.
  • There is irremediable brain damage.
  • Patient is haemodynamically stable.
3Exclusions — do not proceed if present
Exclude
  • Coma due to metabolic or endocrine disturbances, drug intoxication and primary hypothermia (core temperature < 35 °C).
  • When certain neurological disorders cannot be excluded: Guillain–Barré syndrome, locked-in syndrome, botulism, organophosphate poisoning.
  • Coma of undetermined cause.
  • Preterm neonates.

Patients who do not meet all the criteria shall not be considered for brain death certification.

4Clinical Criterion 1 — Deep Coma
  • GCS 3/15 — unresponsive and unreceptive.
  • Absence of coordinated eye movements and motor activity, including decorticate or decerebrate posturing.
  • No spontaneous motor activity (excluding spinal reflexes) and no epileptic seizures.
  • No motor response to pain.
  • In the newborn: no sucking and rooting reflex.

During the observation period the patient must remain deeply comatose with no respiratory effort and no abnormal posture or movements in the cranial nerve distribution.

5Clinical Criterion 2 — Absent Brainstem Reflexes (a–g)

Tests the reflex pathways of the mesencephalon, pons and medulla oblongata in a rostral-to-caudal direction. All must be absent.

a. Pupillary light reflex

Test: absent response to bright light in both eyes. Pupil size may be mid-position (4–6 mm) to fully dilated. Round, oval or irregularly shaped pupils are compatible with brain death.

Pitfalls: many drugs influence pupil size but light response remains intact; exclude pre-existing iris abnormality or previous surgery; topical ocular drugs and corneal/eyeball trauma may produce non-reactive pupils.

b. Oculo-cephalic reflex

Test: perform only when there is no fracture or instability of the cervical spine — in head injury the cervical spine must be imaged first. Fast, vigorous turning of the head from mid-position to 90° on both sides. Any eye movement must be absent.

Pitfalls: after closed head injury or facial trauma, lid oedema and chemosis of the conjunctivae may restrict globe movement.

c. Motor response in cranial nerve distribution

Test: pressure stimulus to the supraorbital nerve, or deep pressure on both condyles at the level of the temporo-mandibular joints. Absent grimace response.

d. Corneal reflex

Test: both eyes tested with a cotton swab. Absent blink response.

e. Vestibulo-ocular reflex (caloric test)

Test: visualise the external auditory meatus with an otoscope first. Head elevated to 30°. Irrigate each external auditory canal with 50 ml of ice water. Allow 1 minute after irrigation and at least 5 minutes between testing each side. Absent eye movement to cold caloric stimulus.

Pitfalls: clotted blood or cerumen may diminish the response; basal fracture of the petrous bone abolishes the response unilaterally (may be identified by an ecchymotic mastoid process — Battle's sign); certain drugs may diminish or abolish the response.

f. Oro-pharyngeal reflex

Test: stimulate the posterior pharynx bilaterally. Absent gag response.

g. Tracheo-bronchial reflex

Test: pass a suction catheter down the endotracheal tube at least to the level of the carina. Absent cough response to tracheo-bronchial wall stimulation.

If any test cannot be performed Severe facial and neck trauma may limit interpretation of all brainstem reflexes. If any test from a–g cannot be performed, ancillary testing is strongly recommended. A "Lazarus sign" — spontaneous motor responses of spinal origin — may be seen.
6Clinical Criterion 3 — Apnoea Test
Protocol
  1. Patient must be in a stable cardiovascular and respiratory state.
  2. Baseline PaCO₂ between 35–45 mmHg.
  3. Pre-oxygenate with 100% O₂ for 10 minutes.
  4. Disconnect from the ventilator.
  5. Deliver 100% O₂ via one of: tracheal catheter (not for paediatrics) · CPAP on the mechanical ventilator · T-piece or resuscitation bag with a functioning PEEP valve connected to the ETT.
  6. Monitor O₂ saturation with pulse oximetry.
  7. Measure PaCO₂ at 5 minutes, and again at approximately 8 minutes if PaCO₂ has not exceeded 60 mmHg.
  8. Reconnect to the ventilator after the test.
  9. Disconnection shall not exceed 10–15 minutes at any one time, provided the patient remains haemodynamically stable.
Result interpretation
Positive No respiratory effort at a PaCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg.
Indeterminate If there is significant hypotension, marked desaturation or unstable cardiac arrhythmias during testing: immediately draw an arterial blood sample, reconnect to the ventilator and analyse the arterial blood gas. If PaCO₂ did not exceed 60 mmHg, the result is indeterminate. It is at the physician's discretion to repeat the test or rely on an ancillary test to finalise the clinical diagnosis.
Chronic lung disease Baseline PaCO₂ may already be above 40 mmHg. The apnoea test is then considered positive if there is no respiratory effort at a PaCO₂ 20 mmHg above the baseline PaCO₂.
7Assessment & Certification — Timing & Time of Death
  • Assessment carried out by two NSR-accredited specialists qualified in diagnosing brain death.
  • Repeat assessment and certification at least 6 hours and up to 24 hours after the first — not necessarily by the same pair of specialists.
  • Paediatric patients: a minimum of 24 hours is required.
Paperwork & time of death
  • The Brain Death Certification form is filled by the first set of doctors (A and B) and completed by the second set (C and D) — or by A and B if the same doctors perform the second test.
  • Time of death is declared by the doctors performing the second test.
  • If the second test cannot be carried out 6 hours later, the time of death is when the second test is completed.
  • If the patient develops asystole before the second test, the time of death is taken at the point of asystole.
  • If an ancillary test is required, clinical tests that can be performed must still be performed twice as recommended. Certification of death follows the second clinical test, taking into account both clinical and ancillary tests.
8Paediatric & Neonatal Considerations
  • No recommendations can be made for preterm infants < 37 weeks of gestational age.
  • For term newborns or older: ancillary tests are required when component(s) of the clinical examination and apnoea test cannot be fully completed (e.g. cyanotic heart disease, uncertainty of clinical findings, or suspected medication effects interfering with evaluation).

9Brain Death Certification in Pregnant Women
  • The same preconditions and exclusion criteria apply.
  • Brain death testing in a pregnant patient is the same as for adults.
  • Somatic support may be continued in the presence of a viable foetus (> 24 weeks gestation) in consultation with immediate family members and with approval of the hospital ethics committee, with every effort made to prevent harmful effects to the foetus.
10Pitfalls — Movements, Drugs & Acid–Base
1. Spontaneous and reflex movements in brain death

These are spinal in origin and do not preclude the diagnosis of brain death. The most common are finger jerks. Also seen: undulating toe flexion sign (snapping the big toe leads to undulating movement of the other toes), triple flexion response, persistent Babinski response, Lazarus sign, pronation–extension reflex, and facial myokymia.

They generally occur during the apnoea test, or are triggered by forceful flexion of the neck, rotation of the body, or by the ventilator synchronous with pulmonary insufflation. They do not occur spontaneously.

2. Common confounders to remember
  1. Severe facial trauma.
  2. Pre-existing pupillary abnormalities.
  3. Toxic levels of sedative drugs, aminoglycosides, tricyclic antidepressants, anticonvulsants, chemotherapeutic drugs, neuromuscular blocking agents.
  4. Sleep apnoea or severe pulmonary disease resulting in chronic retention of carbon dioxide.
  5. Spontaneous and reflex movements (see above).
3. Drugs — a reasonable approach
  • If screening tests reveal traces of a drug below the therapeutic range → brain death can be determined.
  • If the drug or poison cannot be quantified but is certain → observe for at least 5 × the elimination half-life, provided excretion is not affected by other drugs or organ dysfunction.
  • If the drug is unknown but suspicion is high → observe for 48 hours for a change in brainstem reflexes and motor response before proceeding with brain death tests.

Determination of brain death should be deferred in the presence of severe acidosis or alkalosis.

11Ancillary Testing

Ancillary laboratory tests are not usually mandatory. They may be useful when clinical tests cannot be reliably performed, and in certain instances where children are involved. The main objective is to demonstrate the absence of cerebral circulation or absence of cerebral electrical activity.

A. CT Angiography (CTA) — 4-point scoring

Score 1 point for each of the following vessels that does not opacify:

  • Cortical branches of the right middle cerebral artery (MCA)
  • Cortical branches of the left middle cerebral artery (MCA)
  • Right internal cerebral vein (ICV)
  • Left internal cerebral vein (ICV)
Total scoreInterpretation
4 (all absent)Suggests cerebral circulatory arrest
0 (all present)Excludes cerebral circulatory arrest
1–3 (1–3 absent vessels)Inconclusive

Timing is critical An increase in intracranial pressure leading to cerebral circulatory arrest is a major consideration. To avoid false negatives and repetitive examinations, an interval of at least 12 hours after clinical suspicion of brain death before CTA is recommended. False negatives can also occur in craniotomies, children with large fontanelles, or open-skull defects. CTA is the ancillary test of choice for adults and suitable paediatric patients. If indeterminate or negative, repeat CTA after at least 12 hours, or perform an EEG.
B. Conventional angiography (Digital Subtraction Angiography, DSA)
  • If there is no flow seen beyond the skull base after the first run, this indicates absence of cerebral blood circulation — this must be confirmed by a second run, 30 minutes after the first.
  • If both runs show no flow in the intracranial vessels beyond the skull base, absence of cerebral blood circulation is confirmed.

Pitfalls — CTA and DSA
  1. Raised ICP leading to circulatory arrest is a major consideration. Conditions rendering ICP lower than mean arterial pressure (e.g. presence of craniotomy) lead to inconclusive CTA given its high sensitivity for vessel visualisation. If inconclusive, discuss further with the primary team.
  2. False negatives occur if CTA or DSA is performed too early after clinical suspicion of brain death, as ICP may not have risen sufficiently. Observe the 12-hour delay.
  3. There is insufficient evidence to make a consensus recommendation for cerebral CT angiography as an ancillary investigation to support the clinical diagnosis of brain death in patients receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).
C. Nuclear imaging (radionuclide CBF study)
  • Tc-99m HMPAO (technetium-99m radiolabelled hexamethyl-propylene amine oxime) is the widely accepted study.
  • It crosses the blood–brain barrier and is retained by parenchyma after conversion from a lipophilic to a hydrophilic form.
  • Classic appearance of a positive study: "hollow skull phenomenon" or "hot nose sign" — due to absent brain circulation with preservation of external carotid flow.

E. Paediatric ancillary testing — radiology
  • Generally, paediatric patients do not require ancillary studies to establish brain death, and ancillary studies do not substitute for neurological examination.
  • May be useful at 37 weeks of gestation to 30 days of life when concern exists about the validity of the clinical examination — but is less sensitive in this age group than in older children.
  • Requests for patients aged 31 days to 18 years may be made when: component(s) of the examination or apnoea test cannot be safely completed; results of the examination cannot be established; medication(s) interfere with evaluation/examination; or the observation period has to be shortened.
  • Available modalities: conventional angiography, nuclear medicine, cross-sectional studies (CTA and MRI), and transcranial doppler ultrasound. The Paediatric AAP 2011 guidelines have not recommended MR angiography or transcranial doppler as ancillary studies.
  • Radionuclide CBF study: a portable radionuclide camera is not available in Malaysia and transfer of young or unstable children to Nuclear Medicine imposes safety issues. This modality is reserved for hospitals where a static machine is available — transportation of ill children to another facility is not advocated.
12Who Certifies & Where
Qualification of doctors certifying brain death
  • Each hospital is recommended to have a subcommittee to appoint and review doctors authorised to certify brain death in that hospital.
  • Two specialists are considered qualified if they are NSR-accredited and have clinical experience in conducting brain death assessment.
  • They should preferably be: anaesthesiologists, paediatric anaesthesiologists, intensivists, paediatric intensivists, neurologists, paediatric neurologists, neurosurgeons, paediatric neurosurgeons, or paediatric internal medicine specialists.
  • Hospitals without qualified specialists must liaise with a relevant clinician from another tertiary hospital for further advice and management.
  • Doctors involved in organ transplantation are not allowed to be involved in brain death certification.
Criteria for hospitals performing certification

Brain death certification must be done in areas of the hospital with full facilities for intensive cardiopulmonary care of comatose patients, which include ventilation, cardiac monitor and blood gas analysis machine.

Committee & governance
  • All hospitals where brain death certification is carried out shall have a committee functioning as a coordinating body, responsible for general policies, training and accrediting staff, counselling, and overseeing the facilities available.
  • The brain death guideline shall be reviewed every 5–10 years as necessary to accommodate new knowledge and latest clinical practice.
