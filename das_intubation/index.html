2025 Core Principle — Maximise Success, Not Just Failure Management Every airway plan prioritises continuous oxygen delivery (peroxygenation), first-attempt intubation success, uninterrupted waveform capnography to exclude oesophageal intubation, early priming for eFONA, and explicit verbal declaration when transitioning between plans.
1. Airway Assessment, Strategy & Preparation Figure 2 · Pre-induction

Preparation & Strategy Decision Flow (Figure 2)

Step 1: Pre-induction Assessment

History (prior difficulty, OSA/snoring), bedside tests (Upper Lip Bite Test + combined tests), difficult FMV screen, CTM localisation in full neck extension, and physiological difficulty screen.

Step 2: Difficulty Anticipated?

Assess difficulty across Plan A (intubation), Plan B (SAD), Plan C (FMV), or Plan D (eFONA). If ANY difficulty is anticipated → Consider Awake Tracheal Intubation (ATI) or awake tracheostomy.

Step 3: Team Brief & Setup

Share strategy with anaesthetic assistant & theatre team brief. Optimise position before pre-oxygenation. Ensure Plans A–D kit is immediately available. Plan out-of-hours/non-theatre transfers early.

Key Assessment Predictors

  • Previous difficult airway history: Most reliable predictor; check alert cards and medical records.
  • Upper Lip Bite Test (ULBT): Highest diagnostic accuracy among bedside tests; combining multiple bedside tests improves sensitivity.
  • OSA & snoring: More reliable predictors of difficult airway management.
  • Difficult facemask ventilation features: Prior difficulty, OSA, neck radiotherapy, high BMI, increased neck circumference, Mallampati 3/4, male sex.
  • Cricothyroid membrane (CTM): Identify visually, by palpation, or by point-of-care ultrasound with the neck fully extended to inform Plan D.

Investigations & Scoring Tools

  • Nasendoscopy: Rapid, safe, easily performed bedside assessment with significant value in guiding strategy.
  • Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS): Pre-induction CTM/cricoid localisation and gastric aspiration risk assessment.
  • Other imaging: CT, virtual endoscopy, awake videolaryngoscopic assessment, 3D printing where indicated.
  • Validated scoring systems: HEAVEN criteria (pre-hospital), MACOCHA score (critical care), Lim & Nielsen grading (facemask ventilation).
  • Emergency checklists: Use a checklist for emergency tracheal intubation to reduce omissions and improve adherence.
Out-of-Hours & Non-Theatre Environments Airway management outside operating theatres or out of hours carries elevated risk. Explicitly plan timing, clinical urgency, whether to transfer the patient to an anaesthetic room/theatre, early senior input, and allocation of a dedicated team member (not the airway assistant) to summon help.
2. Monitoring, Pharmacology & Peroxygenation Continuous Oxygenation & NMB

Essential Monitoring Standards

  • Pre-induction capnography check: Verify waveform capnography is working and correctly interpretable before induction.
  • Uninterrupted waveform capnography: Gold standard throughout all phases; most sensitive and specific monitor to exclude oesophageal intubation.
  • Do NOT rely on clinical signs alone: Tube misting, chest rise, and auscultation are unreliable in isolation.
  • Pre-oxygenation efficacy: Target ETO2 ≥ 0.9 on facemask (less reliable when using HFNO).
  • Situational awareness triggers: Enable audible SpO2 tones before induction; start a timer at NMB administration.
  • Quantitative NMB monitoring: Use to confirm adequate neuromuscular block before intubation attempts and before emergence/extubation.

Peroxygenation Strategy

  • Definition: Continuous O2 delivery from pre-oxygenation, through apnoea, and throughout laryngoscopy until the airway is secured.
  • Optimal pre-oxygenation: Head-up position (≥30°) combined with positive pressure (HFNO, NIV, or facemask CPAP) maximises functional residual capacity (FRC).
  • High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO >30 L/min): Prolongs safe apnoea, particularly in anatomically or physiologically difficult airways. Requires a patent upper airway.
  • HFNO limitations: Delays rather than guarantees prevention of hypoxaemia; some cannulae must be removed to achieve an effective facemask seal; never initiate HFNO as a rescue technique after facemask ventilation has failed.

Induction Agents & Neuromuscular Blockade

  • Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBs): Use routinely to facilitate tracheal intubation; improves conditions for intubation, SAD ventilation, facemask ventilation, and eFONA. Do not delay NMB administration to check facemask ventilation.
  • Induction agents: Propofol provides favourable intubating conditions, but can precipitate cardiovascular collapse in physiologically difficult airways; consider ketamine, etomidate, or remimazolam in high-risk patients and prepare vasopressors regardless of agent.
  • Awareness risk: A single bolus of propofol may be insufficient during prolonged difficult airway management; maintain anaesthesia throughout attempts.
  • Inhalation induction in adults: Unreliable and associated with substantial risks in anticipated difficult airways.
Critical Warning — Sugammadex in CICO Reversing rocuronium with sugammadex during a “cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate” (CICO) crisis is not a reliable rescue strategy. It does not guarantee a patent upper airway, risks laryngospasm or pulmonary aspiration, and delays immediate airway rescue. If sugammadex has been administered and eFONA is required, use a non-steroidal NMB (other than rocuronium or vecuronium).
3. Plan A: Tracheal Intubation & Facemask Ventilation Figure 3 · Max 3+1 Attempts

Plan A Execution & Escalation Flow (Figure 3)

1. First-Line Approach

Ensure NMB + head-up/ramped position + continuous peroxygenation. Use videolaryngoscopy first-line whenever possible. With hyperangulated blade: use shaped stylet, bougie, or flexible bronchoscope.

2. If Difficulty Occurs (Max 3+1)

Oxygenate via FMV between attempts; maintain anaesthesia. Make an active change each attempt (position, blade type/size, introducer, BURP, suction, remove cricoid force, optimise NMB, change operator; 4th attempt only by more experienced colleague).

Success → Two-Point Confirmation

Confirm BOTH: (1) Visualisation of tube passing through vocal cords (where possible) AND (2) Sustained exhaled CO2 on waveform capnography.

Failure → Declare & Prime

Declare “Failed Intubation” (can be declared after 1st attempt if futile). Summon help, prime eFONA kit (immediately accessible + assign eFONA operator), proceed to Plan B.

Videolaryngoscopy & Introducers

  • Videolaryngoscope first-line: Increases first-attempt success, reduces hypoxaemia, and lowers oesophageal intubation risk across operators and settings.
  • Blade awareness: Macintosh-type vs hyperangulated blades require distinct insertion and tube-delivery techniques.
  • Introducer safety: Never perform blind bougie insertion (Cormack–Lehane Grade 3 or 4); avoid eliciting the bronchial “hold-up” sign due to airway perforation/trauma risk.
  • External laryngeal manipulation (BURP): Backward, upward, rightward pressure on the thyroid cartilage by a trained assistant improves glottic view.

Facemask Ventilation Between Attempts

  • High-risk signal: Difficult facemask ventilation increases failed tracheal intubation risk >10-fold.
  • Optimisation techniques: Correctly sized mask with airtight seal, increased fresh gas flow, chin lift/jaw thrust, oropharyngeal/nasopharyngeal airways, and VE two-handed grip (more effective than CE two-handed grip).
  • Early warning signs: Increased airway pressure, reduced tidal volume, loss of seal, deteriorating capnography, or rising operator effort signal pending failure of Plans A–C — verbalise immediately and call for help.
Plan A Abandonment & Early Discontinuation If hypoxaemia occurs during laryngoscopy, abandon the attempt immediately and prioritise facemask ventilation. Failure of Plan A may be declared at any stage (even after attempt 1) if conditions cannot be improved or further instrumentation risks trauma. If discontinuing anaesthesia at this point, maintain effective FMV or insert a SAD until full NMB reversal and emergence.
4. Plan B: Supraglottic Airway Device (SAD) Rescue Figure 4 · Max 3 Attempts

Plan B Rescue & Decision Tree (Figure 4)

1. Insert 2nd-Generation SAD (Max 3 Tries)

Use 2nd-gen SAD (higher seal, gastric drainage port, intubation conduit). Remove cricoid force if applied. Change size/device and ensure full NMB between attempts; FMV between tries. Confirm ventilation with waveform capnography + SpO2.

Oxygenation Established → Stop, Think, Communicate
  • Default option: Discontinue anaesthesia & wake patient up (safest).
  • High-risk (senior input): Proceed with surgery via SAD (if life-threatening/essential).
  • High-risk (1 attempt only): Intubate via SAD under visualisation (flexible bronchoscope ± Aintree catheter). NO blind intubation.
  • Controlled FONA: Senior surgical/anaesthetic tracheostomy or cricothyroidotomy.
Plan B Fails → Open eFONA Kit

If oxygenation fails after max 3 attempts (or earlier): Declare “Failed SAD Ventilation”, open the eFONA kit, summon urgent help, and proceed to Plan C. Prepare for high likelihood of Plan D.

Device Selection & Efficacy

  • Second-generation SADs: Mandatory over first-generation devices; rescue failed intubation in ~60–65% of cases.
  • Cuffed 2nd-gen SADs: Offer higher oropharyngeal leak pressures and allow cuff pressure adjustment.
  • Attempt discipline: Repeated insertion causes airway oedema/trauma and delays Plan C; limit strictly to 3 attempts.

Safety Rules for Proceeding or Intubating via SAD

  • Waking up: Guide full reversal with quantitative NMB monitoring; remain vigilant for laryngospasm on emergence.
  • Proceeding with SAD in situ: Optimise cuff pressure, pass nasogastric tube to decompress stomach, optimise head/neck position & NMB, expedite safe surgery, and prepare a failure plan.
  • Intubation through SAD: Only one attempt if patient stable, oxygenation adequate, and team trained; blind intubation through a SAD is not recommended.
5. Plan C: Final Attempt at Facemask Ventilation Figure 5 · Pre-CICO Bridge

Plan C Final Optimisation & CICO Transition (Figure 5)

Apply All 5 Mandatory Optimisations Simultaneously
  1. Ensure adequate neuromuscular block
  2. Ensure patient positioning is optimised
  3. Insert airway adjuncts (oropharyngeal, nasopharyngeal, or both)
  4. Use a four-handed (two-person) technique
  5. Ensure adequate depth of anaesthesia
FMV Successful (Capnography + SpO2 Maintained/Improving)

Stop, think, and communicate with senior clinicians. Options: full NMB reversal and careful emergence (monitor closely as reversal can worsen obstruction) OR proceed to controlled FONA.

FMV Unsuccessful → Declare CICO

Immediately declare “Cannot Intubate, Cannot Oxygenate” (CICO), allocate team roles (operator, assistant, upper airway O2), and proceed immediately to Plan D (eFONA).

Clinical Vigilance in Plan C Repeated intubation and SAD instrumentation may have caused airway oedema and bleeding, making facemask ventilation much harder than at induction. At this stage, clinical signs may be the only available marker of effective oxygenation alongside capnography and SpO2. Do not persist with failing mask ventilation — rapid transition to Plan D saves brain and life.
6. Plan D: Emergency Front-of-Neck Airway (eFONA / CICO) Figures 6 & 7 · CICO Action
Immediate CICO Action — Eliminate Choice Under Stress When Plans A, B, and C have failed, CICO exists: hypoxic brain injury or death will ensue without immediate eFONA. Default incision rule: If the cricothyroid membrane (CTM) was not explicitly assessed, is impalpable, or there is ANY uncertainty, perform a vertical skin incision as default.

Plan D Readiness & Execution Checklist (Figure 6)

1. Allocate Roles & Position

Senior team member allocates: (a) eFONA operator, (b) operator’s assistant, (c) upper airway O2 provider. Achieve maximal neck extension (pillow under shoulders or drop head of bed).

2. NMB & Upper Airway O2

Confirm full neuromuscular block (never attempt eFONA without full NMB; use non-steroidal NMB if sugammadex given). Apply 100% O2 via upper airway throughout (SAD, facemask, or nasal cannula).

3. Equipment Ready

No. 10 scalpel blade, bougie, size 6.0 mm cuffed tracheal tube, and suction. (Note: Alternative scalpel–bougie–tube techniques such as right-to-right cephalad-to-caudad are also acceptable if trained.)

Figure 7: Vertical Scalpel–Bougie–Tube Sequence (19 Steps)

Phase 1–2: Incision & Dissection (Steps 1–6) Midline vertical skin incision up to 8 cm (caudad to cephalad); blunt finger dissection to locate CTM.
Phase 3: CTM Stab & Bougie (Steps 7–14) Transverse CTM stab, rotate 90° caudally, swap hands, slide coudé bougie 10–15 cm, remove scalpel.
Phase 4: Intubate & Confirm (Steps 15–19) Rotate & railroad 6.0 mm tube, remove bougie, inflate cuff, ventilate 100% O2, confirm capnography, secure.

Phase 1: Positioning & Skin Incision

  1. 1Stand on the patient’s left-hand side if right-handed (reverse if left-handed).
  2. 2Identify laryngeal anatomy in maximum neck extension; locate midline with non-dominant hand.
  3. 3Apply tension to the skin and stabilise the larynx with the non-dominant hand.
  4. 4Make a midline vertical skin incision up to 8 cm in length, caudad to cephalad (bottom to top).
  5. 5Use blunt dissection with fingers of both hands to separate tissues and stabilise larynx.
  6. 6Use the non-dominant index finger to identify the cricothyroid membrane (CTM).

Phase 2: CTM Incision & Bougie

  1. 7Hold No. 10 scalpel in dominant hand; make a transverse stab incision through CTM with cutting edge facing towards you.
  2. 8Keep scalpel perpendicular to skin and turn 90° so sharp edge points caudally (towards the feet).
  3. 9Swap hands; hold the scalpel with your non-dominant hand.
  4. 10Maintain gentle lateral traction pulling scalpel towards you, handle vertical to skin (not slanted).
  5. 11Pick up the bougie with your dominant hand.
  6. 12Slide the coudé tip of the bougie down the side of the scalpel blade into the trachea.
  7. 13Rotate and align the bougie with the trachea and advance gently to 10–15 cm.
  8. 14Remove the scalpel.

Phase 3: Tube Railroading & Post-eFONA

  1. 15Stabilise trachea, apply skin tension, and hold bougie with non-dominant hand.
  2. 16Railroad a size 6.0 mm cuffed tracheal tube over the bougie with dominant hand, rotating as advanced.
  3. 17Remove the bougie.
  4. 18Inflate cuff, ventilate with 100% oxygen, and confirm with waveform capnography.
  5. 19Secure the tube.
After eFONA Exclude bronchial intubation and pneumothorax. Arrange surgical review for definitive management. If eFONA fails: extend incision, perform further blunt dissection, change patient/operator position, change operator, or use a smaller tube. Provide debriefing and psychological support to all staff involved.

Dosing per DAS 2025 Table 1 (no latency between induction agent and NMB; adjust dosing for patient physiology).

  • Cricoid force: Classically 10 N before induction → 30 N after loss of consciousness by a trained assistant. Use in patients at particularly high risk of pulmonary aspiration. Remove cricoid force immediately if laryngoscopy view is poor, when inserting a SAD in Plan B, if intubation difficulty occurs, or if active vomiting occurs.
  • Aspiration risk & stomach prep: Assess risk (history, exam, point-of-care gastric ultrasound); consider prokinetics/antacids; suction existing NG tube or consider insertion; keep suction on and at hand.
  • Positioning & ventilation: Head-up ≥30°; ETO2 ≥0.9 (consider HFNO); gentle low-pressure mask ventilation or apnoeic oxygenation if required; videolaryngoscopy with bougie or stylet prepared.

Physiologically Difficult Airway

Applies to physiological/pathophysiological states (e.g. older age, pregnancy, sepsis, cardiac failure) increasing risks of hypoxaemia, aspiration, and peri-intubation cardiovascular collapse.

  • Pre-induction optimisation: Correct fluid status, prepare vasopressors, and select induction agents carefully (propofol caution; consider ketamine, etomidate, or remimazolam).
  • Dedicated haemodynamic lead: Nominate a team member to independently monitor and manage haemodynamic status so the airway manager can focus on intubation.
  • First-attempt urgency: Multiple attempts sharply raise adverse events; prioritise positive-pressure pre-oxygenation and peroxygenation.

Patients Living with Obesity

Higher risk of difficult FMV, SAD ventilation, intubation, FONA, and rapid haemoglobin desaturation (higher basal metabolic rate, reduced FRC).

  • Consider Awake Tracheal Intubation (ATI) and performing airway management in the operating theatre.
  • Pre-oxygenate head-up (≥30°) and use HFNO for peroxygenation.
  • Early 2nd-generation SAD: Consider early SAD ventilation if facemask ventilation is suboptimal.
  • Call for help earlier than in non-obese patients.
8. Emergence, Human Factors, POCUS, Documentation & Training Non-Technical & Governance

Post-Intubation Care, Reversal & Emergence

  • Quantitative NMB monitoring: Use to guide maintenance, ensure complete reversal before emergence/extubation, and manage controlled emergence when discontinuing anaesthesia after failed Plan A, B, or C.
  • Emergence vigilance: Watch closely for laryngospasm or worsening upper airway obstruction after reversal of neuromuscular block.
  • Post-eFONA checks: Exclude bronchial intubation and pneumothorax; obtain surgical review.
  • Structured handover: Communicate airway events and ongoing plan to PACU, ward, or critical care staff.

Human Factors & Team Dynamics

  • Priming: Plan A fails → ensure eFONA kit is immediately accessible & identify eFONA operator. Plan B fails (3 SAD tries) → assistant opens eFONA kit.
  • Transitioning: Counteract task fixation using attempt limits, NMB timer, and physiological triggers. Declare every transition aloud to the team.
  • Calling for help: Driven by clinical scenario, not seniority; assign a non-assistant team member at the team brief when difficulty is anticipated.
  • Airway assistant: Empowered to confirm sustained exhaled CO2, track time/attempts, prompt transitions, and apply graded assertiveness (e.g. PACE).

Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS)

  • CTM & cricoid localisation: Pre-induction sonographic marking (with neck extended) is significantly more accurate than digital palpation.
  • Gastric ultrasound: Assesses pulmonary aspiration risk (e.g. non-fasted patients, emergency surgery, GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy).
  • Confirmation caveat: Tracheal ultrasound can confirm tube placement in studies, but is time-consuming and may distract from immediate care compared with capnography + visualisation.

Documentation, M&M & Training

  • Clinical documentation: Record assessment, devices/techniques, difficulties (distinguish training use vs actual/anticipated difficulty), and outcomes.
  • Patient & GP communication: Inform patient verbally and in writing; notify primary care using SNOMED CT 718446005; enter into national registries (DAS Difficult Airway Registry / DAS eFONA Registry).
  • Education & M&M: Multidisciplinary simulation, tea trolley training, capnography training for all staff, regular eFONA rehearsal, psychological support after CICO, and M&M review of every failed intubation and eFONA.
