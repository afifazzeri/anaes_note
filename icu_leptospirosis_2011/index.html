1 · Overview, transmission & risk Basics
  • Zoonosis caused by pathogenic spirochaetes of the genus Leptospira (L. interrogans; >200 serovars in 25 serogroups).
  • Incubation period: usually 10 days (range 2–30 days).
  • Transmission: contact of skin, mucosa or conjunctiva with water or soil contaminated by the urine of infected rodents, carrier or diseased animals. Ingestion of contaminated water may also cause infection. No documented human-to-human transmission.
  • Seasonality: usually seasonal, beginning with the rainy season and declining as rainfall recedes; outbreaks are associated with heavy rainfall and flooding. Sporadic cases occur year-round.
  • Severity: case-fatality estimates worldwide range from <5% to 30%. Severe leptospirosis is commonly fatal if aggressive management is not instituted early.
  • Notifiable in Malaysia under the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases Act 1988 since 9 December 2010.

High-risk groups (exposure history to elicit)

  • Agricultural workers, sewerage workers, livestock handlers, pet shop workers
  • Military personnel, search & rescue workers, disaster relief workers (e.g. during floods)
  • Outdoor/recreational activities — water recreation, jungle trekking, adventure trips, outdoor sports
  • Travellers not previously exposed to the bacteria in their environment
  • People with chronic disease and open skin wounds
History is paramount. The guideline states that leptospirosis is difficult to distinguish from other diseases on clinical grounds alone — a history of possible exposure is essential to aid clinical diagnosis.
2 · Clinical manifestations & red flags Recognise

Clinical manifestations are highly variable. The guideline describes four broad clinical categories:

  1. A mild, influenza-like illness (ILI)
  2. Weil's syndrome — jaundice, renal failure, haemorrhage and myocarditis with arrhythmias
  3. Meningitis / meningoencephalitis
  4. Pulmonary haemorrhage with respiratory failure
Differential trap — "the Great Mimicker". Confusion is particularly common in the tropics with dengue and other haemorrhagic fevers, malaria, typhoid, melioidosis and influenza. Presentations may also overlap as the infection progresses.

Red flags for critical care review

  • Airway / Neuro Meningeal irritation, meningoencephalitis, altered mental status
  • Respiratory Pulmonary haemorrhage with respiratory failure

  • Haemorrhagic Intestinal and pulmonary bleeding
  • Cardiac Arrhythmia or cardiac failure (myocarditis)
  • Hepatic Jaundice (Weil's syndrome)
3 · Case classification (clinical / probable / confirmed) Definitions
ClassificationCriteria
Clinical case
(suspected)		 Acute febrile illness with a history of exposure to water and/or an environment possibly contaminated with infected animal urine, with ANY of the symptoms/signs listed in section 2 (headache; myalgia of calf/lumbar region; arthralgia; conjunctival suffusion; meningeal irritation; anuria/oliguria and/or proteinuria; jaundice; haemorrhages from intestines and lungs; cardiac arrhythmia or failure; skin rash; GI symptoms).
Probable case A clinical case AND a positive ELISA / other rapid test.
Confirmed case A suspected OR probable case with any one of:
  • MAT — single serum titre ≥ 1:400
  • MAT paired sera — four-fold or greater rise in titre
  • Positive PCR (sample taken within 10 days of disease onset)
  • Positive culture for pathogenic leptospires — blood within 7 days of onset; urine after the 10th day
  • Demonstration of leptospires in tissues using immunohistochemical staining (e.g. post-mortem cases)
  • Where laboratory capacity is not well established: positive by two different rapid diagnostic tests

Cases that require confirmation: all hospitalised cases and all suspected leptospirosis death cases.

4 · Laboratory diagnosis & timing windows Diagnostics

Biphasic illness vs. diagnostic yield

Week 1 — acute / leptospiraemic phase

  • Blood & CSF culture — best yield
  • PCR on whole blood / urine — best yield
  • Serology (ELISA / MAT) may still be negative
➜

Days 5–10 onward — immune phase

  • IgM appears → ELISA / rapid test turns positive
  • MAT titre rises
  • Urine culture from day ≥ 7
  • Severe syndromes declare themselves (Weil's, meningitis, pulmonary haemorrhage)
➜

Convalescent sample (≥ 1 week later)

  • Repeat serum for four-fold rise in MAT titre
  • Follow-up samples give epidemiological information (presumptive infecting serogroup)

Key laboratory notes

  • MAT is the gold standard / cornerstone of serodiagnosis because of its unsurpassed diagnostic (serovar/serogroup) specificity compared with other currently available tests. A second serum sample must be taken to detect a four-fold or greater rise in titre.
  • Rapid test kits: any leptospirosis rapid test kit used must be validated / approved by IMR. Where laboratory capacity is limited, two different rapid tests positive can confirm a case.
  • ELISA / other rapid tests detect IgM. Presence of IgM may indicate current or recent infection. Serum may be positive 5–10 days after onset but usually not before. IgM-class antibodies may remain detectable for several years.
  • If the initial sample was taken early, the ELISA may be positive but MAT negative — a follow-up sample is required.
  • Tests may be negative if the infecting serogroup does not react with the Patoc 1 serovar strain used as antigen.
  • Antibiotics given from the beginning of the illness may delay the immune and antibody response.
  • Culture sensitivity is usually <30%; it is not useful to guide management — it is done mainly for epidemiological surveillance. Isolation followed by typing informs which leptospires are circulating in an area.
  • Leptospires die quickly in urine — a clean urine sample should be inoculated into appropriate culture medium not more than 2 hours after voiding. Survival in acid urine may be increased by making it neutral.
  • Isolation is also possible from blood (first 7 days) or CSF (days 4–10) during acute illness, from urine (days ≥ 7) and from tissue samples using special media. Inoculation of young guinea pigs, hamsters or gerbils can also be carried out.
  • Post-mortem diagnosis: in addition to serology and culture, leptospires can be demonstrated in tissues using PCR or immunohistochemical staining, notably by direct immunofluorescence.

5 · Treatment Antibiotics
Early treatment with antibiotics is essential.

Adults

SeverityRegimen
Severe cases High-dose IV C-penicillin 2 million units 6-hourly for 5–7 days
Less severe cases Oral antibiotics such as doxycycline 2 mg/kg up to 100 mg 12-hourly for 5–7 days; tetracycline, ampicillin or amoxicillin
Alternatives Third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone and cefotaxime, and quinolone antibiotics, may also be effective
Supportive Monitoring and supportive care as appropriate, e.g. dialysis, mechanical ventilation
Jarisch–Herxheimer reactions may occur after the start of antimicrobial therapy. It has also been described in patients with leptospirosis in the paediatric table.

6 · ICU & perioperative considerations Critical care

The 2011 guideline is not an anaesthesia text. The points below organise its clinical content for critical care use; items marked inferred are clinical inferences drawn from the guideline's severity categories and case definition, not verbatim recommendations.

Severe phenotypes to anticipate on ICU admission

  • Weil's syndrome — jaundice + renal failure + haemorrhage + myocarditis with arrhythmias. Anticipate dialysis (the guideline explicitly lists dialysis under supportive care).
  • Pulmonary haemorrhage with respiratory failure — anticipate mechanical ventilation (explicitly listed under supportive care).
  • Meningitis / meningoencephalitis — meningeal irritation is a case-definition criterion; inferred consider impaired consciousness and airway protection.
  • Myocarditis with arrhythmias or cardiac failure — continuous rhythm awareness; cardiovascular instability may complicate induction and positive-pressure ventilation.

Organ support explicitly named in the guideline

  • Dialysis
  • Mechanical ventilation
  • "Monitoring and supportive care as appropriate"

Practical points for the perioperative / ICU team

  • Antibiotics are time-critical — "early treatment with antibiotics is essential". Do not delay definitive therapy while awaiting MAT confirmation.
  • Be ready for a Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction after the first antimicrobial dose — it is explicitly flagged in the guideline for both adults and children.
  • Interpret serology with the timeline in mind. An early sample can be ELISA-positive but MAT-negative; a follow-up sample is required. Antibiotics started at the outset may delay the immune/antibody response.
  • Do not rely on culture to guide management — sensitivity is usually <30% and culture is performed mainly for epidemiological surveillance.
  • Collect the convalescent serum at least 1 week later — confirmation of hospitalised cases depends on it.
  • Any suspected leptospirosis death requires laboratory confirmation; post-mortem PCR and immunohistochemistry on tissues are available (IMR).
  • inferred Severe disease is commonly fatal if aggressive management is not instituted at an early stage — a low threshold for ICU referral and organ-support planning is appropriate.
  • inferred Occupational/recreational exposure history, water and flood exposure, and rodent contact should be actively elicited if not already documented — it is "paramount to aid clinical diagnosis".
Fluid, electrolyte and renal considerations: the guideline lists anuria or oliguria and/or proteinuria, and renal failure as part of the clinical picture and Weil's syndrome respectively, with dialysis as supportive care. Beyond this the source document does not specify fluid or electrolyte targets.
7 · Prophylaxis & post-exposure therapy Prevention
The cost-effectiveness and risk-versus-benefit of antibiotic prophylaxis for leptospirosis remains unclear.

Pre-exposure prophylaxis

May be considered for people at high risk of exposure to potentially contaminated sources — e.g. soldiers going into jungles, rescue teams, persons involved in activities in possible high-risk areas such as adventure sports.

DrugDoseNotes
Doxycycline 200 mg stat dose, then weekly throughout the stay For pregnant women and those allergic to doxycycline, use azithromycin. Benefit remains controversial — balance against potential side effects (e.g. doxycycline-induced photosensitivity, nausea).
Azithromycin 500 mg stat dose, then weekly throughout the stay

Empirical treatment for post-exposure

In an outbreak there may be a role for post-exposure prophylaxis for those exposed to a common source as the index case.

8 · Prevention & exposure advice Counselling

Prevention and control should be targeted at (a) the infection source, (b) the route of transmission between the infection source and the human host, or (c) prompt and proper treatment of infection.

Advice for persons with occupational or recreational exposure to potentially contaminated water or soil

  • Wear waterproof protective clothing such as rubber boots and gloves.
  • Cover skin lesions with waterproof dressings.
  • Wash with clean water immediately after exposure.
  • Seek immediate medical treatment if symptoms develop within the incubation period.

General public messages

  • Keep homes and premises free from rodents.
  • Vaccinate pets against leptospirosis.
  • Promote cleanliness at recreational areas, food premises and housing areas.
  • Interagency collaboration (local authorities, PERHILITAN, JPV, JLN, etc.) to maintain cleanliness, especially rodent control.
