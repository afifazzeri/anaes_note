Paediatric Obesity

Anaesthesia & Critical Care — Quick Reference

Adapted from ATOTW 581 · Anaesthesia Tutorial of the Week · WFSA

▶1 · Key Points
  • Paediatric obesity is a growing problem.
  • The definition of childhood obesity uses age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles (WHO z-scores / CDC percentiles) — not absolute BMI numbers.
  • Children living with obesity are more likely than non-obese children to require surgical intervention.
  • Anaesthesia-related critical incidents are more common in this population.
  • Certain drugs must be dosed according to either the ideal (IBW), adjusted (AdjBW) or total (TBW) body weight.
  • The surgical encounter offers a potential teachable moment to address the causes of obesity and promote health.
▶2 · Definitions & Aetiology

Defining childhood obesity

  • Both the WHO and the US CDC classify BMI into Z scores and percentiles respectively, rather than absolute numbers.
  • Reason: children are still growing and BMI increases non-linearly with age and gender.
  • WHO BMI-for-age (5–19 years) bands: z > +2 = obesity, +1 to +2 = overweight, −2 to +1 = normal, −3 to −2 = thinness, < −3 = severe thinness.

Aetiology

  • In the majority, obesity results from an interaction between predisposing genetic factors, environmental exposures, socio-economic and perinatal factors.
  • Common in high-income countries but has also spread to middle- and low-income areas — mostly due to an obesogenic environment of low-quality diet coupled with poor physical activity.
  • A smaller proportion is secondary to another disease process (e.g. hypothyroidism, Prader–Willi syndrome).

▶4 · Preoperative Assessment — System by System

Airway

  • Adipose tissue around the face and neck may result in difficult bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation, exacerbated by macroglossia and obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA).
  • Difficult BVM ventilation: 3.7% of children with obesity vs 0.6% of children with healthy weight.
  • Obesity is NOT associated with a higher rate of difficult intubation.

Respiratory

  • Increased chest wall weight and abdominal girth impair respiratory mechanics → reduced chest compliance and functional residual capacity (FRC) → hypoventilation; worse in the supine position.
  • Atelectasis from increased closing capacity → shunt and air trapping.
  • Increased metabolic demands → poorly tolerant of apnoea, desaturates rapidly compared with healthy-weight children.
  • More susceptible to respiratory infections; higher prevalence of asthma.
  • 60% of children with obesity have OSA. Commonest cause is tonsillar hypertrophy (historically treated with adenotonsillectomy). Children with obesity also have redundant palatopharyngeal tissue and macroglossia — may be amenable to surgery but also likely to need ongoing non-invasive ventilation (NIV).

Cardiovascular

  • A child with obesity is 3× more likely to develop hypertension than a child with healthy weight — due to increased blood flow required to supply a larger tissue mass, met by increased cardiac output and blood volume.
  • Structural cardiac lesions may arise from sustained hypertension.
  • Long-term consequences of OSA → pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure.
  • Hypertension + dyslipidaemia + T2DM → vascular disease and increased cardiovascular risk. All reversible with weight reduction.

Vitamin DAn inverse relationship exists between body mass and vitamin D in children with obesity — possibly from insufficient intake or sequestration of lipophilic 25-hydroxy-vitamin D in adipose tissue. Low circulating vitamin D is associated with insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidaemia and atherosclerosis. Supplementation does not appear to reverse these cardiovascular and metabolic effects.

Haematological

  • More likely to develop iron-deficiency anaemia.
  • Anaemia may exacerbate symptoms of comorbid cardiovascular or respiratory disease.

Gastrointestinal

  • Increased abdominal pressure contributes to gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) — present in up to 20% of children with obesity.
  • Unclear whether this is associated with delayed gastric emptying and increased aspiration risk. Evidence is mixed and no specific guidelines exist to adjust standard fasting times for children with obesity.

Endocrine

  • At risk of prediabetes and T2DM — an aggressive disease in children with high treatment failure rates.
  • Early comorbidities and complications: non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, dyslipidaemia, polycystic ovary syndrome, nephropathy.

Psychology

  • Increased prevalence of mood, anxiety and other psychiatric disorders.
  • Poor school performance, low self-esteem and social withdrawal are more common.
  • Psychological disorders are also a secondary cause of obesity.
▶5 · Screening, Investigations & Consent

Screening for comorbidities

  • Children living with obesity are at risk of multisystem comorbid disease — refer to relevant specialists for cardiovascular, endocrine or metabolic disorders.
  • Some paediatric surgical centres use standard preoperative blood tests for all children with obesity: FBC, urea and electrolytes, liver function, fasting glucose and HbA1c, thyroid function, fasting lipids, vitamin D level.
  • Polysomnography (PSG) is recommended for all children with obesity presenting for adenotonsillectomy — but practically difficult, as waiting times can be long.

Consent for general anaesthesia

  • Discuss the increased risk of critical airway incidents: airway obstruction, difficult face-mask ventilation and bronchospasm.
  • Language matters. Avoid stigmatising terminology such as fat, obese or morbidly obese. Use neutral terms such as unhealthy weight and BMI.
  • Place the person before the condition: "child living with obesity" is preferred over "obese child".
  • When discussing anaesthesia with young people, invite their input. The Society for Obesity and Bariatric Anaesthesia in the UK (SOBA UK) has produced a consent guideline for children living with obesity.
  • Prepare children and their caregivers for planned modifications to the induction process. For example, if planning to induce on the operating table, explain that they will directly enter the operating room and appreciate that this will likely be an unfamiliar environment.
▶6 · The Teachable Moment
  • The concept of a teachable moment describes a situation likely to motivate positive behavioural change.
  • Children presenting for surgery — and their caregivers — are faced with the increased risk of anaesthesia caused by obesity. This can have a motivational effect.
  • Use the time between preoperative assessment and surgery as an opportunity for preoptimisation: improving diet, activity level and weight.
  • This might require referral to a specialist paediatric weight-loss service, exercise programme, or dietician.
▶7 · Induction of Anaesthesia

Premedication

  • Premedication can be used, but consider dexmedetomidine or ketamine (which preserve respiratory drive), or a reduced dose of benzodiazepine in those with symptoms of OSA — the latter can cause hypoventilation.

Route of induction

  • IV induction is recommended for children living with obesity due to the greater risk of difficult airway management.
  • However, IV access is more likely to be difficult — which can be distressing, especially if multiple attempts are required. Weigh risks and benefits of inhalational vs IV induction.
  • Assess venous access preoperatively, apply topical analgesia, and consider an ultrasound-guided approach. If opting for inhalational induction, consent for multiple attempts at vascular access under anaesthesia.

CautionIV induction frequently induces apnoea. A child with obesity will desaturate quickly once apnoeic — reduced FRC and higher metabolic rate. Desaturation may not be immediately reversible with BVM ventilation, because this is more challenging.

Drug dosing at induction

  • Induction boluses of propofol and ketamine should be dosed according to IBW.
  • IBW is calculated from the child's height and the 50th percentile BMI (BMI50) on a BMI chart for his or her age.

Inhalational induction

  • Can be used, but approach cautiously when difficult BVM ventilation is anticipated; inhalational induction has a slower onset in children with obesity.
  • Administer preoxygenation prior to inhalational induction.
  • Optimal positioning in a ramped position (using pillows, a shoulder roll, or both) will assist with ventilation.
  • Aim to keep the child spontaneously breathing until IV access has been secured.
  • A child with obesity and OSA may obstruct once anaesthetised, leading to desaturation and a light plane of anaesthesia — exacerbating difficult ventilation.
▶8 · Airway Management & Ventilation

Difficult bag-valve-mask ventilation

  • Use an appropriately sized oropharyngeal airway and consider a 2-person ventilation technique.
  • The opening pressure of the lower oesophageal sphincter is ~20 cm H₂O. Ventilation pressures higher than this may distend the stomach with air → decreased pulmonary compliance → worse ventilation.
  • Decompress the stomach with an orogastric tube to remedy this.
  • Gentle pressure over the stomach can also dispel entrained air — but may cause regurgitation if the child has delayed gastric emptying or is not properly fasted.

Airway device choice

KeyEven for minor procedures, it is considered safer to use an endotracheal tube (ETT) rather than a laryngeal mask airway (LMA) in children with obesity. An ETT protects the airway in the presence of GORD, whereas correct LMA sizing is often difficult in a child with obesity → inadequate seal, air in the stomach, increased aspiration risk.

Ventilation strategy

  • Children with obesity are more likely to require positive pressure ventilation (PPV); higher ventilation pressures may be needed due to reduced lung compliance.
  • Use PEEP to prevent basal collapse and compensate for reduced FRC.
  • Use pressure control ventilation, aiming for a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg based on IBW, to limit barotrauma.
  • An ETT optimises conditions for PPV. If the child is spontaneously breathing via a supraglottic airway, use pressure support ventilation and PEEP to avoid hypotension.

Laryngoscopy & supraglottic airways

  • Endotracheal intubation is not more difficult in children living with obesity — but obtaining optimal head positioning for laryngoscopy can be.
  • Video laryngoscopy is recommended as the first-line technique.
  • When a supraglottic airway is appropriate, consider a second-generation LMA and size it based on TBW.
▶9 · Positioning & Equipment

Positioning

  • In addition to the ramped position for induction, consider operating table extenders or straps to ensure the child can be safely and securely positioned throughout the procedure.
  • Use gel pads to cushion pressure points and reduce risk of neuropraxia or pressure damage.
  • Check the maximal weight allowance for patient beds, trolleys and operating tables — especially in adolescents.
  • Consider inducing anaesthesia on the operating table — this reduces at least one patient transfer, likely safer for the patient and healthcare staff.
  • When transfers are unavoidable, use adjuncts such as patient slide boards, sliding sheets and hover mattresses. Request additional staff members to assist with transfers.

CautionIntraoperative changes in position may exacerbate the physiological changes caused by obesity. Supine, lithotomy or Trendelenburg positions amplify reductions in total lung volume, FRC and lung compliance — due to upward pressure of abdominal contents and impeded diaphragmatic excursion.

Monitoring & equipment

  • Use appropriately sized non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) cuffs.
  • The forearm is an alternative to the upper arm for monitoring NIBP, or consider placing an invasive arterial line to ensure accurate monitoring.
▶10 · Venous Thromboembolism Prophylaxis
  • Most paediatric surgical patients are at low risk of developing VTE and therefore do not require thromboprophylaxis.
  • However, two peak incidences of VTE exist in children:
    • Age < 2 years — associated with central venous catheters.
    • Adolescents > 13 years — with other risk factors, including obesity.
  • In adolescents, the risk factors of pregnancy, combined oral contraceptive pill and smoking become relevant.

APAGBIThe Association of Paediatric Anaesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland recommends the use of anti-embolism stockings and intermittent pneumatic compression devices in children > 13 years and > 40 kg undergoing surgery lasting > 60 minutes.

▶11 · Analgesia
  • Some evidence shows children with obesity and overweight are at risk of prolonged postoperative pain.
  • However, those with OSA are more sensitive to opioid-induced hypoventilation and airway obstruction.
  • Therefore, the use of opioid analgesia should be decided on an individual basis, weighing up the risks of hypoventilation and airway obstruction against uncontrolled pain.
  • An opioid patient-controlled analgesic can be used safely, with appropriate dose adjustments.
  • Morphine is dosed according to IBW; alfentanil and fentanyl according to AdjBW.

Opioid-sparing approach

  • Combine multimodal analgesia, regional anaesthesia and non-pharmacological techniques — such as parental presence in the postoperative care unit (PACU).
  • Regional analgesic techniques can provide excellent analgesia whilst reducing the overall requirement for opioids.
  • However, anatomical planes might be more difficult to visualise on ultrasound in a child with excess adipose tissue.
  • Local anaesthetics should be dosed according to IBW.

RememberMany analgesic drugs should be dose-adjusted in children with obesity, using either the calculated IBW or AdjBW — see the drug dosing table.

▶12 · Emergence & Recovery
  • As with induction, take extra care to optimise the conditions for safe emergence.
  • A ramped position is useful in optimising ventilation.
  • Increase the fraction of inspired oxygen to 100% prior to emergence, aiming for an expired oxygen concentration > 85%.
  • Insertion of a bite block or oropharyngeal airway prior to extubation can be useful and may avoid negative pressure pulmonary oedema.
  • Children with obesity should be fully awake prior to extubation to reduce the risk of airway obstruction.
  • If neuromuscular blockade has been used, confirm reversal prior to extubation. Reversal agents neostigmine and sugammadex are both dosed on TBW.
