Anaesthesia for Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

Quick reference for anaesthetists & intensivists · remote-site anaesthesia, physiological responses, drug choice

Combined summary · 2014 tutorial + BJA Education 2022

1 · Key Points & Exam Quick-Fire
  • ECT is a safe and effective treatment for severe medication-resistant depression; also useful in mania and catatonia.
  • Usually undertaken on remote sites, often in elderly patients with significant comorbidity.
  • Cardiovascular and central nervous system responses are the most dangerous physiological effects.
  • Anaesthesia must be of adequate depth without adversely affecting treatment efficacy — most anaesthetic agents are anticonvulsant.
  • There are no absolute contraindications to ECT; all listed contraindications are relative.
  • Relative contraindications: raised intracranial pressure, recent cerebrovascular accident, untreated cerebral aneurysm, myocardial ischaemia or uncontrolled cardiac failure, unstable major fracture, severe osteoporosis.
  • Modified ECT (anaesthesia + muscle relaxant) is the recommended standard; WHO has called for a worldwide ban on unmodified ECT.

2 · Indications & Efficacy

Accepted indications

  • Acute, life-threatening depression — high suicide risk, or very poor food and/or fluid intake.
  • Drug-resistant depression — failure to respond to two medications given at adequate dose for an adequate period, or where treatment is limited by unacceptable side effects. Consider early initiation if there has been a good response previously, or if the patient is known to respond only to ECT.
  • Acute catatonia — where first-line intramuscular benzodiazepines have failed to produce improvement.
  • Mania — where treatment has failed to alleviate the condition or is limited by side effects.

Not generally indicated

  • Schizophrenia — unless there is an affective component.
  • Age < 16 years — ECT is generally not considered.

Efficacy & risk

  • Remission rates for depression of 60–80% can be expected.
  • Significant relapse rate, particularly if left untreated in the first year.
  • Mortality 1 per 73,440 cases (2014 source) — on a par with dental anaesthesia and lower than treatment with antidepressants or psychotherapy. The 2022 review quotes ~1 per 10,000 patients (1 per 80,000 treatments), similar to anaesthesia for minor surgery.
Why it matters clinically: ECT has a quicker onset than other treatment modalities and is potentially life-saving in catatonia — for example when there is no oral intake. Balance the risks of untreated depression against the risks of anaesthesia and ECT.
3 · Treatment Course, Electrode Placement & Seizure Monitoring
  • Given as a course of treatment — most patients require 6–10 treatments, performed twice weekly.
  • Bilateral ECT is used more commonly; electrodes are placed over the poles of the temporal lobes. It is preferred when speed of clinical recovery takes priority.
  • Unilateral ECT is performed on the non-dominant hemisphere and has the advantage of minimising cognitive adverse effects. Used if there are concerns over post-ictal confusion.
  • EEG monitoring is mandatory in the UK. It should show an initially localised seizure that becomes a generalised tonic–clonic seizure within 5–10 seconds.
  • Seizure duration: ideally > 15 s and < 120 s. A seizure > 120 s is classified as "prolonged" and pharmacological agents may be needed to terminate it. (The 2022 review notes optimal duration is unclear; too short — < 10 s — or too long — > 120 s — may reduce clinical efficacy, and some evidence suggests the amount of current delivered matters more than seizure length.)
  • Maintenance ECT is not recommended within NICE guidelines (lack of robust evidence base), but is considered a valid treatment option by the Royal College of Psychiatrists and the American Psychiatric Association for those in whom it is difficult to maintain mood.
  • ECT may be given to patients with epilepsy, but anticonvulsants may raise the baseline seizure threshold, requiring higher energy. ECT is itself an anticonvulsant (it has been used to treat status epilepticus) and seizure thresholds rise through the course.
4 · Physiological Response — Central Nervous System
  • ↑ Intracranial pressure.
  • ↑ Regional cerebral blood flow, cerebral oxygen consumption and glucose utilisation.
  • ↑ Blood–brain barrier permeability.
  • Headache, confusion and transient memory loss are common in the immediate post-treatment period.
  • Conflicting evidence as to whether neuronal cell death occurs, and if so whether it is clinically relevant.
  • Rare neurological complications reported: transient ischaemic deficits, intracranial haemorrhage and cortical blindness. Prolonged seizures or status epilepticus are other complications.

Cognitive adverse effects (the common ones)

  • Disorientation, impaired attention and memory problems are frequent post-ictally.
  • Short-term memory impairment lasting several weeks occurs in more than 50% of patients. It usually resolves within 6 months, but permanent memory loss is possible.
  • Both retrograde and anterograde amnesia can occur.
  • Non-memory cognitive functions (e.g. intelligence, judgement) are unaffected.
  • May be greater in patients with pre-existing dementia.
  • Reduce cognitive effects by altering stimulus intensity or waveform, using unilateral electrode placement, and lengthening the inter-ECT interval.
Intracranial pressure caution: ICP rises with ECT. In patients with an intracranial mass lesion, ICP may be reduced by pretreatment with steroids and diuretics and by hyperventilation before applying the electrical stimulus. Raised ICP is a relative contraindication.
5 · Physiological Response — Cardiovascular & Other Systems

Phase 1 — Parasympathetic discharge (10–15 seconds)

  • Begins with the electrical stimulus.
  • Bradycardia, hypotension, or even asystole may occur.

Phase 2 — Sympathetic response (peaking at 3–5 minutes)

  • Catecholamine release: systolic arterial pressure rises 30–40%, heart rate rises ≥ 20%.
  • Cardiac arrhythmias occasionally occur during this phase.
  • Myocardial oxygen consumption increases, as determined by the rate–pressure product (RPP).
  • RPP increases are more marked with bilateral ECT, in older patients, and during hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia.
  • Simultaneously, seizure activity increases tissue oxygen consumption, potentially reducing myocardial oxygen supply.
  • Myocardial ischaemia and infarction can therefore occur, particularly with pre-existing disease.
  • Left ventricular systolic and diastolic function can remain decreased up to 6 hours after ECT — even in normal hearts.
  • Cardiac rupture has been described (very rare).

Other physiological effects

  • ↑ Intraocular pressure.
  • ↑ Intragastric pressure — does not appear to be clinically significant.

General physical effects

  • During unmodified fits there was a high incidence of fractures and dislocations — now rare with modified ECT.
  • Headache, myalgia (from the seizure or from succinylcholine), drowsiness, weakness, nausea and anorexia — usually mild.
  • Increased salivation.
  • Dental damage to crowns, veneers, bridges, implants or intraosseous denture supports; oral cavity lacerations of tongue and gums.
Recovery-room reality check: the commonest side effects are confusion, agitation, violent behaviour, amnesia, headache, myalgia, and nausea and vomiting. Cardiovascular complications may still occur during recovery.
6 · Mortality & Overall Risk
  • Despite the physiological effects — and frequent use in elderly people with significant comorbidity — ECT is a low-risk procedure.
  • Mortality ~1 per 10,000 patients (1 per 80,000 treatments), similar to anaesthesia for minor surgical procedures.
  • Cardiovascular complications (arrhythmia and myocardial infarction) and, to a lesser extent, pulmonary complications (laryngospasm and aspiration) are the most common causes of death and serious morbidity.
7 · Preoperative Assessment — History, Examination, Investigations

Who assesses, and where

  • An initial medical assessment should be carried out by the attending psychiatrist. Anaesthetic high-risk cases should be referred to the anaesthetic team for assessment and optimisation before the treatment course begins.
  • Assume patients may be poor historians, may have neglected regular medications, and may not have accessed community medical services.
  • Carry out a detailed assessment at the start of a treatment course, then a check for any changes before each subsequent treatment. Repeat the full assessment if there is any change in physical status.

History — focus on

  • Ischaemic heart disease, cardiac failure, severe valvular heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension.
  • Severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, gastro-oesophageal reflux (aspiration risk).
  • Previous anaesthetic history and any known drug allergies.
  • Dentition: caries, loose teeth, and any artificial work — crowns, veneers, bridges, implants, intraosseous denture supports. Document it and warn the patient of the risk of damage.
  • Baseline vital signs and weight.

Examination — seek evidence of

  • Cardiac failure, severe valvular disease, dysrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension.
  • Poor dentition.
  • Dehydration requiring fluid therapy.
  • Airway assessment and aspiration risk.

Investigations

  • Essential: full blood count, urea and electrolytes.
  • Glycosylated haemoglobin in diabetic patients.
  • 12-lead ECG in all patients above the age of 60 years.
  • As indicated: pregnancy testing, sickle cell test, INR.
  • Lithium level if the patient has been on long-term lithium treatment.
  • Other investigations as clinically indicated. The time taken to optimise problems has to be balanced against the urgency of ECT — but "emergency" ECT usually begins within days, not hours.
Remote-site safety (RCoA / AAGBI standards): anaesthesia should be provided by an experienced anaesthetist, assisted by suitably trained operating department practitioners or anaesthetic nurses, with appropriately trained recovery staff. Resuscitation equipment and drugs must be immediately available; monitoring, trained assistance and recovery facilities must meet AAGBI standards; and resources to transfer the patient to critical care must always be in place. Overall responsibility for anaesthetic services for ECT should be allocated to a lead consultant anaesthetist.
ASA III or higher: discussion should precede any decision to provide ECT at a remote site — taking into account the individual patient's needs, the hazards of transfer, and contingency plans for transfer to a critical care facility if needed.
8 · Psychotropic Medications & Anaesthetic Interactions

Psychotropic medications (antipsychotics, mood stabilisers, antidepressants and anxiolytics) have a range of side effects and the potential to interact with anaesthetic agents.

Drug classKey side effects / risksAnaesthetic interaction
Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) Postural hypotension in the elderly; widening of the QRS complex; prolongation of the QT interval. Hypertensive crisis can be precipitated with indirectly acting sympathomimetic drugs. Direct-acting sympathomimetics should be used cautiously.
Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) Profound pressor effect with both direct and indirectly acting sympathomimetic agents. Traditionally stopped at least 2 weeks prior to ECT.
Selective serotonin re-uptake inhibitors (SSRIs) May precipitate SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion). Interact with meperidine and tramadol → serotonin syndrome (hyperreflexia, agitation, hyperthermia).
Lithium Narrow therapeutic index; can trigger nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Check lithium level if on long-term treatment.
Reassuring note from the 2022 review: interactions with anaesthetic drugs are uncommon, as the drugs in question (indirect sympathomimetics, meperidine, tramadol) are not generally required during ECT.
9 · Contraindications

Absolute contraindications

NONE. There are no absolute contraindications to ECT. Every listed contraindication is relative and must be balanced against the risks of untreated mental illness.

Relative contraindications

  • Myocardial infarction within the last 3 months, or unstable angina.
  • Cerebrovascular event within the last 3 months.
  • Raised intracranial pressure or untreated cerebral aneurysm.
  • Unstable major fracture or cervical spine injury; severe osteoporosis.
  • Phaeochromocytoma.
  • Uncontrolled cardiac failure or severe valvular disease.
  • Deep venous thrombosis (until anticoagulated).
  • Retinal detachment or glaucoma.

Devices & implants

  • Cochlear or other brain implants — pose technical challenges; discuss with the neurology team. Unilateral ECT has been achieved successfully in the presence of cochlear implants.
  • Pacemakers — ECT has been administered safely. Risks are low because only small amounts of electricity reach the device due to high tissue resistance. Skeletal muscle potentials during the seizure may trigger pacemaker activity; temporary conversion to fixed-rate pacing before ECT is recommended.
  • Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) — deactivate defibrillation and anti-tachycardia functions before ECT and reactivate immediately afterwards (or in the early recovery period).

Pregnancy

  • A large number of reports suggest ECT is safe and effective throughout pregnancy, and it may be preferable to some forms of drug therapy.
  • Plan in consultation with the obstetrician. Consider risks to the mother (aspiration, premature labour) and the fetus (spontaneous abortion, death).
  • Prophylactic tocolytic therapy is recommended if there is a history of premature labour.
  • Rapid sequence induction with an endotracheal tube may be used to secure the airway; take measures to avoid supine hypotension syndrome from aortocaval compression after the second trimester.
  • Sevoflurane can be used to maintain anaesthesia in the later stages of pregnancy to reduce the risk of uterine contractions.
10 · Induction Agents — Comparison Table

The dose is titrated against the patient's weight for the first treatment, then modified depending on clinical response, haemodynamic stability and seizure threshold. A systematic review concluded that all currently available induction agents are suitable for ECT; small variations in emergence and recovery times should not govern drug choice. Whichever drug is used, it is preferable to use the same agent throughout a course to avoid interfering with the seizure threshold (which generally rises over a course).

Opioid co-induction

  • Alfentanil 10–25 µg kg⁻¹ or remifentanil 1 µg kg⁻¹ (over 30–60 s) can be used with the induction agent to increase seizure duration and reduce the haemodynamic response.
  • It is unclear whether the effect on seizure duration is an inherent effect of the opioid or a result of its dose-sparing effect on the induction agent.
  • Combining opioids with agents other than etomidate can produce similar effects.
11 · Muscle Relaxants
  • Used to reduce the risk of injury from the convulsion. Complete muscular paralysis is not essential — the reduced or modified muscular contractions, along with EEG monitoring, are used to monitor seizure duration.
  • Succinylcholine (suxamethonium) 0.5–1.5 mg kg⁻¹ is the muscle relaxant of choice, due to its rapid onset and short duration.
  • Larger doses up to 1.5 mg kg⁻¹ may be required, particularly in severe cachexia, osteoporosis or pre-existing skeletal injury.
  • Muscle pains are usually mild, seen more commonly in younger patients, and easily resolved with non-opioid analgesia — typically paracetamol.
  • If succinylcholine is contraindicated, non-depolarising agents can be used safely:
    • Atracurium 0.3–0.5 mg kg⁻¹
    • Mivacurium 0.08–0.2 mg kg⁻¹ — short acting, but seizure modification is reportedly inadequate at low doses (0.08 mg kg⁻¹); use at least 0.15 mg kg⁻¹.
    • Rocuronium 0.3–0.6 mg kg⁻¹ — generally used only as a precurarising agent, but could theoretically be used with sugammadex.
  • With non-depolarisers, allow sufficient time for onset, and anticipate airway management while waiting for the effects to wear off.
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS): a serious side effect of some antipsychotic drugs with clinical similarities to malignant hyperthermia — muscle rigidity, fever, elevated creatine kinase, delirium and autonomic instability. Avoid succinylcholine and other MH triggers.
12 · Adjunct Drugs

Anticholinergics — for the parasympathetic phase

  • Glycopyrrolate 100–600 µg — minimises parasympathetic stimulation. Superior anti-sialagogue effects, no adverse central nervous system effects, and results in less post-ECT tachycardia.
  • Atropine 300–600 µg — has been used for the same reason, but has the disadvantage of a high peak, prolonged tachycardia, and crosses the blood–brain barrier.
  • Routine atropine premedication is NOT recommended — detrimental effects on myocardial work and oxygen demand.

Beta-blockers — for the sympathetic phase

  • Sympathetic stimulation can be attenuated with esmolol 1.0–2.0 mg kg⁻¹, labetalol 0.05–0.4 mg kg⁻¹, or verapamil 0.1 mg kg⁻¹.
  • Beta-blockers may be given pre-procedurally (atenolol) or intra-procedurally (labetalol, esmolol).
  • Controversy: whether labetalol and esmolol reduce seizure duration.

Other agents for haemodynamic control

  • Calcium-channel blockers can effectively control arterial pressure: sublingual nifedipine and IV nicardipine (but reflex tachycardia occurs), as has diltiazem — although it may reduce seizure duration.
  • Alpha-2 agonists such as dexmedetomidine blunt the hyperdynamic response.
  • Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) also blunts the hyperdynamic response and should be considered in patients at high risk of myocardial ischaemia.
  • Intravenous lidocaine is NOT effective for this purpose.
13 · Airway Management & Conduct of Anaesthesia

Objectives

  • Provide a short duration of unconsciousness and adequate muscle relaxation for the electrical stimulus and subsequent seizure.
  • Return to consciousness should be rapid, and full orientation is desirable.
  • Minimise the physiological and physical effects described above.

Airway & ventilation

  • Pre-oxygenate with continuous oxygen flow.
  • Airway and breathing should be supported once anaesthesia is achieved; ventilation can be gently assisted with a face mask.
  • Intubation of the trachea is not routinely required, unless there are specific risk factors for reflux (or in pregnancy, where RSI with an ETT may be used).
  • Hyperventilation just before the electrical stimulus lowers the seizure threshold and can prolong seizure duration — but note it also aggravates the rise in rate–pressure product.
  • A bite block is inserted prior to treatment to protect the patient's teeth, lips and tongue.
  • Manual ventilation during the clonic phase to avoid oxygen desaturation; maintain until adequate spontaneous ventilation resumes.
  • During initial treatments the stimulus magnitude may be titrated until an adequate seizure is generated — in these circumstances further boluses of induction agent are required to maintain anaesthesia.

Pre-treatment checklist

  • Avoid sedative premedication — it delays emergence and interferes with seizure generation.
  • Check consent, fasting status and vital parameters; administer regular medications.
  • Encourage the patient to empty their bladder — incontinence is common.
  • Secure IV access and apply full monitoring.

A typical treatment sequence (as described at the authors' trust)

  1. Full anaesthetic assessment on first presentation, with particular attention to aspiration risk; note any artificial dentition and warn of the risk of damage.
  2. For repeat patients, review to ensure no acute changes in health or medication; repeat the full assessment if physical status has changed.
  3. Check consent, vital parameters and fasting status. Secure IV access and full monitoring.
  4. Commence pre-oxygenation; administer an anticholinergic agent if one is to be used.
  5. Induce anaesthesia; administer the muscle relaxant once verbal communication is lost.
  6. Commence gentle assisted ventilation and observe for signs of fasciculation.
  7. Carefully insert a bite block once the patient is ready.
  8. Apply EEG electrodes to the head; place the paddles on the temporal regions.
  9. The anaesthetist stands clear; the electrical current is applied.
  10. Evaluate for tonic–clonic seizure activity and document it.
  11. Remove the bite block when seizure activity has stopped; continue airway support.
  12. Transfer to the recovery room once spontaneous ventilation has returned. Offer a warm drink and light meal when appropriate.
14 · Recovery & Postoperative Care
  • Continue monitoring oxygen saturation, heart rate and non-invasive blood pressure in the recovery room until the patient is awake and maintains oxygen saturation on room air.
  • Most patients recover quickly and can be discharged according to standard criteria.
  • The presence of a trained escort familiar to the patient can be reassuring.
  • Commonest side effects: confusion, agitation, violent behaviour, amnesia, headache, myalgia, and nausea and vomiting. Cardiovascular complications may also still occur at this stage.
  • Emergence agitation can be the most challenging problem to treat. Small doses of midazolam may be useful if simple measures — such as a secluded, calm recovery environment — do not help.
  • Regular review during the treatment course is required to assess response to ECT and to detect any adverse physical events early, so they can be avoided, minimised or treated.
  • Adverse psychological effects are rare, but some patients develop an intense fear of treatment — support and information are important in reducing this side effect.

Cerebral aneurysm An increase in aneurysm wall stress can lead to enlargement or rupture. The increase in cerebral blood flow velocity during ECT is generally less with propofol than thiopental. 16 · Consent & the Mental Health Act
  • Illnesses requiring ECT can feature significant impairment of mental capacity, which may preclude obtaining informed consent. When prescribing ECT, capacity must first be assessed.
  • An adult has capacity if they can understand and retain the information relevant to the decision, believe that information, and weigh it to arrive at a choice.
  • When a patient can understand the nature, purpose and likely risks and benefits, ECT cannot be given without consent.
  • Even when a patient is capable, NICE recommends doctors encourage the involvement of either an independent person who speaks on behalf of the patient (an 'advocate') or the person's carer.
  • When a patient is unable to consent, approval from the patient's family should ideally be sought, but this is not legally required. Care must be taken to ensure it is legal to deliver ECT.
  • UK framework:
    • Not detained, not resisting, but unable to give informed consent → Incapacity legislation may be used.
    • Detained under Mental Health legislation → some patients still have capacity to consent. If a detained patient is incapable of consenting, a formal, independent second opinion from the appropriate governing body (e.g. the Care Quality Commission in England, the Mental Welfare Commission in Scotland) must be arranged.
    • Resisting or objecting to treatment, or unable to express consent → the second opinion is essentially to confirm that ECT should only be administered to save the patient's life or to prevent serious deterioration or suffering.
  • Emergency treatment (where the rate of deterioration dictates that time is of the essence) may be administered without consent or a formal second opinion, although it is good practice to obtain an informal second opinion before proceeding.
  • Where a patient has an Advance Directive or Statement indicating they would not wish to receive ECT, this would normally be overridden only in an emergency.
17 · Summary
  • Appropriately administered ECT is safe and effective.
  • Anaesthetists must be aware of the physiological responses to ECT and how to modify them.
  • They must also understand the anaesthetic factors that may influence the efficacy of ECT — principally the anticonvulsant effect of induction agents and the balance between adequate depth and seizure generation.
  • ECT is usually administered at remote locations — adequately trained staff and an appropriately equipped unit are imperative for safe practice.
  • 'Modified ECT' — the use of anaesthesia and muscle relaxants — is the recommended standard practice.
  • Full anaesthetic assessment, with attention to aspiration risk, is essential.
