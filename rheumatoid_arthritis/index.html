Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune, chronic systemic inflammatory disorder characterised by deforming, symmetrical polyarthritis of varying extent and severity, associated with synovitis of joint and tendon sheaths, loss of articular cartilage and erosion of juxta-articular bone. It is a multi-system disorder that may affect the function of other organ systems.

Why this matters to the anaesthetist The same inflammatory process that destroys peripheral joints also attacks the cricoarytenoid joints (airway obstruction), the transverse ligament and odontoid peg (atlanto-axial instability), the temporomandibular joint (limited mouth opening) and the lungs, heart, kidneys and bone marrow (reduced reserve).
  • Prevalence: 0.5–1.5% worldwide; ~1–2% of the UK population. Higher in Northern European and North American populations.
  • Incidence: 1.5 men and 3.6 women per 10,000 people per year.
  • Sex: three times more common in women than men.
  • Peak onset: 30–55 years.
  • Genetics: approximately 70% of cases are associated with the HLA-DR4 subtype.
  • Serology: ~80% are sero-positive for rheumatoid factor (compared with 5–10% of the general population).
  • Environmental: as yet unidentified viral or bacterial agents are thought to play a role.
  • Other risk factors: female gender, family history of RA, food allergies and intolerances, altered gut flora, psychological stress, exposure to heavy metals, cigarette smoking.
Key numbers to remember 70% HLA-DR4 · 80% rheumatoid factor positive · 25% atlanto-axial instability · >50% extra-articular manifestations · 10% severe disability from repeated exacerbations.

Musculoskeletal

  • Persistent, painful joint swelling with morning stiffness (worst in the morning, improving with activity); joint involvement is often symmetrical.
  • Commonly affected: wrists, fingers, neck, shoulders, elbows, hips, knees, ankles and feet.
  • Signs: soft tissue swelling, raised temperature, tenderness on pressure or movement, limitation of movement, and later deformities and nodules.
  • A prodrome of fatigue and generalised weakness may precede the acute inflammation. Exacerbations may be associated with fever and lymphadenopathy.
  • Repeated exacerbations lead to progressive joint damage and severe disability in 10% of patients.

Axial skeleton

Axial skeleton involvement is common and often asymptomatic. This has major consequences for airway management and should be looked for in all long-term RA patients. See Section 5.

Diagnosis (for context)

  • Mainly clinical, confirmed with laboratory tests for autoantibodies and elevated inflammatory markers.
  • Plain radiography: periarticular soft tissue swelling, joint space narrowing, erosions, osteopenia.
  • Ultrasound and MRI demonstrate early disease.
  • Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (anti-CCP / ACPA) are now used to diagnose early disease.
Red flag — hoarseness in a long-standing RA patient Treat as cricoarytenoid arthritis until proven otherwise. It is variable in frequency, often unrecognised, and is a cause of postoperative airway obstruction and death.

Cricoarytenoid arthritis

  • Joint and soft tissue swelling cause laryngeal stenosis.
  • Symptoms: hoarseness, stridor, a sense of pharyngeal fullness when speaking and swallowing, dysphagia, dyspnoea, and occasionally severe upper airway obstruction.
  • Laryngeal amyloidosis and rheumatoid nodules may also cause obstruction of the larynx.
  • Assessment: fibreoptic nasopharyngoscopy (indicated for hoarseness) combined with CT imaging.

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ)

  • TMJ involvement causes limitation of mouth opening, which may render direct laryngoscopy impossible.
  • Assess preoperatively with the Mallampati score, mouth opening and mandibular protrusion.

Practical management of suspected cricoarytenoid involvement

  1. Avoid endotracheal intubation in favour of supraglottic airway devices (LMA and similar).
  2. If intubation is essential, use the smallest internal diameter tracheal tube possible.
  3. Problems occur at extubation — oedema combined with an already narrow airway can cause complete obstruction, sometimes several hours postoperatively.
  4. Consider an airway exchange catheter at extubation.
  5. Observe in a high-dependency area for some time after extubation.
  6. In severe cases a preoperative tracheostomy may be required.
Also remember Previously asymptomatic cricoarytenoid arthritis may cause airway obstruction postoperatively purely as a result of the additional oedema caused by tracheal intubation. This may necessitate reintubation or, in some cases, a tracheostomy.

The atlanto-axial joint is commonly affected in RA because of attenuation of the transverse ligament and erosion of the odontoid peg (dens). This causes atlanto-axial instability in about 25% of patients. The risk increases over time and is greater in patients with severe disease and in those who are seropositive.

Danger Acute atlanto-axial subluxation may cause spinal cord compression or compression of the vertebral arteries, leading to quadriparesis or sudden death. Patients may be entirely asymptomatic.

Symptoms that should prompt imaging

  • Neck or occipital pain (the earliest and most common symptom).
  • Radicular symptoms — paraesthesia to shoulders and arms with head movement.
  • Painless sensory loss in the hands.
  • Any preoperative neurological deficit (document it).

Subtypes of atlanto-axial subluxation

SubtypeFrequencyMechanismWorsened byAnaesthetic implication
Anterior Up to 80% C1 moves forward on C2 as a result of destruction of the transverse ligament; risk of cord compression by the odontoid peg. Neck flexion Direct laryngoscopy is usually well tolerated. Subluxation is worsened by the head moving anteriorly while the upper cervical spine remains static (e.g. a pillow behind the head).
Posterior ~5% Destruction of the odontoid peg causes backward movement of C1 on C2. Neck extension Risk of cord compression during C1/C2 extension — which occurs during direct laryngoscopy. Avoid direct laryngoscopy.
Vertical 10–20% Destruction of the lateral mass of C1 allows the odontoid peg to sublux through the foramen magnum, compressing the cervico-medullary junction. Extension High risk. Avoid extension; consider AFOI or tracheostomy under local anaesthesia.
Lateral / rotatory — Degenerative changes in the C1/C2 facet joints. Rotation Spinal nerve and vertebral artery compression.

Subaxial subluxation is uncommon and occurs below C2. It leads to earlier symptoms of nerve compression. Ankylosis of the cervical spine may lead to a fixed flexion deformity of the neck, and osteoporosis may be worsened by the regular use of steroids.

Diagnosis

  • If AAS is suspected, plain X-rays of the neck in flexion and extension are often diagnostic.
  • Subluxation exists when the distance between the atlas and the odontoid peg exceeds 4 mm in patients older than 44 years and 3 mm in younger patients. (The atlanto-dental interval / AADI.)
  • A flexion/extension CT or MRI should be considered if symptoms are associated with severe pain, if neurological signs are present, or if significant abnormality is noted on plain X-ray.
  • MRI is more accurate than X-ray for demonstrating nerves and soft tissues of the cervical spine; cord compression, spinal canal involvement and the position of the odontoid peg in relation to the medulla and brainstem are best seen with MRI. MRI may not be available if surgery is urgent.
  • There are no published evidence-based guidelines or general consensus on obtaining cervical spine X-rays before surgery in asymptomatic patients, but it may be prudent in long-term patients with severe disease.
  • In an emergency, or in an unconscious patient with known RA where flexion/extension views are impractical, regard the rheumatoid cervical spine as unstable and treat it accordingly.
Positioning pearl For anterior AAS, the subluxation is worsened by the head moving anteriorly while the upper cervical spine remains static — exactly what happens when a pillow is placed behind the head. The best choice for rheumatoid patients is a doughnut head ring with a hole large enough to accommodate the occiput: it supports the cervical spine without allowing anterior movement of the head.

Perioperative drug decisions
  • Methotrexate can be continued in the perioperative period — good disease control is beneficial.
  • Other immunosuppressants: there is a lack of data.
  • Biological therapy: perioperative discontinuation for elective surgery remains controversial. Recent studies suggest their use does not cause specific adverse effects and may improve recovery from postoperative anaemia.
  • If signs of postoperative infection develop: suspend DMARDs temporarily and start appropriate antimicrobials.

History

  • Duration of RA, severity, drug treatments and systemic complications.
  • Any history of cervical surgery — cervical fixation devices may cause impaired neck extension.
  • Preoperative neurological deficit, if any, should be documented.
  • Functional status: can the patient use a standard PCA? (Wrist and finger involvement may preclude it.)

Examination

  • Rheumatoid patients are often in pain and have deformities that restrict simple movements — take care during examination.
  • Note the range of movement of all joints so as to optimise patient position and minimise injury during anaesthetic procedures (venous access, nerve blocks) and positioning for surgery.
  • These patients have very fragile skin that is easily damaged.
  • Fragile veins make peripheral venous access unreliable; central venous access is often difficult due to neck deformity.

Airway assessment (essential)

  • Detailed assessment including mouth opening, TMJ function, neck flexion and extension.
  • Assess TMJ involvement preoperatively using the Mallampati score, mouth opening and mandibular protrusion.
  • The extent of neck flexion and extension should be assessed and documented. Perioperatively, care should be taken to avoid any movement beyond this range.

Systemic assessment

  • Steroid cover: patients taking more than 10 mg prednisolone per day should receive appropriate perioperative steroid cover (see Section 11).
  • Anaemia: normocytic normochromic anaemia due to chronic disease, or microcytic anaemia due to gastric bleeding and drug toxicity of the bone marrow. There may be an increased requirement for red-cell transfusion.
  • Cachexia: indicates poorly controlled disease and increased long-term cardiovascular risk. Cachexia and poor muscle bulk may also indicate myopathy which may impair respiratory muscle function — such patients may need to be considered for postoperative mechanical ventilation.

The two central problems are the increased prevalence of a difficult airway (RA patients needing cervical spine surgery have a high prevalence of grade 3 or 4 laryngoscopy) and the presence of AAS, which may result in spinal cord injury during airway management.

Step 1 — Know the neck

  • Assess and document the extent of neck flexion and extension. Do not move beyond that range.
  • Anterior AAS (80%): worsened by C1/C2 flexion. Direct laryngoscopy is usually well tolerated.
  • Posterior and vertical AAS: risk of cord compression during C1/C2 extension, which occurs during direct laryngoscopy — therefore avoid direct laryngoscopy. They are much less common and are mostly symptomatic.

Step 2 — Choose the device

TechniqueComment
Supraglottic airway (LMA) Use where possible. Requires minimal neck manipulation for insertion and causes less trauma and subsequent laryngeal oedema than a tracheal tube. May be difficult to insert in patients with fixed flexion deformities of the neck; a reinforced LMA may be preferable if the angle between the oral and pharyngeal axes at the back of the tongue is less than 90°.
Direct laryngoscopy & tracheal intubation Neck manipulation should be minimised, ideally with manual in-line stabilisation, even in the absence of overt cervical spine instability. The classical "sniffing" position (head hyperextended on a flexed neck) can exacerbate anterior AAS with neurological injury. There are no case reports to date of spinal cord injury secondary to direct laryngoscopy, nor evidence of outcome difference with a particular technique — but all due care must be taken.
Intubating LMA (ILMA) Can aid intubation with minimal neck manipulation, but is not popular due to a high risk of failure without fibreoptics, the associated trauma to the airway, and the force exerted on the posterior pharyngeal wall at C2–C3.
Awake fibreoptic intubation (AFOI) Considered the appropriate and safer option in rheumatoid patients with an anticipated difficult airway or known cervical spine instability. It generally carries less risk of major difficulty than direct laryngoscopy in this subgroup and allows assessment of neurological symptoms indicating spinal compression. Not useful under emergency conditions or if excessive blood or secretions are in the airway.
Surgical tracheostomy under local anaesthesia Indicated in emergency situations, in patients with symptoms of upper airway obstruction, and in severe cervical spine instability where AFOI is not possible. Potential difficulties include extreme fixed flexion deformity with little or no access to the trachea, tracheal deviation and patient distress.

Step 3 — Position the neck safely

  • The doughnut head ring with a hole large enough to accommodate the occiput supports the cervical spine without anterior movement of the head — the best choice for rheumatoid patients.
  • Consider asking the patient to position themselves on the operating table before being anaesthetised.
  • The same considerations apply at emergence and when transferring the patient from the operating table to the bed — do it as gently as possible.
Emergency rule If you cannot obtain flexion/extension views (emergency, unconscious patient) in a known RA patient, treat the rheumatoid cervical spine as unstable and manage the airway accordingly.

There is no single recommended anaesthetic technique. The choice depends on the patient's general condition, the type of surgery, patient preference and the skill of the anaesthetist. Regional anaesthesia, general anaesthesia, or a combination of the two may be employed.

Regional anaesthesia

  • If possible, regional anaesthesia should always be considered: it avoids airway manipulation, provides good postoperative pain relief and reduces polypharmacy.
  • Regional and neuraxial blocks may be technically difficult due to spinal arthritis and loss of anatomical landmarks from contractures or deformities.
  • A higher than normal level should be anticipated during a subarachnoid block.
  • Upper limb surgery may be performed under brachial plexus block; lower limb surgery under spinal, epidural or specific nerve blockade.
  • Long-term NSAID therapy has not been shown to increase the risk of epidural/spinal haematoma; the American Society of Regional Anaesthesia endorses central neuraxial block in such patients as safe.

General anaesthesia — general principles

  • See Section 8 for airway device selection.
  • Positioning: problems due to joint deformities and fragile skin from steroid therapy. Pressure points should be meticulously padded, the neck adequately supported, and skin handled with care. Avoid excessive manipulation of stiff and fixed joints.
  • The prolonged relative spinal malposition during general anaesthesia, in a patient with possible spinal stenosis, may cause cord injury. Avoid hypotension — attention to the maintenance of spinal cord perfusion may be important.
  • Infection risk: immunosuppressive treatment increases the risk of infection. Adopt a strict aseptic technique for intravascular access, regional blocks, arterial/CVP lines and urinary catheterisation. Give appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis before starting surgery.
  • Eyes: apply methylcellulose eye drops — up to 15% of RA patients have keratoconjunctivitis sicca and are at risk of corneal ulceration.
  • Steroids & glucose: long-term steroid therapy causes adrenal suppression. Monitor blood glucose closely and control with insulin if necessary. Patients on steroids and NSAIDs are at risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and should receive gastric acid prophylaxis.
  • Vascular access: vasculitis and thin, fragile skin can make IV access difficult; central venous catheters may be difficult because of limited neck movement; the radial artery may be inaccessible because of flexion deformities of the wrist.

Airway & breathing

  • Careful observation of the airway and breathing is required in the immediate postoperative period.
  • Previously asymptomatic cricoarytenoid arthritis may cause airway obstruction postoperatively as a result of the additional oedema caused by tracheal intubation. This may occur several hours postoperatively and may necessitate reintubation or, in some cases, a tracheostomy.
  • Increased sensitivity to anaesthetic agents may occur — depending on the patient's condition and the nature of surgery, ITU/HDU care may be needed.

Analgesia

  • Pain should be adequately controlled to permit early mobilisation. Multimodal analgesia is recommended.
  • Some patients have chronic pain issues and their pain may be difficult to manage.
  • Opioid analgesia can be used in carefully monitored doses to avoid respiratory compromise.
  • Patients with joint deformities of the hand may not be able to use a standard PCA — they can be instructed in the use of a mouth-controlled device.

Other postoperative care

  • Thromboprophylaxis: RA patients tend to be in a hypercoagulable state and have a slower recovery and return to mobilisation — prescribe appropriate prophylaxis.
  • Physiotherapy: standard physiotherapy and breathing exercises should be instituted as early as possible due to the increased infection risk and risk of respiratory complications.
  • Renal function: monitor carefully in view of possible pre-existing impairment, the effects of medication and perioperative fluid shifts.
  • Infection: if signs of a postoperative infection develop, suspend DMARDs temporarily and start appropriate antimicrobials.
  • Positioning: the same considerations regarding patient positioning and neck movement apply at emergence from anaesthesia as with intubation.
