▶ 1. Key Recommendations at a Glance The headline changes every intensivist should know
Diagnosis
  • Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) combining NS1 antigen / IgM / IgG is the first-line diagnostic test in adults.
  • Within 30 days post-dengue vaccination: use RT-qPCR (preferred) or NS1 antigen. IgM and IgG must not be used as confirmatory tests — vaccine-induced antibodies mimic natural infection. Rely on clinical judgement.
Outpatient / Ward
  • Oral fluid 200 mL per hour while awake (unless fluid restricted — individualise).
  • Urine output documented in all patients; aim to pass urine 4–6 times/day.
  • NSAIDs and corticosteroids should not be used. If already on them, continuation is a clinical judgement call.
Emergency Department
  • Point-of-care ultrasound (PoCUS) should be considered as an adjunct for early plasma-leak detection and haemodynamic assessment — guided by clinical indication, availability and operator competency.
  • Febrile phase: encourage oral fluids; start IV only if unable to tolerate oral or dehydrated; calculate total fluid requirement (oral + IV) with the Holliday–Segar formula.
  • Critical phase without shock: total fluid quota administered over 48 hours.
  • Crystalloids are the initial and preferred fluid for resuscitation in dengue shock. Colloids may be considered in persistent shock — never for maintenance.
  • Guide fluid by dynamic assessment of fluid responsiveness, not static measures.
  • In refractory shock, aggressively hunt for other causes of shock.
Complications
  • Suspect occult bleeding in persistent haemodynamic instability despite adequate fluid resuscitation.
  • Start blood / blood products promptly for clinically significant bleeding — do not wait for haematocrit to drop.
  • Packed red cells for significant bleeding.
  • No routine prophylactic platelets or plasma in patients without active bleeding.
ICU
  • Severe dengue → prompt identification and ICU referral.
  • HFNC and NIV may be used in selected dengue patients with respiratory failure.
  • Mechanical ventilation indications follow the same criteria as other critically ill patients.
  • Noradrenaline is the preferred vasopressor for persistent shock despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Why fluid behaves differently in dengue — the revised Starling model

Dengue NS1 and the host immune response injure the endothelial glycocalyx layer (EGL). The EGL is the key regulator of microvascular fluid exchange. When it is injured:

  • The barrier is lost and fluid shifts into the interstitium.
  • Both crystalloids and colloids leak into extravascular compartments and contribute to fluid accumulation.
  • Venous reabsorption is minimal; filtered fluid returns via lymphatics.
  • Large IV volumes during the leaking phase → combined lymphatic return and residual fluid can cause intravascular overload during recovery.
Clinical implication Fluid therapy must be phase-specific and individualised, guided by the degree of leakage and timing of endothelial recovery — avoiding both inadequate perfusion and fluid-related complications. Degree of leakage is inferred from HCT trends, haemodynamics, urine output, third-space loss, with PoCUS as a useful adjunct.

Critical phase timeline

Plasma leakage progresses gradually, peaks at ~24 hours after onset and declines, usually resolving within 48 hours. Duration may be prolonged if:

  • Patient presents in shock — may already have been in the critical phase for up to 24 h, so possibly another 24 h remain.
  • Uncorrected dehydration in the febrile phase — may be at an early stage at presentation, so leakage may exceed 24 h.

▶ 4. Fluid Management — Phase by Phase Febrile · critical without shock · fluid quota · DSS resuscitation
Common pitfalls in fluid therapy
  1. Excessive fluid in stable cases: unnecessary boluses in febrile phase; prolonged fixed/graded regimens in stable patients; continuing IV fluids into recovery phase.
  2. Inappropriate monitoring: infrequent monitoring and no timely rate adjustment; boluses based on a single parameter (rising HCT, tachycardia, cool peripheries) without comprehensive assessment; inadequate resuscitation in DSS.
  3. Injudicious colloids: e.g. human albumin as maintenance in leaking dengue patients.
  4. Failure to adjust for comorbidity: excessive fluid in heart failure or kidney disease.
▶ 4.1 Febrile phase
  • Maintain vascular access with a cannula in situ.

  • Total fluid requirement (oral + IV) calculated using the Holliday–Segar formula.
  • Additional fluid may be needed for acute losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration).

▶ 4.2 Critical phase WITHOUT shock — the fluid quota

Key indicators that the critical phase has begun

  • Temperature: rapid drop may signal entry into critical phase.
  • Rising HCT: increase ≥20% from baseline.
  • Platelet drop: <100 ×10⁹/L — earliest and most useful indicator.
  • PoCUS: pericholecystic fluid, thickened gallbladder wall, ascites, pleural effusion.

Some patients may still have low-grade fever during the early phase of leakage — do not be falsely reassured.

Fluid quota concept

Total fluid quota = Maintenance + 5% deficit 5% deficit = 50 mL/kg. This represents the expected plasma volume loss during the critical phase.
The quota includes both oral and IV intake and is administered over 48 hours.
Crystalloid is the IV fluid of choice.
Not all patients need the full quota — rate, peak and duration of leakage vary. Many need less.
The quota applies only to non-shock dengue patients in the critical phase. If the patient progresses to shock, the quota no longer applies.
Exceptions needing more fluid: DKA and HHS.

Practical steps (Group B2 — warning signs, critical phase)

  1. Calculate the total fluid quota (M + 5%) at the start of the critical phase, using max weight 70 kg.
  2. Encourage oral fluid. Patients entering the critical phase may require IV crystalloid.
  3. Initial IV: if unable to tolerate oral, start 3–5 mL/kg/h for 1–2 h. Reassess; reduce to 2–3 mL/kg/h for 1–2 h, then maintenance rate. If oral intake reaches 1–2 mL/kg/h, IV is not necessary.
  4. Total fluid must remain within the quota. Frequent monitoring of CCTV, BP, pulse pressure, HCT and urine output determines subsequent requirement.
  5. Urine output: calculate mL/kg/h at each void. Target 0.5–1 mL/kg/h.
  6. Catheterise early if urine output monitoring is difficult or in high-risk categories.

▶ 4.3 Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS) — resuscitation
Dengue shock is a medical emergency Manage in HDU or ICU. Fluid resuscitation must be initiated promptly and must not be delayed while waiting for HDU/ICU admission. Recurrent shock episodes may occur after initial resuscitation because leakage can persist for 24–48 hours.

Fluid choice

  • Crystalloids are the initial and preferred fluid for resuscitation. Options: balanced crystalloids (Hartmann's, Sterofundin) or 0.9% saline.
  • Balanced solutions produce lower chloride, higher bicarbonate and higher pH vs 0.9% saline — no difference in mortality, AKI or organ dysfunction (Cochrane review).
  • Colloids may be considered in refractory shock where instability persists despite adequate fluid resuscitation.
  • Albumin 5% is the colloid of choice. Gelatin is not recommended (no demonstrated benefit, potential harm).
  • Colloids should be reserved for resuscitation — never as maintenance.

Weight adjustment for fluid therapy

Obese patients (BMI >27.5 kg/m²) ABW = IBW + 0.4 × (actual weight − IBW)
IBW (female) = 45.5 + 0.91 × (height in cm − 152)

Caveats

  • PPV/SVV unreliable in: spontaneous breathing efforts, arrhythmias, low tidal volume ventilation, reduced lung compliance (ARDS), high RR:HR ratio, RV dysfunction, raised intra-abdominal pressure, open chest.
  • PLR may be less reliable in intra-abdominal hypertension. Haemodynamic effect occurs within 30–90 seconds and is transient — measure within that window. Avoid manual leg lifting; use an electric bed.
  • PLR contraindications: traumatic brain injury (↑ICP risk), lower limb DVT, lower extremity fracture/amputation, venous compression stockings. Known limitations: severe hypovolaemia, discomfort/adrenergic stimulation.
  • IVC indices: only moderate diagnostic accuracy — not a sole test. May be used as an adjunct when other dynamic tests are not feasible.

Fluid tolerance — who may be harmed even if fluid-responsive?

Fluid tolerance = capacity to receive fluid without developing organ impairment due to congestion.

  • Clinical: increasing breathlessness or oxygen requirement, pulmonary oedema, elevated JVP.
  • PoCUS: pulmonary congestion (diffuse B-lines), systemic congestion (VExUS).
Frank–Starling in one line A preload challenge increases stroke volume only when the heart is on the preload-responsive portion of the curve. On the flat portion, or with poor systolic function, there is little or no increase — fluid only adds congestion.

▶ 5.2 Respiratory support

Respiratory failure in severe dengue arises from plasma leakage → pleural effusion, pulmonary oedema from fluid overload, and ARDS.

HFNC and NIV

  • HFNC: no dengue-specific evidence; systematic review data in acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure show improved oxygenation and reduced need for NIV/tracheal intubation vs conventional oxygen, without clear mortality benefit.
  • NIV: evidence limited, mainly small paediatric studies; may improve oxygenation and reduce invasive ventilation, particularly when the cause is fluid-overload pulmonary oedema or pleural effusion.
  • NIV contraindications: cardiac or respiratory arrest, inability to protect airway, ongoing vomiting / high aspiration risk, haemodynamic instability.
  • HFNC contraindications: inability to protect airway, severe haemodynamic instability, upper airway obstruction.

▶ 5.4 Metabolic acidosis & lactate
  • Lactic acidosis from hypoperfusion is the most common cause in dengue shock.
  • Lactate <2 mmol/L in a critically ill patient generally suggests adequate tissue perfusion — but high lactate is not necessarily from tissue hypoxia or shock.
  • Targeting lactate normalisation in a non-shock patient can precipitate excessive fluid administration. Combine lactate with other perfusion markers — CRT and urine output.
  • If metabolic acidosis persists or worsens despite adequate fluid resuscitation, consider:
    • Bleeding masked by haemoconcentration
    • Worsening acute liver injury
    • Worsening acute kidney failure
    • Sepsis with distributive shock
    • Cardiac dysfunction
    • Medications — beta-2 agonists, metformin, SGLT-2 inhibitors
    • Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis from saline infusion
    • Concomitant ketoacidosis — diabetic or euglycaemic
Sodium bicarbonate Do not use routinely in dengue-associated AKI and metabolic acidosis. In patients with severe metabolic acidaemia (arterial pH ≤7.20) with moderate-to-severe AKI, IV sodium bicarbonate may be considered to correct severe acidaemia and reduce the need for kidney replacement therapy. Individualise — consider volume status, serum sodium, calcium and fluid-overload risk.
▶ 5.5 Kidney support

Pathophysiology of AKI: acute tubular necrosis from prolonged shock, direct viral kidney injury, indirect immune-mediated injury, rhabdomyolysis.

Supportive management

  • Volume optimisation while strictly avoiding fluid overload (which worsens intravascular fluid extravasation)
  • Monitor and manage electrolyte abnormalities
  • Avoid nephrotoxic medications
  • Kidney replacement therapy (KRT)
Do not start KRT early In non-dengue critically ill patients, early KRT offers no survival advantage over waiting for specific clinical triggers. Early KRT was associated with more KRT-related hypophosphataemia (RR 1.94, 95% CI 1.24–3.02) and CVC-related bloodstream infection (RR 1.66, 95% CI 1.04–2.66). Initiate only for urgent indications — not based on AKI stage or creatinine alone.

Urgent indications for KRT

  • Refractory metabolic acidosis, pH <7.20 despite resuscitation
  • Life-threatening hyperkalaemia
  • Refractory volume overload
  • Symptomatic uraemia

In haemodynamically unstable severe dengue requiring KRT, continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT) is recommended where available.

▶ 5.6 Electrolytes
  • Routine monitoring of potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, chloride is critical.
  • Hyponatraemia, hypophosphataemia and hypocalcaemia are associated with dengue severity.
  • Hypocalcaemia reduces myocardial and vascular response to vasopressors — complicating shock management. Severe hypocalcaemia (ionised Ca <0.8 mmol/L) in the critically ill is independently associated with higher mortality.
  • Perform electrolyte panels at regular intervals in all ICU patients; correct promptly per local ICU protocols.
▶ 5.7 Invasive procedures in ICU

Central venous line

  • Indications: prolonged vasopressor infusion; inadequate peripheral venous access.
  • A well-functioning peripheral cannula may be used for vasopressors for short durations (typically <2 hours) — with vigorous monitoring for extravasation and local tissue injury.
  • For rapid volume expansion, large-bore peripheral access (14G or 16G) is superior to a standard central line (lower resistance, higher flow rates).
  • Thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy are relative contraindications, but bleeding risk after insertion is low. Routine prophylactic platelets/FFP are not recommended.
  • Consensus Statement on Patient Blood Management 2024: consider prophylactic platelet/FFP if platelet <20 ×10⁹/L or INR >2. If given, insert the line during or within 30–60 minutes of transfusion.
  • Use real-time ultrasound where available.
  • Avoid subclavian cannulation — subclavian vein and artery are not accessible for direct compression.

Arterial catheter

  • Indications: continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring, repeated arterial blood gas sampling, haemodynamic monitoring (e.g. PPV analysis).
  • Severe thrombocytopenia and severe coagulopathy are relative contraindications, but bleeding complication rates are low. Routine prophylactic platelet/FFP not recommended — individualise.

Gastric tube

  • Avoid the nasogastric route — use an orogastric tube, which is generally less traumatic.

▶ 6. Bleeding & Transfusion Occult bleeding · thresholds · products · adjuncts

Recognising bleeding

  • Minor mucocutaneous bleeding (gingival bleeding, epistaxis, petechiae, mild per-vaginal bleeding) is common and usually self-limiting.
  • Severe overt bleeding: upper GI haemorrhage (haematemesis, melaena), massive per-vaginal bleeding, severe epistaxis requiring intervention, intracranial haemorrhage, pulmonary haemorrhage, large soft-tissue haematomas.
  • Occult bleeding — not clinically apparent but causes unrecognised intravascular loss and persistent haemodynamic instability.
Suspect significant occult bleeding when
  • A drop in HCT without clinical improvement despite adequate fluid replacement
  • Severe metabolic acidosis and end-organ dysfunction despite adequate fluid replacement
  • HCT not as high as expected for the degree of shock explained by plasma leakage alone
  • In shock, an HCT rise of ≤20% from baseline (or only minimal elevation) should be interpreted with caution — plasma leakage alone may be insufficient to explain the shock
Tip: in occult bleeding, colloids may unmask the bleed by causing haemodilution and revealing a disproportionate fall in HCT.

Risk factors for bleeding

  • NSAID use, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy
  • Pre-existing peptic ulcer disease
  • Severe renal or liver failure
  • Persistent metabolic acidosis
  • Any form of trauma, including intramuscular injection
  • Severe plasma leakage and prolonged shock → mucosal ischaemia, coagulopathy, DIC

Specific bleeding sites

▶ Upper gastrointestinal bleeding

Common causes: haemorrhagic/erosive gastritis, peptic ulcer, oesophageal ulcer. May present as haematemesis, melaena, haematochezia or occult bleeding. Bleeding may not be apparent for hours until the first melaena is passed.

  • Haemodynamic stabilisation with appropriate fluid resuscitation
  • Blood transfusion (packed red cells) and correction of thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy
  • Proton pump inhibitor
  • Endoscopy — consider in significant or persistent GI bleed despite optimal medical therapy. Endoscopic injection therapy is not supported in GI bleeding secondary to severe dengue. Endoscopic haemostatic therapy is generally recommended for peptic ulcers with recent haemorrhage (active arterial bleeding, non-bleeding visible vessels, non-bleeding adherent clots, persistent oozing).
▶ Intracranial haemorrhage

Rare but life-threatening, with high mortality. Prognosis is worse with severe thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, low GCS, large haematoma, shock or multiorgan involvement.

  • Early recognition of neurological symptoms and prompt brain imaging
  • Haemodynamic stabilisation and correction of thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy
  • Selective neurosurgical intervention (e.g. decompressive craniectomy) after stabilisation, in selected cases:
    • Significant intracranial haemorrhage
    • Progressive neurological deterioration after correction of coagulopathy
  • Multidisciplinary care — critical care, neurology and neurosurgery
Recommendation 9 (CPG 2026) Occult bleeding should be suspected in dengue patients with persistent haemodynamic instability despite adequate fluid resuscitation · Blood and blood products transfusion should be started promptly in clinically significant bleeding without waiting for a drop in HCT · Packed red blood cells for significant bleeding · No routine prophylactic platelets or plasma in patients without active bleeding · Platelet transfusion should be considered for significant bleeding or invasive procedures with bleeding risk.
▶ 7. Organ Complications & Expanded Dengue Syndrome Liver · cardiac · HLH · neurological
▶ 7.1 Dengue-related liver involvement
  • Range: asymptomatic transaminitis → severe hepatitis → acute liver failure (ALF).
  • AST is typically higher than ALT — reflects extra-hepatic sources and systemic inflammation.
  • Transaminases peak in the first week and normalise within 2–3 weeks in uncomplicated cases.
  • Dengue-induced severe hepatitis (DISH): >10-fold rise in serum transaminases.
  • Severe hepatic involvement: AST or ALT ≥1000 IU/L.
  • Acute liver failure: coagulopathy (INR ≥1.5) with hepatic encephalopathy, in the absence of pre-existing cirrhosis. Occurs in ~1–2% of cases; mortality 40–50%, particularly with dengue shock, prolonged hypotension or multiorgan dysfunction.

Risk scoring

In DISH, MELD ≥15 or EZ-ALBI ≥ −30 identifies high risk of developing ALF. Development of ALF and elevated baseline INR are independent predictors of mortality.

Atypical liver involvement — think alternative diagnosis

Consider targeted assessment if: ALT predominance, disproportionate hyperbilirubinaemia, or worsening function beyond day 5–7.

Management of ALF

  • Primarily supportive; avoid hepatotoxic medications; manage in ICU.
  • N-acetylcysteine (NAC): role remains uncertain; limited to observational studies and case series. Routine use not recommended for severe transaminitis without ALF. May be considered in ALF. Reported IV regimens:
    • 150 mg/kg over 15–60 min, then 12.5 mg/kg/h for 4 h, then 6.25 mg/kg/h thereafter; or
    • Continuous infusion 100 mg/kg/day for up to 5 days.
    Use a low volume of diluent to avoid fluid overload. NAC may cause anaphylactoid reactions in a very small percentage and may interrupt treatment.
  • Advanced therapies (liver transplantation, MARS, therapeutic plasma exchange, CRRT) — limited to case reports/small series; insufficient evidence to support routine use.
▶ 7.2 Cardiac complications

Manifestations: arrhythmias, functional myocardial impairment, myocarditis. Suspect when there is fluid-refractory shock or haemodynamic compromise disproportionate to capillary leakage / HCT increase.

Investigations

  • ECG: changes are often transient and may be the only sign of cardiac involvement with normal biomarkers and echocardiogram. Common: sinus bradycardia (commonest), AV block, atrial fibrillation, T-wave and ST-segment abnormalities.
  • Cardiac biomarkers (CK/CK-MB or troponin): limited sensitivity; may be normal.
  • Echocardiography: ejection fraction is difficult to interpret (influenced by preload and afterload). May show systolic and diastolic dysfunction with segmental wall abnormalities of the septum and right ventricular wall.

Management

  • Cautious fluid resuscitation — give just enough IV fluid to maintain adequate tissue perfusion.
  • No evidence to support antiviral or immunomodulatory treatment (beta-interferon, corticosteroids, IVIG) for dengue myocarditis. A Cochrane review found IVIG did not confer a survival benefit in adults with acute myocarditis vs placebo.
▶ 7.3 Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)

Potentially fatal syndrome of extreme immune activation → cytokine storm (↑IL-2, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, interferon-γ from uncontrolled histiocyte and T-cell activation). Clinical course is severe and may mimic sepsis.

  • Commonly presents with unexplained persistent fever or resurgence of high-grade fever after initial defervescence.
  • Also: hepatosplenomegaly, rash, bleeding, CNS manifestations, jaundice.
  • Labs: bicytopaenia/pancytopaenia, coagulopathy, hypertriglyceridaemia, hypofibrinogenaemia, hyperferritinaemia, transaminitis, hyperbilirubinaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, hyponatraemia.
  • HScore estimates probability of reactive haemophagocytic syndrome in adults. Lack of adequate criteria at one juncture does not exclude the diagnosis.
  • Bone marrow examination is not necessary when enough criteria are fulfilled; bone marrow aspiration appears safe in adults and older children with dengue-associated HLH.
  • Serum ferritin >10,000 is not pathognomonic in adults. Trend matters more than absolute level. Watch for sudden exponential rise in ALT, AST and LDH (may occur in <12 hours).

Treatment

  • Mainstay is supportive. Milder forms may recover spontaneously.
  • Severe HLH: short courses of immunosuppression — dexamethasone 10 mg/m² daily for 3–4 days is commonly used.
  • IVIG — consider especially if steroids alone are insufficient, or concern about secondary infection.
  • Etoposide — suggested as rescue therapy.
▶ 7.4 Neurological complications

Reported in 4.2–13% of all hospitalised dengue patients. Categorised as:

  1. Direct neurotropic — encephalitis, myositis
  2. Indirect/metabolic — encephalopathy; cerebrovascular complications from thrombocytopenia and platelet dysfunction; hypokalaemic paralysis
  3. Immune-mediated (IMNC) — ADEM, Guillain–Barré syndrome, acute transverse myelitis, opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome

Dengue encephalitis

  • Classically: fever, headache, seizures, altered consciousness, focal neurological signs after 5–7 days of fever.
  • MRI may be normal or show non-specific cerebral oedema or the classic double doughnut sign.
  • Lumbar puncture if no contraindications — CSF for dengue virus-specific RNA. Sensitivity of RT-PCR is low. CSF may be normal or show pleocytosis and high protein.
  • No specific treatment — supportive.

Dengue encephalopathy

Most commonly reported neurological complication. May result from prolonged shock, hypoxia, cerebral oedema, acute liver failure, acute renal failure, hyponatraemia and cerebral haemorrhage.

Myositis

Spectrum from subclinical CK elevation, self-limiting myalgia and weakness, rhabdomyolysis, to life-threatening fulminant myositis with severe quadriparesis and respiratory failure.

Immune-mediated

A cross-sectional study showed IVIG and plasmapheresis — standard treatment for GBS — can also be used in dengue-associated GBS. Most immune-mediated neurological complications have favourable recovery with appropriate immunomodulatory therapy.

Outcomes

  • Pregnant women have over twice the likelihood of adverse outcomes — preterm birth, low birth weight, miscarriage, particularly in the third trimester.
  • Substantially higher hospitalisation rates and nearly five-fold increased risk of progressing to severe dengue vs non-pregnant patients.
  • Maternal complications: thrombocytopenia, shock, postpartum haemorrhage, maternal mortality, AKI, ARDS, acute liver failure.
  • Foetal/neonatal: preterm birth, low birth weight, intrauterine foetal demise, miscarriage.

Admission & monitoring

  • All pregnant patients with confirmed dengue should be hospitalised.
  • Care led by a collaborative obstetric + physician team; early critical care involvement as indicated.
  • Gestation <28 weeks → medical or obstetric ward; ≥28 weeks → obstetric ward (preterm labour/delivery risk).
  • Monitoring frequency: no warning signs → daily review; warning signs → 4–6 hourly vitals and labs; severe dengue → continuous or 1–2 hourly.
  • Foetal monitoring: ultrasound and cardiotocography.
  • Paracetamol is safe; avoid NSAIDs and aspirin (bleeding risk).

Labour & delivery

  • Avoid labour and delivery during the critical phase. Postpone elective induction or planned Caesarean until platelet recovery begins, ideally >50 ×10⁹/L.
  • Vaginal delivery is the preferred mode; Caesarean only for obstetric indications.
  • Early group and cross-match for all. Minimise bleeding: avoid genital tract trauma, limit difficult instrumental deliveries, active management of third stage of labour.
Platelet thresholds in pregnancy Routine prophylactic platelet transfusion is not recommended without active bleeding — individualise based on maternal status and anticipated mode of delivery.
• Uncomplicated vaginal delivery: >30 ×10⁹/L
• Instrumental vaginal delivery, Caesarean section, spinal anaesthesia: >50 ×10⁹/L
Regional anaesthesia may be contraindicated with severe thrombocytopenia due to spinal haematoma risk — multidisciplinary discussion regarding general anaesthesia safety.
  • Post-delivery: intensive monitoring for at least 24–48 hours for ongoing plasma leakage, occult bleeding, postpartum haemorrhage or sudden haemodynamic deterioration. Serial HCT and platelet monitoring into the postpartum period.
  • Avoid intramuscular injections in thrombocytopenia — haematoma risk.
  • Breastfeeding is recommended — benefits outweigh transmission risk. Dengue is not a contraindication. If too ill to breastfeed, support expressing milk until well enough to resume.
▶ 8.2 Heart failure
  • Higher risk of shock, bleeding and death — may need early hospitalisation for close monitoring.
  • Early admission despite absence of warning signs.
  • Modify fluid volume to reduce fluid overload risk.
  • Early cardiologist consultation may be needed during the critical phase.
  • Consider loop diuretics in overloaded, haemodynamically stable patients.
  • Temporarily discontinue antiplatelets or anticoagulants if platelet <100,000/µL, if bleeding occurs, or for a few days during the critical phase.
  • Frequent reassessment of cardiac ischaemia and electrolytes.
▶ 8.3 End-stage kidney disease / chronic kidney disease
  • Higher rates of severe dengue, ICU admission and mortality. CKD at presentation is an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality (aOR 3.907, 95% CI 1.785–8.552).
  • Predictors of mortality: altered consciousness (aOR 23.008), pulmonary oedema (aOR 13.525), leukocytosis during hospitalisation (aOR 1.131).
  • In dialysis-dependent ESKD: 36.7% presented with warning signs, 36.7% developed severe dengue, 8.2% in-hospital mortality. Warning signs significantly associated with plasma leakage (OR 12.36) and severe haemorrhage (OR 5.1) — more likely in patients >65 years.

Management: early admission despite absence of warning signs; modify fluid volume; early nephrologist consultation for KRT planning in oliguric patients at risk of fluid overload.

HCT interpretation In ESKD, baseline Hb and HCT may differ due to chronic anaemia or haemodilution from fluid overload. Interpret HCT using the patient's own baseline Hb/HCT and serial trends.
▶ 8.4 Liver cirrhosis
  • Increased risk of severe dengue and mortality.
  • Cautious use of paracetamol for fever/pain.
  • Manage hepatic failure with fluid and blood transfusion — at risk of GI bleeding and severe shock.
  • IV vitamin K may be needed if PT/INR is prolonged.
  • Individualised fluid management with close monitoring for warning signs and severe dengue.
▶ 8.5 Diabetes mellitus
  • Higher risk of adverse outcomes: metabolic instability, greater dehydration risk, and diagnostic difficulty distinguishing dengue shock from metabolic complications.
  • Dengue can trigger DKA and HHS — these can mimic DSS or occur concurrently.
  • Hyperglycaemia causes osmotic diuresis → worsens hypovolaemic shock; gives a false impression of good urine output.
  • Requires early and regular metabolic assessment: capillary blood sugar, ketones/acid–base status, renal function/electrolytes.
  • In hospitalised patients: stop oral hypoglycaemics during acute illness (hypoglycaemia risk with poor intake; metformin → lactic acidosis with dehydration/hepatic dysfunction). Insulin-based regimes preferred.
Recommendation 13 In dengue patients with DKA/HHS, fluid requirement should follow the DKA/HHS regimen. In plasma leakage without DKA/HHS, fluid therapy should be guided by clinical assessment.
▶ 8.6 Hypertension
  • Patients with pre-existing hypertension have consistently higher baseline BPs throughout illness. BP may still decline around defervescence due to plasma leakage.
  • A "normal" BP reading in a hypertensive patient may signify relative hypotension and early shock, not stability.
  • Ask about baseline BP; closely monitor trends in BP, pulse pressure, CCTV and urine output.
  • Antihypertensives may be withheld during acute dengue, especially in the critical phase, to prevent hypotension. Restart gradually in the recovery phase with haemodynamic stability.
  • Drug-specific issues: beta-blockers may mask tachycardia or worsen bradycardia; calcium channel blockers may cause tachycardia; ACEi/ARBs and diuretics increase risk of renal injury and hypovolaemia.
▶ 8.7 Geriatric population
  • Managing dengue in older adults (≥60 years) is challenging due to physiological changes, comorbidities and atypical presentation.
  • Atypical presentation: fever may be absent, low-grade or delayed; lethargy and poor appetite predominate over classical myalgia, retro-orbital pain and rash. May mimic sepsis, acute coronary syndrome, stroke/delirium, or decompensated chronic illness.
  • Leucopenia is an important early marker — good discriminatory ability for differentiating dengue from other febrile illnesses (AUC 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.96), with increasing predictive value with age. Thrombocytopenia is a later, less reliable sign; rising HCT can be subtle with coexisting anaemia or chronic disease.
  • Increased risk of severe disease due to comorbidities, polypharmacy, frailty and sarcopenia. Also at increased risk of haemorrhagic symptoms, kidney and liver problems, and bacterial coinfection.
  • Signs suggesting bacterial coinfection: persistent fever >5 days, AKI, leucocytosis and neutrophilia.
  • Fluid therapy must be individualised and cautious — high risk of fluid overload, pulmonary oedema and heart failure exacerbation.
  • Lower threshold for hospital admission, especially with underlying comorbidities, inadequate home setting for safe monitoring, or atypical features.
▶ 8.8 Obesity
  • NHMS 2023: 54.4% of Malaysian adults are overweight or obese.
  • Obese dengue patients have significantly higher incidences of severe hepatitis (AOR 2.06), petechiae (AOR 1.35), dyspnoea (AOR 1.38) and severe thrombocytopenia (p<0.001) — though no difference in overall severe dengue or mortality rates.
  • Consider admission to ensure early escalation of care is feasible.
  • Clinical assessment may be less reliable due to body habitus — rely on haemodynamic trends and objective parameters.
  • Use Adjusted Body Weight (ABW)-based fluid dosing with frequent monitoring.
  • Screen for associated conditions: diabetes, hypertension, OSA, NAFLD.
▶ 8.9 Haemoglobinopathies
  • Dengue may present with persistent fever, progressive anaemia and thrombocytopenia.
  • Haemoconcentration may be absent — anaemia from haemolysis predominates.
  • HCT is unreliable as a marker of plasma leakage.
  • Clinical deterioration may be missed or delayed if conventional dengue markers are relied upon.
  • Packed cell transfusion is a critical, often life-saving intervention for symptomatic anaemia — the underlying cause is typically haemolysis rather than plasma leakage or bleeding.
  • Transfusion indications: symptomatic anaemia, rapid decline in haemoglobin, or clinical signs of tissue hypoxia or shock.

▶ 9.2 Discharge criteria

A. Clinical stability

  • Afebrile for 24–48 hours and clinically improving
  • Stable haemodynamics
  • Adequate oral intake with improved appetite
  • Good urine output (0.5–1.5 mL/kg/h)
  • No active bleeding
  • No respiratory distress

B. Laboratory stability

  • Platelet count: increasing trend (trend matters more than absolute number)
  • Haematocrit stable without IV fluids
  • Resolving organ dysfunction

Discharge counselling

  • Adequate oral fluid intake
  • Resume old medications as instructed
  • Avoid strenuous activity until full recovery
  • Attend follow-up review and repeat blood investigations at the nearest primary care clinic as needed
▶ 9.3 Post-vaccination diagnostic considerations
Within 30 days of TAK-003 (Qdenga) vaccination
  • Preferably confirm with RT-qPCR on serum collected within 5 days of symptom onset.
  • A positive RT-qPCR may reflect wild-type dengue, vaccine-strain viraemia, or both → consider genomic sequencing where available (especially severe, fatal, cluster, or vaccine-safety cases).
  • Dengue IgM and IgG should not be used as standalone confirmatory tests — vaccine-induced antibodies resemble natural infection.
  • NS1 antigen may be considered if RT-qPCR unavailable, but a positive result does not differentiate wild-type from vaccine-derived virus, and a negative result does not fully exclude dengue or vaccine-strain viraemia.
  • Rely on clinical evaluation and judgement to diagnose and treat.

Recommendation 1: RDT with combined NS1 antigen/IgM/IgG should be used as the first-line test to diagnose dengue in adults.

▶ 9.4 Dengue vaccination (2026 position)
  • Vector control remains the cornerstone of dengue prevention.
  • TAK-003 (Qdenga): live-attenuated tetravalent vaccine, DENV-2 backbone, two-dose schedule 3 months apart, no pre-vaccination serological screening required. Approved in Malaysia for anyone aged ≥4 years.
  • Seroconversion >90% for all serotypes (except DENV-4 ~84%) after dose 1; >96% for all serotypes after dose 2. Efficacy >80% against DENV-2, ~40% against DENV-1; protection against DENV-3 and DENV-4 primarily in those with prior dengue exposure.
  • Real-world: vaccine effectiveness 50.2% after one dose, 61.7% after two doses against symptomatic dengue; 67.5% protection against dengue-related hospitalisation after a single dose. Protection after a single dose declines after ~90 days — complete the two-dose schedule.
  • Ministry of Health Malaysia does not currently include TAK-003 in the National Immunization Program given low-to-moderate transmission intensity (aligning with WHO May 2024 position paper).
Recommendation 17 Dengue vaccination may be considered in all adults, particularly those with comorbidities who are at increased risk of severe dengue, unless contraindicated.

Also noted: Butantan-DV (single-dose, NIH TV003 backbone) — phase 3 efficacy 67.3% against symptomatic dengue, 89% protection against severe dengue/dengue with warning signs; licensed in Brazil 2025, no regulatory approval outside Brazil reported. CYD-TDV (Dengvaxia) — WHO recommends only in individuals with confirmed prior dengue infection.

▶ 9.5 Complementary treatments
  • Carica papaya leaf extract — meta-analyses suggest a modest increase in platelet count (MD 20.27, 95% CI 6.21–34.73; MD 35.45, 95% CI 23.74–47.15) and reduced length of stay (MD −1.90 days; MD −1.98 days).
  • However: no consistent benefit for bleeding complications, disease severity or mortality. Primary studies heterogeneous with methodological limitations and insufficient safety data.
  • Should not be used with the sole aim of accelerating hospital discharge — improvements in platelet count do not reliably reflect overall clinical recovery.
  • Lack of evidence to support Carica papaya leaf extract in the management of dengue infection.

Breastfeeding is recommended (2015 advised delaying during acute viraemia).
Vaccination "There are published and ongoing trials on the efficacy of dengue vaccine among the paediatric population" — no recommendation for use. Recommendation 17: dengue vaccination may be considered in all adults, particularly those with comorbidities at increased risk of severe dengue, unless contraindicated. Detailed TAK-003 efficacy and real-world effectiveness data. MOH does not currently include it in the National Immunization Program.
Complementary treatment Papaya leaf, cactus extract, crab/padi frog soup, bitter gourd, porcupine bezoar, tawa-tawa, pegaga and isotonic drinks have not been proven scientifically. Focus on plasma leakage and immune activation, not platelet count alone. Retains the principle, adds meta-analytic data: Carica papaya leaf extract may modestly raise platelets and shorten stay, but no consistent benefit for bleeding, severity or mortality. Lack of evidence to support its use. Should not be used solely to accelerate discharge.
Epidemiology context Incidence rose from 32 to 361 per 100,000 (2000–2014); CFR reduced from 0.6% to 0.2%; national target CFR <0.2%. WHO declared a Grade 3 public health emergency in 2024 (>14 million cases, >11,000 deaths globally). Malaysia: 117 dengue deaths in 2024, CFR 0.096% — below the 0.2% target. Cyclical epidemics every 4–5 years (2015, 2019, 2023). 30% of 2023 deaths had no prior comorbidity.
Methodology US/Canadian Preventive Services Task Force 2001 levels of evidence; GRADE principles being incorporated. Search 26 May – 24 July 2015; last 5 years. USPSTF 2015 levels of evidence; GRADE for recommendation strength; AGREE II adherence. Search 12 Jan – 25 Jan 2025, repeated before 25 Jan 2026; last 10 years. 30 DG meetings.
Monitoring frequency FBC 4–12 hourly in critical phase; urine output 2–4 hourly (hourly in shock); haemodynamic monitoring 2–4 hourly. Broadly retained; urine output in febrile and reabsorption phases changed to 6–8 hourly. Adds PoCUS to the monitoring parameter list and explicit selection criteria for targeted ultrasound.
Discharge criteria Improved well-being, afebrile 24–48 h, rising WCC then platelets, stable HCT, resolution of organ dysfunction. Formalised into absolute clinical and laboratory discharge criteria, with platelet trend emphasised over absolute number, HCT stable without IV fluids, and structured discharge counselling.
Bottom line for critical care The 2026 edition moves dengue management from reactive graded infusion to quota-based, physiology-guided, phase-specific fluid therapy, formalises dynamic fluid-responsiveness assessment, elevates PoCUS to a routine bedside adjunct, expands special population guidance, and gives explicit vasopressor, respiratory and renal escalation pathways.
