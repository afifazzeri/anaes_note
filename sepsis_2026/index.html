1 · Weight, IBW, Adjusted Body Weight & 30 mL/kg Fluid

#10 — For sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, suggest at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid in the first 3 h (conditional, low certainty). Use actual body weight; use adjusted or ideal body weight if BMI > 30 kg/m². Reassess frequently — avoid under- and over-resuscitation. Tailor to cachectic/underweight patients.

2 · Lung-Protective Tidal Volume

#71 Sepsis + ARDS → 6 mL/kg predicted body weight over > 10 mL/kg (strong, high certainty).
#72 Sepsis-associated hypoxaemic respiratory failure without ARDS → 6–8 mL/kg IBW over 4 to < 6 mL/kg (conditional, low).
#73 Plateau pressure upper limit 30 cmH₂O (strong, high certainty). Screen regularly for ARDS — it is missed in ~50%.

3 · MAP & Target Check

#13 Initial MAP target 65 mmHg over higher (strong, moderate). In practice use a range (e.g. ±5 mmHg).
#14 Age ≥ 65 y: initial MAP range 60–65 mmHg over higher (conditional, low).

Escalation logic: #53/#55 norepinephrine is first-line over dopamine, epinephrine, selepressin, vasopressin, angiotensin II. #56 add vasopressin on escalating norepinephrine (conditional, moderate). #57 if MAP still inadequate on norepinephrine + vasopressin, add epinephrine (conditional, very low). #58 with concomitant cardiac dysfunction, either norepinephrine or epinephrine may be first-line.
In our practice: vasopressin added at a median norepinephrine dose of 0.3 µg/kg/min (IQR 0.2–0.5); epinephrine added at a median 0.8 µg/kg/min (IQR 0.5–1.0); corticosteroids started by 34% at < 0.2, 38% at 0.2–0.3 and 28% at > 0.3 µg/kg/min.

5 · Oxygenation — P/F, S/F & ARDS Category

#65 Measure oxygenation by SpO₂ or ABG with physical examination and clinical acumen (conditional, very low). ABG is the gold standard; SpO₂/FiO₂ is less accurate in shock, darker skin tones, and SpO₂ < 90% or > 97%.
#67 HFNC over conventional oxygen when P/F < 200 or S/F < 235 (conditional, very low).

6 · Lactate Clearance & Capillary Refill Time

#51 Serial lactate measurements to guide resuscitation (conditional, low). Individualise fluid after the initial bolus — do not continue fluids until lactate normalises.
#52 Capillary refill time as an adjunct to other perfusion measures (conditional, low). Target CRT ≤ 3 seconds.

7 · Glucose Unit Converter & Insulin Threshold

#92 Start insulin therapy at a glucose level of ≥ 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L) — strong recommendation, moderate certainty (carry over).
In patients with sepsis AND either hypotension and/or elevated lactate

Monitoring

  • Immediately and at least hourly: monitor blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, level of consciousness.
  • Within 1 hour: measure initial serum lactate and establish capillary refill time.

Fluids

  • Within 3 hours: give initial fluid resuscitation of at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid.
  • Use balanced solutions (e.g. Ringer's lactate).

Vasoactive agents, if hypotensive

  • Time-sensitive: consider initiating norepinephrine concurrently with fluid resuscitation in patients with life-threatening end-organ hypoperfusion.
  • Use norepinephrine as the first-line vasopressor.
  • Start vasopressors peripherally rather than delaying until central access is secured.
  • Target a MAP of ≥ 65 mmHg, or 60–65 mmHg in patients aged ≥ 65 years.
If hypotension and/or lactate elevation persist after initial fluid resuscitation

Reassess perfusion

  • Assess fluid-responsiveness using dynamic measures (e.g. change in stroke volume or pulse pressure with passive leg raise / fluid bolus).
  • Trend lactate and capillary refill time.

Additional fluid

  • Give additional fluid based on fluid-responsiveness testing.
  • Use balanced solutions.
  • Consider supplemental albumin in selected patients.

Vasopressors & adjuncts

  • Initiate norepinephrine if not already started.
  • Use hydrocortisone, IV if norepinephrine requirement persists.
  • Add vasopressin if norepinephrine dose is increasing.
  • Add epinephrine as third-line agent if norepinephrine dose is still increasing.
  • Add dobutamine or use epinephrine if cardiac dysfunction is present.

Also throughout

  • At least hourly: measure blood pressure.
  • Consider continuous blood pressure monitoring with arterial line.
  • Ensure adequacy of source control.
  • Consider other causes of shock.
Perform frequent, ongoing reassessment and closely monitor patients to avoid harms of under- or over-resuscitation. Supplemental albumin may be appropriate for patients who have already received large volumes of crystalloid or have cirrhosis. Avoid albumin in patients with traumatic brain injury.

#1Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateNew / Carried over

For hospitals and health systems, we recommend using a performance improvement program for sepsis, including sepsis screening for acutely ill, high-risk patients; standard operating procedures for treatment; and implementation of sepsis quality improvement strategies.

Remark: Performance improvement programs and QI strategies may vary by setting and in accordance with a hospital/healthcare system's ability to implement. Certainty: moderate for screening, very low for SOPs, moderate for QI strategies.

#2Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowNew

For hospitals and health systems, we suggest using a "code sepsis" or "sepsis huddle" protocol over not using such a protocol.

Remark: "Code sepsis" or "sepsis huddle" protocols involve a multidisciplinary team huddle at the bedside to discuss and expedite sepsis diagnosis and treatment following a positive sepsis screen.

#3Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

In acutely ill adults en route to hospital by ambulance or flight, we suggest using a standard sepsis screening tool over not using a screening tool.

#4Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateRevisited

For acutely ill patients in hospital, we recommend using NEWS, NEWS2, MEWS, or SIRS over qSOFA as a single tool to screen for sepsis.

Remark: No ideal screening tool exists. qSOFA has poor sensitivity but remains useful for detecting clinical deterioration (electronic qSOFA screening with alert activation improved 90-day in-hospital mortality in the SCREEN trial).

#5Good PracticeNew

Sepsis is a clinical diagnosis and should not be ruled in or ruled out using a single biomarker or diagnostic test.

#6Insufficient evidenceNew

There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation regarding use of novel rapid host response diagnostics. None provides a simple "positive/negative" result; all should be used with full clinical evaluation.

#7Strong FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowNew

For adults with possible, probable, or definite sepsis or septic shock, we recommend collecting blood cultures as soon as possible and ideally before the administration of antimicrobial therapy.

Remark: Do not delay antimicrobials to obtain cultures, particularly in hypotension. Blood culture positivity fell from 31.4% pre-antimicrobial to 19.4% at a median 70 min post-antimicrobial. Number of sets and anaerobic bottles should be individualised; in low-resource settings a single set can be justified.

#8Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with possible, probable, or definite sepsis or septic shock, we suggest measuring blood lactate.

#9Good PracticeCarry over

Sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies; treatment and resuscitation should begin immediately.

#10Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ Low

For adults with sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, we suggest administering at least 30 mL/kg of IV crystalloid in the first 3 h.

Remarks: Consider individual patient characteristics and context when selecting initial fluid volume. Perform frequent, ongoing reassessment and closely monitor to avoid harms of under- or over-resuscitation. Weight-based fluid volume should be calculated on actual body weight, or adjusted/ideal body weight in patients with BMI > 30 kg/m².

#11Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with sepsis-induced hypotension, we suggest initial IV crystalloid fluid bolus resuscitation followed by vasopressor support if hypotension persists.

Remark: In patients with unstable septic shock, immediate concurrent administration of vasopressors together with IV crystalloid may be warranted on a case-by-case basis. Features of unstable shock: severely reduced blood pressure, mottled skin, ashen appearance, cyanosis/decreased oxygen saturation, tachycardia, altered mentation.

#12Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

In adults with septic shock, we suggest starting vasopressors peripherally to restore mean arterial pressure rather than delaying initiation until central venous access is secured.

Remark: Data are insufficient to recommend a duration of use, dose, or access route. Midline catheters were not considered. Pooled complication rate for peripheral vasopressors was 5.97%.

In our practice: 86.6% of panel members use peripheral vasopressors at least sometimes; 46.6% use them for up to 6 h; 13.8% up to 24 h; 27.6% have no set duration.
#13Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateCarry over + new remark

For adults with septic shock, we recommend an initial MAP target of 65 mmHg over higher MAP targets.

Remark: In practice it is not feasible to maintain MAP at exactly 65 mmHg, so a reasonable range (e.g. within 5 mmHg) should be used. Vasopressors should be titrated to maintain MAP within this range.

#14Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowNew

For adults with septic shock 65 years old or older, we suggest an initial MAP range of 60–65 mmHg over higher ranges.

For adults with sepsis or septic shock who require ICU admission, we suggest admitting the patients to the ICU within 6 h.

For adults with possible, probable, or definite septic shock, we recommend administering antimicrobial therapy immediately, ideally within 1 hr of recognition.

#17Strong FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with probable or definite sepsis without shock, we recommend administering antimicrobial therapy immediately, ideally within 1 hr of recognition.

#18Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with possible sepsis without shock, we suggest a time-limited course of rapid investigation and, if concern for infection persists, the administration of antimicrobial therapy within 3 hours from the time when sepsis was first suspected.

#19Good Practice

Clinicians should perform a rapid assessment of the likelihood of infectious vs. non-infectious causes of acute illness in adults with possible sepsis without shock.

#20Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with a low likelihood of infection and without shock, we suggest deferring antimicrobial therapy while continuing to closely monitor the patient.

#21Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with definite or probable sepsis and hypotension (i.e. septic shock) and who have an anticipated time to in-hospital medical evaluation of over 60 min, we suggest administering antimicrobial therapy in the ambulance or flight.

Remark: Prehospital antibiotic delivery should be implemented only after having a structured process in place to screen for sepsis in ambulance or flight (see #3).

#22Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowCarry over

For adults with possible or probable sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using clinical evaluation alone over procalcitonin plus clinical evaluation to decide whether to start antimicrobial therapy.

#23Good Practice

Adults with sepsis or septic shock should be rapidly evaluated for specific anatomical diagnoses or sources of infection that require emergent source control.

#24Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock and a specific anatomical diagnosis or source of infection that requires source control, we suggest early source control over late source control, ideally within 6 hr of diagnosis of sepsis or septic shock requiring source control.

Remark: Earlier source control is considered better when feasible. Prolonged medical stabilisation without source control in severely ill patients, particularly with septic shock, is unlikely to succeed.

#25Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock at high risk of infection with a specific multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogen, we suggest using empirical antimicrobial therapy with coverage for this MDR pathogen.

Remark: Risk factors for MDR pathogens include colonisation with the MDR pathogen of concern, previous infection with the MDR pathogen of concern, prolonged use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, and prolonged hospitalisation in a unit with a high prevalence of the MDR pathogen.

#26Conditional AGAINST⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock at low risk of infection with a specific MDR pathogen, we suggest against using empirical antimicrobial therapy with coverage for this MDR pathogen.

#27Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ Low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against using empirical antifungal therapy.

Remark: Empiric antifungal therapy should be considered on a case-by-case basis in selected patients with sepsis or septic shock and risk factors for fungal infection, including immunosuppression, prolonged use of antibiotics, prolonged hospitalisation, and intra-abdominal sources of infection.

#28Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock without risk factors for anaerobic infection, we suggest using an empiric antibiotic regimen without anaerobic coverage.

Remark: When coverage of potential MDR pathogens is required, agents with anaerobic activity (e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenems) are appropriate when alternative agents lacking anaerobic coverage are insufficient.

#29Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock with specific risk factors for anaerobic infection, we suggest using an empiric antibiotic regimen that includes anaerobic coverage.

Remark: Risk factors include intra-abdominal or deep-seated gynaecological/obstetric source of infection, necrotising soft-tissue infection, head and neck infection, and CNS abscesses or empyema.

#30Insufficient evidence

There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation regarding use of departmental (i.e. ICU-wide) microbiological surveillance samples of the upper respiratory tract to guide empirical antimicrobial therapy.

In our practice: 36% of the panel uses departmental surveillance samples of the upper respiratory tract; of those, 79% collect samples weekly.
#31Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using pathogen-specific rapid diagnostic tests on a case-by-case basis in selected patients based on clinical features, local pathogen- and resistance patterns, seasonality, and availability of tests and antibiotic stewardship guidance.

#32Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against using Candida fungal biomarkers to guide initiation of empiric antifungal therapy.

Remark: May be considered on a case-by-case basis in selected patients at high risk of Candida infection, including those with immunosuppression, prolonged exposure to antibiotics, prolonged hospitalisation, and intra-abdominal source of infection.

#33Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend using prolonged infusion of beta-lactams for maintenance (after an initial loading dose) over bolus administration.

Remark: Prolonged infusion reduced short-term mortality (RR 0.91; 95% CI 0.85–0.97; high certainty) — 25 fewer deaths per 1,000 patients. Administer a loading dose before prolonged infusion. Most benefit with shorter half-life agents (piperacillin-tazobactam, carbapenems). Prioritise infusion pump supply in low-resource settings.

#34Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using antimicrobial therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) on a case-by-case basis in selected patients, based on clinical features, local pathogen- and resistance patterns, drug class, and availability of TDM.

#35Good Practice

Clinicians should continuously reevaluate patients, search for alternative diagnoses, and discontinue empiric antimicrobial therapy if an alternative cause of illness is demonstrated or strongly suspected in adults with suspected sepsis or septic shock but unconfirmed.

#36Strong FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend de-escalation of antimicrobial therapy over no de-escalation when a confirmed microbiological diagnosis and susceptibility profile is available.

Remark: De-escalation involves discontinuing unnecessary antimicrobial therapy or narrowing the spectrum of antimicrobial agents where appropriate.

#37Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest de-escalation of antimicrobial therapy over no de-escalation when no pathogens are identified on final culture results.

Remark: In low-resource settings with limited or low-quality laboratory services and high rates of MDR pathogens, negative cultures may not provide the same reassurance — weigh the risk of undetected MDR pathogens carefully.

#38Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ Low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock who are receiving empiric antifungal therapy, we suggest against use of Candida fungal biomarkers to guide discontinuation of empiric antifungal therapy.

Remark: May be considered on a case-by-case basis in clinically improving selected patients at high risk of Candida infection.

#39Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowCarry over

For adults with an initial diagnosis of sepsis or septic shock and adequate source control, we suggest using shorter over longer duration of antimicrobial therapy.

Remark: Supported by the BALANCE trial (7 vs 14 days for bloodstream infection, non-inferiority of 7 days).

#40Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with an initial diagnosis of sepsis or septic shock and adequate source control where optimal duration of therapy is unclear, we suggest using procalcitonin AND clinical evaluation to decide when to discontinue antimicrobial therapy over clinical evaluation alone.

#41Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateNew

In mechanically ventilated adults with sepsis or septic shock in units with a low prevalence of antimicrobial resistance, we suggest using selective decontamination of the digestive tract (SDD).

Remark: SDD = non-absorbable topical antimicrobials to oropharynx and upper GI tract plus a short course of broad-spectrum IV antimicrobial. Suggested probable reduction in short-term mortality (RR 0.91; moderate certainty) and possibly less antimicrobial resistance.

#42Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with septic shock, we suggest using either invasive or noninvasive blood pressure monitoring.

Remark: Invasive blood pressure monitoring is advised in patients with shock who: require intermediate- to high-dose vasopressors, escalating doses of vasopressor, or multiple vasopressors; are receiving frequent arterial blood sampling; or have noninvasive blood pressure measurements which are inconsistent on repeated assessments.

In our practice: 62.7% use invasive blood pressure monitoring in septic shock. Of those not using it, 92% measure noninvasive BP at least every 15 min during early shock or with escalating vasopressor doses.

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend using crystalloids as first-line fluid for resuscitation.

#44Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

For adults with sepsis or septic shock undergoing initial resuscitation, we suggest using balanced crystalloids over 0.9% saline.

Remark: For patients with sepsis and traumatic brain injury, we suggest using 0.9% saline.

#45Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using crystalloids alone over crystalloids with supplemental albumin for fluid resuscitation.

Remark: Use of supplemental albumin may be appropriate for patients who have already received large crystalloid volumes or have cirrhosis. Supplemental albumin should be avoided in patients with traumatic brain injury.

#46Strong AGAINST⊕⊕⊕⊕ High

#47Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

#48Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock who have already received fluid resuscitation with 30 mL/kg and have persistent hypoperfusion, we suggest using either a liberal or a restrictive fluid resuscitation strategy based on individual patient and health system factors.

Remark: Wide variability in protocols and volumes across trials. Patient and health system factors to consider include current clinical conditions and chronic illnesses (e.g. heart failure) and availability of monitored beds (i.e. if a restrictive approach necessitates vasopressor use).

#49Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ Low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using dynamic measures to guide fluid resuscitation over physical examination or static measures alone.

Remark: Dynamic measures include response to a passive leg raise or a fluid bolus using stroke volume (SV), stroke volume variation (SVV), pulse pressure (PP), or pulse pressure variation (PPV). A 10–15% increase in the chosen parameter reflects fluid responsiveness.

#50Insufficient evidence

For adults with septic shock, there is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation on using minimally invasive or noninvasive cardiac output monitoring in addition to usual care.

Remark: Minimally invasive refers to devices requiring an arterial catheter. Noninvasive refers to devices using bioreactance. Usual care refers to care without a pulmonary artery catheter. The use of critical care ultrasound was not evaluated.

#51Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevisited

Remark: Fluid administration should be individualised after initial fluid bolus and monitoring of lactate decrement, rather than continuing fluids until lactate normalisation is achieved.

#52Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using capillary refill time to guide resuscitation as an adjunct to other measures of perfusion.

#53Strong FORCarry over

For adults with septic shock, we recommend using norepinephrine as the first-line agent over dopamine, epinephrine, or selepressin.

Certainty: Dopamine — high certainty; Epinephrine — low certainty; Selepressin — low certainty.

#54Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with septic shock, we suggest against using terlipressin.

#55Conditional FORRevisited

For adults with septic shock, we suggest using norepinephrine as the first-line agent over vasopressin or angiotensin II.

Certainty: Vasopressin — low certainty; Angiotensin II — very low certainty. Vasopressin was associated with probably less use of RRT (RR 0.70, moderate certainty). Vasopressin and angiotensin II tend to be more expensive and less available than norepinephrine, especially in low-resource settings.

#56Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateRevisited

For adults with septic shock on escalating doses of norepinephrine, we suggest adding vasopressin.

Remark: Meta-analysis found a probable reduction in mortality with adding vasopressin compared with increasing norepinephrine alone (RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.79–1.01, moderate certainty). Probably less atrial fibrillation (RR 0.66), possibly more digital ischaemia.

In our practice: 85.1% add vasopressin at a median norepinephrine dose of 0.3 µg/kg/min (IQR 0.2–0.5).
#57Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with septic shock and inadequate MAP levels despite norepinephrine and vasopressin, we suggest adding epinephrine.

Remark: In settings where vasopressin is not available, epinephrine can be added to norepinephrine alone.

#58Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with septic shock with concomitant cardiac dysfunction, we suggest using either norepinephrine or epinephrine as first-line vasopressor.

Remark: Norepinephrine may be preferred in patients with tachyarrhythmia or significant sinus tachycardia. Conversely, epinephrine may be preferred in patients with bradyarrhythmia or significant sinus bradycardia.

#59Insufficient evidence

For adults with refractory septic shock and escalating vasopressor requirements, there is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation on IV methylene blue.

Remark: Although methylene blue may improve blood pressure, there is insufficient evidence to determine if its use as rescue therapy improves survival; some patients with potentially treatable disease may value a trial.

In our practice: 69% of panelists "never" or "almost never" use methylene blue as rescue therapy, 23% "sometimes", 6% "usually", 1.5% "almost always".
#60Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisit

For adults with septic shock and cardiac dysfunction with persistent hypoperfusion despite adequate fluid status and arterial blood pressure, we suggest using inotropes over no inotropes.

Remark: For patients requiring vasopressors to maintain mean arterial pressure at target, inotropes should be used in addition to (not instead of) vasopressors.

#61Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisit

For adults with septic shock with persistent hypoperfusion and cardiac dysfunction despite adequate fluid resuscitation and arterial blood pressure, we suggest adding dobutamine to norepinephrine or using epinephrine alone.

Remark: Data were insufficient to make a recommendation for dobutamine vs. milrinone.

#62Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with septic shock and cardiac dysfunction with persistent hypoperfusion despite adequate volume status and arterial blood pressure, we suggest against using levosimendan.

#63Insufficient evidenceNew

For adults with septic shock and ongoing requirement for vasopressors, there is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation on use of oral midodrine.

In our practice: 32.8% of panel members use midodrine in adults with septic shock; of those, 86.4% use it after the acute phase to facilitate discontinuation of IV vasopressors.
#64Conditional AGAINST⊕◯◯◯ Very low

For adults with septic shock, we suggest against using beta-blockers as a treatment for septic shock.

Remark: This recommendation is based on evidence for short-acting, IV beta-blockers (esmolol and landiolol) prescribed for treatment of septic shock.

#65Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with sepsis, we suggest measuring oxygenation by either pulse oximeter (SpO₂) or arterial blood gas (SaO₂) in conjunction with physical examination and clinical acumen.

Remarks: Arterial blood gas measurements are the gold standard for assessing oxygenation; include other important information such as pH, PaCO₂, lactate, and bicarbonate; and are preferable when available. SpO₂/FiO₂ by pulse oximeter may substitute for PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, but is less accurate in patients in shock, with darker skin tones, and/or with oxygen saturations < 90% or > 97%.

#66Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ Low

Remark: Most trials used a lower target of approximately 90–93% SpO₂ and a higher target of SpO₂ ≥ 96%.

In our practice: panelists target SpO₂ between 90% (IQR 90–92%) and 96% (IQR 94–98%) for patients with sepsis and acute hypoxemic respiratory failure.

#69Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very low

#74Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

For adults with sepsis and moderate-severe ARDS, we suggest using higher positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) over lower PEEP.

#75Strong AGAINST⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

For adults with sepsis and moderate-severe ARDS, we recommend against using an incremental PEEP titration strategy.

#76Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

For adults with sepsis and moderate-severe ARDS, we suggest using prone ventilation for greater than 12 hr daily.

#77Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate

For adults with sepsis and moderate-severe ARDS, we suggest using intermittent NMBA boluses over continuous NMBA infusion.

#78Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with severe ARDS due to sepsis, we suggest using veno-venous ECMO when conventional mechanical ventilation fails in experienced centres with infrastructure to support its use.

#79Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevisited

For adults with septic shock, we suggest using IV corticosteroids.

Remark: Corticosteroids may result in a small reduction in 28-day mortality (RR 0.92) and long-term mortality (RR 0.94); greater incidence of shock reversal at 7 days (RR 1.29, high certainty); increases in hyperglycaemia and hypernatraemia. No increased benefit above 260 mg/day hydrocortisone equivalent.

In our practice: 88.4% use IV corticosteroids in septic shock; 90% prescribe 200 mg hydrocortisone-equivalent per 24 h; 86% as intermittent doses rather than continuous infusion; 63% never give concomitant fludrocortisone.
#80Conditional AGAINST⊕◯◯◯ Very low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against the use of antipyretic therapy, either pharmacologic or surface cooling, for the purpose of improving clinical outcomes.

Remark: This recommendation does not apply to using antipyretic therapy for pain control, patient symptom control, or for patients with other indications for temperature control, such as neurocritical care patients or patients after cardiac arrest.

#81Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against using IV vitamin C.

Remark: Possibly no difference in mortality at 90 days in low risk-of-bias trials (RR 1.06). IV vitamin C can result in factitious hyperglycaemia.

#82Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against using IV immunoglobulin.

#83Conditional AGAINST⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against using blood purification techniques, including hemoperfusion, high-dose hemofiltration, or plasma exchange.

#84Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock we suggest against using polymyxin B hemoperfusion.

#85Conditional AGAINST⊕◯◯◯ Very low

For adults with sepsis and septic shock, we suggest against using Vitamin D therapy for sepsis treatment.

Remark: This recommendation does not pertain to patients who are on lower doses of Vitamin D supplement for other indications or receiving it as part of standard nutritional practice. In the VIOLET trial, the sepsis subgroup had a higher 90-day mortality risk with high-dose (540,000 IU) enteral vitamin D.

#86Conditional AGAINST⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against using XueBiJing injection outside of jurisdictions where it has regulatory approval.

#87Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, and who have risk factors for GI bleeding, we suggest using stress ulcer prophylaxis with proton-pump inhibitors over not using stress ulcer prophylaxis.

Remark: PPIs reduced the risk of clinically important gastrointestinal bleeding (RR 0.48, moderate certainty). Important risk factors for clinically important bleeding include acute kidney injury, male gender, coagulopathy, shock, and chronic liver failure. In the absence of PPIs, H2 receptor antagonists are a reasonable alternative.

#88Conditional AGAINST⊕◯◯◯ Very low

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against using probiotics.

#89Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowNew

For adults with septic shock after the acute resuscitation phase, we suggest using active fluid removal.

Remark: Acute resuscitation refers to escalating doses of vasopressors, ongoing high doses of vasopressors, or needing ongoing volume expansion. Active fluid removal refers to diuretics and, if diuretics are insufficient, ultrafiltration or extracorporeal fluid removal. Factors to be considered when deciding to initiate active fluid removal include cardiorespiratory function; vasopressor dose; clinical course; peripheral oedema; weight; and fluid balance.

#90Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend using a restrictive transfusion strategy over a liberal transfusion strategy.

#91Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest early (within 72 h) initiation of enteral nutrition.

#92Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend initiating insulin therapy at a glucose level of ≥ 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L).

#93Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock and acute kidney injury, with no definitive indication for renal replacement therapy, we suggest against using renal replacement therapy.

#94Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock and acute kidney injury warranting renal replacement therapy, we suggest either continuous or intermittent renal replacement therapy.

#95Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with septic shock and hypoperfusion-induced lactic acidaemia, we suggest against using sodium bicarbonate therapy to improve haemodynamics or to reduce vasopressor requirements.

#96Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowCarry over

For adults with septic shock, severe metabolic acidaemia (pH ≤ 7.2), and acute kidney injury (AKIN score 2 or 3), we suggest using sodium bicarbonate therapy.

#97Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend using pharmacologic venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis unless a contraindication exists.

#98Strong FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend using low molecular weight heparin over unfractionated heparin for VTE prophylaxis.

#99Conditional FOR⊕⊕⊕◯ ModerateCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using pharmacological VTE prophylaxis alone over pharmacological VTE prophylaxis plus mechanical VTE prophylaxis.

#100Good PracticeCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, clinicians should discuss goals of care and prognosis with patients and/or families.

#101Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest addressing goals of care early (within 72 h) over late (72 h or later).

#102Insufficient evidenceRevisited

For adult patients with sepsis or septic shock, there is insufficient evidence to issue a recommendation regarding the use of a specific standardized criterion to identify patients for goals of care discussions.

#103Good PracticeNew

Health systems should implement strategies to ensure that patients being discharged from hospital after sepsis or septic shock have the opportunity to execute advanced directives.

#104Insufficient evidenceNew

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, there is insufficient evidence to issue a recommendation on the systematic establishment of advanced care directives before hospital discharge.

#105Insufficient evidence

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, there is insufficient evidence to issue a recommendation regarding formal time-limited trials (TLTs) of critical care.

Remark: A TLT is a collaborative plan to use life-sustaining therapy for a defined duration, after which response to therapy informs the decision as to whether to continue or escalate curative intent ICU care or to instead focus care on other goals.

In our practice: 64% of the panel uses TLTs; among these, 64% "sometimes" and 27% "always" explicitly discuss the use of TLTs with the patient or surrogate decision maker.
#106Good PracticeCarry over

The principles of palliative care (which may include palliative care consultation based on clinician judgement) should be integrated into the treatment plan, when appropriate, to address patient and family symptoms and suffering.

#107Conditional AGAINST⊕⊕◯◯ LowCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest against routine formal palliative care consultation for all patients over palliative care consultation based on clinician judgement.

#108Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowCarry over

For adults with sepsis and septic shock admitted to ICU, we suggest using a critical care transition program, compared with usual care, upon transfer to the floor.

#109Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest using a handoff process of critically important information at transitions of care over no such handoff process.

#110Good PracticeCarry over

Hospitals and health systems should screen patients with sepsis or septic shock for economic and social support needs (including housing, nutritional, financial, and spiritual support), and make referrals where available to meet these needs.

#111Good PracticeRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, comprehensive medication reconciliation should be performed at transitions in care, including at ICU and hospital discharge.

#112Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowRevisited

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we suggest comprehensive medication reconciliation using a pharmacist-based approach at transitions in care.

#113Good PracticeCarry over

Clinical teams should provide adults with sepsis or septic shock and their families the opportunity to participate in shared decision making in post-ICU and hospital discharge planning to ensure discharge plans are acceptable and feasible.

#114 / #118Good PracticeCarry over

For adult survivors of sepsis or septic shock and their families, clinicians should provide information about the hospital stay, sepsis and related diagnoses, treatments, and common impairments after sepsis in the written and verbal discharge summary.

#115Good PracticeCarry over

For adults with sepsis or septic shock who developed new impairments, hospital discharge plans should include follow-up with clinicians able to support and manage new and long-term sequelae.

#116Good PracticeNew

Healthcare systems should implement strategies to ensure that patients, their families, and their primary care providers are provided with adequate information to navigate the transition out of hospital.

#117Insufficient evidenceRevisited

For adults who survive hospitalization with sepsis or septic shock, there is insufficient evidence to issue a recommendation regarding a specific structured multi-component discharge planning process.

#119Conditional FOR⊕◯◯◯ Very lowCarry over

For adults with sepsis and septic shock and their families, we suggest offering written and verbal sepsis education (diagnosis, treatment, post-ICU/sepsis syndrome) before hospital discharge and in the follow-up setting.

#120Good PracticeNew

Health systems should implement strategies to ensure clinicians have the knowledge and competency to support sepsis survivors and their families during the post-hospital recovery.

#121Insufficient evidenceNew

There is insufficient evidence to issue a recommendation regarding providing sepsis-focused educational material to primary care providers as compared with usual care.

#122Good PracticeNew

Health systems should implement strategies to support sepsis survivors and their families during the post-hospital recovery.

#123Insufficient evidenceRevisited

There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation on early post-hospital discharge follow-up vs. routine post-hospital discharge follow-up.

#124Good PracticeCarry over

Health systems should facilitate assessment and follow-up for physical, cognitive, and emotional problems after hospital discharge for sepsis or septic shock.

#125Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevisited

For adult survivors of hospitalization for sepsis or septic shock, we suggest offering post critical illness follow-up services.

Remark: Follow-up services may vary in format, intensity, and duration, depending on locally available resources and patient needs. Possible small improvements in quality-of-life physical health component, anxiety and PTSD symptoms, and a potential reduction in hospital readmissions.

#126Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevised

For adult survivors of hospitalization for sepsis or septic shock who received invasive mechanical ventilation > 48 hr, we suggest offering physical rehabilitation services after hospital discharge.

Remark: Physical rehabilitation services may vary in format, intensity, and duration, depending on locally available resources and patient needs.

#127Conditional FOR⊕⊕◯◯ LowRevised

For adult survivors of hospitalization for sepsis or septic shock, we suggest offering services that support mental health after hospital discharge.

Remark: Mental health support services may vary in format, intensity, and duration, depending on locally available resources and patient needs.

#128Good PracticeRevised

Adult survivors of hospitalization for sepsis or septic shock who demonstrate clinical symptoms of mental health disorders should be referred to appropriate healthcare professionals for evaluation and management.

#129Insufficient evidenceNew

For adult survivors of hospitalization for sepsis or septic shock, there is insufficient evidence to issue a recommendation regarding cognition-targeted therapies vs. usual care.

Remark: Where cognitive-targeted therapies are being used, it is reasonable to continue using them as they are likely acceptable and feasible.
