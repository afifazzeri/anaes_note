Key points At a glance
  • Good preoperative assessment and planning are critical for optimal perioperative management of diabetes mellitus.
  • Clinicians must ascertain the type of diabetes in all patients, including children.
  • Guidelines strongly recommend preoperative HbA1c measurement.
  • No CGM or CSII pump has been certified for perioperative use — use is possible, but with caveats and backup plans.
  • After surgery, a basal–bolus insulin regimen is preferable to a sliding-scale (short-acting, bolus-only) protocol.
  • GLP-1 RAs: the 2025 UK multidisciplinary consensus recommends continuing these drugs perioperatively, with aspiration risk stratification and mitigation — not routine cessation.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: guidance is evolving. The 2025 UK consensus recommends omitting the day before and day of the procedure. The 2026 SPAQI consensus takes a tailored approach — continue in many low-risk scenarios, discontinue in higher-risk ones — with monitoring and dextrose/insulin rescue for euglycaemic DKA (eDKA).
Overriding principle on the day of surgery Avoid hypoglycaemia rather than chasing hyperglycaemia — but never withhold insulin entirely in T1DM or insulin-dependent T2DM (risk of ketoacidosis).
Latest guideline updates — GLP-1 RAs & SGLT2 inhibitors 2025–2026

Two major multidisciplinary consensus statements have been published since the 2024 BJA Education review. They change practice for the two newest drug classes.

GLP-1 receptor agonists & dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists (Anaesthesia 2025 — El-Boghdadly et al.)

  • Continue GLP-1 RAs (and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists) throughout the perioperative period.
  • Perform individualised pulmonary aspiration risk assessment accounting for drug, patient and procedural factors.
  • Discuss risks and mitigation strategies with the patient using shared decision-making.
  • Adhere to standard fasting guidelines; upper GI symptoms alone should not be used to determine gastric content.
  • Consider point-of-care gastric ultrasound before induction to facilitate risk stratification, if appropriate.
  • Consider regional anaesthesia as the primary technique if appropriate.
  • Aspiration mitigation may include: prokinetics; tracheal tube; modified rapid sequence induction (with or without cricoid force); head-up induction; gastric tube to empty the stomach before induction and extubation; awake tracheal extubation.

SGLT2 inhibitors (Anaesthesia 2025 and SPAQI/BJA 2026)

  • Anaesthesia 2025 (UK consensus): omit SGLT2 inhibitors the day before and the day of the procedure. Restart once eating and drinking normally (usually 24–48 h after surgery). If staying in hospital, check capillary ketones are < 0.6 mmol/L before restarting. For patients on a very low-energy / liver-reduction diet, stop the SGLT2i at the commencement of the diet.
  • SPAQI 2026 (multidisciplinary consensus): a tailored, risk-stratified approach based on diabetes status, heart failure (HF), chronic kidney disease (CKD), surgery type, and dietary factors — continue in many lower-risk scenarios while monitoring for eDKA, and discontinue in higher-risk scenarios. See the expanded SGLT2i section below.
Why the change? Both papers recognise that SGLT2 inhibitors have important cardiorenal benefits that may be lost on discontinuation, and that eDKA — while serious — is rare and can be detected and mitigated with monitoring, dextrose and insulin. GLP-1 RAs have a very low hypoglycaemia risk and their gastric-emptying effect wanes with continued use (tachyphylaxis); the 2025 UK consensus therefore favours continuation with aspiration risk mitigation rather than routine cessation.
Why it matters — epidemiology & risk Background
  • ~536 million adults (20–79 yrs) worldwide had diabetes in 2019 — global prevalence 10.5% (IDF).
  • Diabetes is more prevalent in surgical patients than in the general population: a meta-analysis of 90 studies (866,427 surgical records) found an overall prevalence of 17%.
  • Highest in cardiovascular surgery (up to 39%), followed by orthopaedic surgery; T2DM prevalence in bariatric surgery is 26%.
  • Diabetes in surgical patients is associated with more perioperative complications, longer hospital stay, higher morbidity and mortality.
  • Poor glycaemic control exacerbates these risks: higher incidence of surgical site infection (SSI), delayed wound healing, cardiovascular events and respiratory complications.
Pathophysiology & types of diabetes Table 1

In health, SGLT1 and GLUT transporters facilitate glucose uptake after oral intake; GLP-1 secreted by intestinal L-cells stimulates insulin secretion; insulin drives glucose into cells (glycolysis) and excess is stored as glycogen.

In diabetes this regulation is impaired. Most patients have T2DM (insulin resistance and/or insufficient insulin production), but around 10% of adults have T1DM or other less common forms — each with distinct perioperative implications.

Paediatrics Don't assume the type
  • Diabetes is one of the most common chronic diseases in children; incidence of both T1DM and T2DM is rising, partly driven by childhood obesity.
  • 20–40% of patients with newly diagnosed T1DM are obese.
  • Historically T2DM accounted for ~10% of paediatric patients; today it accounts for >30% in the USA.
  • Obesity-induced insulin resistance can unmask T1DM earlier, and obesity influences progression of islet autoimmunity.
Clinical pearl A child with DM does not necessarily have T1DM, and a child with obesity and DM does not necessarily have T2DM. Type matters — requirements and management differ.
Preoperative assessment & goals Pre-op

Primary assessment

  • Type of diabetes.
  • Antihyperglycaemic treatment (including pumps, CGMs, GLP-1 RAs, SGLT2 inhibitors).
  • Quality of glycaemic control (HbA1c).
  • Severity of diabetes-related complications: cardiovascular disease, chronic renal failure, autonomic dysfunction, delayed gastric emptying.

Secondary goals

  • Optimise glycaemic control and general prehabilitation (diet and exercise interventions improve glycaemic control and general health).
  • A structured perioperative pathway can increase efficiency and reduce elective waiting lists.

Risk profile to keep in mind

  • Higher prevalence of (advanced) coronary artery disease → risk of postoperative (silent) myocardial ischaemia and perioperative mortality after non-cardiac surgery.
  • Autonomic dysfunction and peripheral neuropathy increase the risk of silent ischaemia.
  • Diabetes is a risk factor for stroke, congestive heart failure and surgical site infection.
HbA1c — thresholds & the postponement dilemma Figure 1

2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline on non-cardiac surgery (NCS):

  • (i) Class IIa: In patients at high surgical risk, consider screening for increased HbA1c before major surgery and improving preoperative glucose control.
  • (ii) Class I: In patients with diabetes or disturbed glucose metabolism, a preoperative HbA1c test is recommended if not performed in the previous 3 months. If HbA1c ≥ 8.5% (≥ 69 mmol/mol), postpone elective NCS if safe and practical.

Evidence caveat The evidence behind the strong ESC recommendation is poor. Diabetes, raised preoperative HbA1c and perioperative hyperglycaemia are independently associated with worse outcomes, but optimising preoperative glucose control in patients with a high HbA1c has not been studied in RCTs. The COVID-19 pandemic showed that postponing surgery seriously affects patients' health and quality of life — weigh the lack of evidence for HbA1c lowering against the harm of delay. The authors support improving HbA1c in every patient but are wary of the consequences before surgery.

Screening for unknown diabetes via HbA1c is not further substantiated; the incidence of undiagnosed diabetes — and therefore cost-effectiveness — varies widely with income, resources, national guidelines and primary care quality.

GLP-1 receptor agonists & dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists Updated 2025

Mechanism

  • Endogenous GLP-1 is a gut-derived incretin hormone that lowers glycaemia by stimulating insulin production and secretion from pancreatic β-cells and by reducing glucagon secretion from α-cells.
  • Also inhibits gastric emptying and reduces appetite → less food intake, weight loss, improved glycaemic control — but also the main adverse effect: nausea.
  • Pancreatic effects are hyperglycaemia-dependent → risk of hypoglycaemia is extremely low.
  • Dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists (e.g. tirzepatide) add GIP receptor agonism; GIP increases glucagon secretion and works on skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity, while GLP-1 works mainly on the liver.

Benefits & expanding indications

  • Large cardiovascular outcome trials: lower rates of myocardial infarction, stroke and revascularisation.
  • Initially second-line for T2DM; now expanding to weight loss in obesity (regardless of T2DM).
  • Tirzepatide produces mean weight loss of > 20% over 72 weeks in people without diabetes.
  • Dual and triple agonists (GLP-1, GLP-2, glucagon, GIP) are arriving; use is expected to increase significantly.
  • Perioperative data suggest acute use may improve glycaemic control, lower postoperative insulin requirements, reduce hypoglycaemia and improve cardiovascular outcomes.

Perioperative recommendation 2025 UK consensus

Continue GLP-1 RAs throughout the perioperative period The 2025 multidisciplinary consensus (Association of Anaesthetists, Association of British Clinical Diabetologists, BOMSS, CPOC, JBDS, RCoA, SOBA, UKCPA) recommends continuing GLP-1 RAs and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists, with individualised aspiration risk assessment, risk stratification and mitigation.

Why the change from routine cessation?

  • Delayed gastric emptying is most notable with recent commencement or dose escalation; tachyphylaxis occurs with prolonged use.
  • Even one week of cessation is associated with a high risk of residual gastric content in patients who fasted appropriately; withholding for up to three weeks has also been associated with increased gastric volume.
  • Case reports of pulmonary aspiration exist, but the evidence for routine cessation is weak and the harms of cessation (loss of glycaemic and cardiovascular benefit) are real.

Aspiration risk mitigation — what to consider

  • Shared decision-making with the patient about aspiration risk and mitigation strategies.
  • Adhere to standard fasting guidelines; upper GI symptoms alone should not be used to determine gastric content.
  • Consider point-of-care gastric ultrasound before induction, if appropriate, to facilitate risk stratification.
  • Consider regional anaesthesia as the primary technique, if appropriate.
  • Anaesthesia and airway management aimed at reducing aspiration risk on induction, during maintenance and after emergence. This may include:
    • Prokinetics
    • Tracheal tube
    • Modified rapid sequence induction (with or without cricoid force, per local practice)
    • Head-up position for induction
    • Gastric tube to empty the stomach before induction and extubation
    • Awake tracheal extubation
Drug names GLP-1 RAs all end in “-natides” (e.g. exenatide, lixisenatide) or “-glutides” (e.g. liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide). Dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists include tirzepatide. Half-lives range from a few hours to a week (once-weekly preparations).
SGLT2 inhibitors — tailored perioperative management Updated 2026

Mechanism & benefits

  • Induce renal glucose excretion; initially only moderately effective on glycaemic control.
  • Large cardiovascular outcome trials in T2DM: improved major cardiovascular outcomes, reduced heart failure hospitalisation and slowed progression of chronic kidney disease.
  • Effects on heart and kidney are unlikely to be mediated by glucose control alone — possibly via low-grade hyperketonaemia improving cardiac myocyte mitochondrial efficiency, plus osmotic diuresis reducing cardiac workload and glomerular pressure.
  • Indications now expanding to heart failure and CKD even without diabetes; co-first-line with metformin in high cardiovascular risk.
  • Half-life ~12–13 h; no active metabolites. However, the pharmacodynamic effect on urinary glucose excretion lasts well beyond effective plasma concentrations — at 24 h only ~25% of plasma drug remains, but glycosuria continues beyond 48 h. At 72 h, < 1% remains and urinary glucose excretion normalises.
  • Drug names all end in “-gliflozins” (canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, bexagliflozin, sotagliflozin).

Euglycaemic DKA (eDKA) — the main perioperative concern

eDKA — can occur with normal glucose SGLT2 inhibitors lower glucose and change the insulin/glucagon ratio, predisposing to ketosis. Surgical stress and counter-regulatory hormones further drive hyperketonaemia. Ketones can rise quickly to > 3.0 mmol/L and pH may fall to < 7.3 — the threshold for DKA — while glucose remains < 11.0 mmol/L (hence “euglycaemic”). Do not rely on glucose alone to exclude DKA.

Key eDKA risk data

  • Perioperative eDKA rates are higher than non-operative rates: reported at 6.40 per 1000 person-years.
  • A large population-based cohort of > 147,000 surgical patients with T2DM found an elevated incidence rate ratio (IRR 6.33, 95% CI 5.57–7.18) in SGLT2i users vs non-users.
  • Meta-analysis of 22 trials (80,235 patients): SGLT2i increased eDKA risk vs control (RR 2.32, 95% CI 1.64–3.27).
  • Risk significantly increased in patients with HbA1c > 7.9% (RR 2.24) but not ≤ 7.9%; increased in CKD (RR 2.70) and high atherosclerotic CVD risk trials (RR 2.46), but not significantly in HF trials.
  • Some meta-analyses show no difference vs placebo in T2DM — the picture is nuanced.

Risk factors for eDKA

  • T2DM — a major risk factor; very few cases in patients without T2DM.
  • Poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c > 8%), insulin use, prior history of DKA.
  • Reduction or omission of insulin doses — commonly recommended perioperatively — further enhances risk.
  • Dietary factors: carbohydrate-restricting or ketogenic diets, prolonged perioperative fasting, dehydration.
  • Acute illness leading to poor oral intake, caloric reduction, intravascular depletion and stress hormone release.
  • Surgical stress: cardiac and major non-cardiac surgery are particularly high-risk; bariatric surgery is also high-risk due to prolonged carbohydrate restriction.

  • Postoperative monitoring for eDKA in patients continuing SGLT2i within 3 days (4 days for ertugliflozin) and:
    • Cardiac surgery, irrespective of SGLT2i continuation (C).
    • Major non-cardiac surgery, if T2DM (C).
    • A very low-carbohydrate diet > 1 week (e.g. bariatric surgery diet) (C).
  • Restart SGLT2i only when patients resume normal oral intake (E).

Monitoring & rescue — practical points

  • Most eDKA cases are detected after surgery.
  • Patients having ambulatory procedures without PONV and eating normally do not need routine postoperative laboratory testing; SGLT2i can be resumed. Educate about eDKA symptoms (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting) and advise contacting the prescriber if they occur.
  • For higher-risk procedures or prolonged preoperative carbohydrate fast / very low-carbohydrate diet with SGLT2i continued within 3–4 days, monitor for eDKA until oral intake resumes.
  • Anion gap is a useful screen: a normal anion gap virtually excludes eDKA; an anion gap > 10 alerts to possible eDKA and should prompt serum ketone / β-hydroxybutyrate and arterial pH measurement, with consideration of dextrose or insulin infusions.
  • β-hydroxybutyrate: > 0.5 mM indicates ketosis; ≥ 3 mM highly suggestive of DKA; intermediate values ≥ 1.5 mM warrant further monitoring; < 1.5 mM helps exclude DKA.
  • Urine ketones can be used if serum unavailable, but sensitivity is lower (detects acetoacetate, a metabolite of β-hydroxybutyrate), which can delay diagnosis.
Dextrose / insulin strategy Dextrose: low-rate infusion (e.g. 50–100 mL/h of 5% dextrose) in patients with T2DM without hyperglycaemia; omit if glucose > 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L).

Insulin: consider for perioperative hyperglycaemia and selectively in addition to dextrose in T2DM to prevent ketosis. Concomitant insulin may not be necessary in well-controlled T2DM not using insulin, but should be considered in those who are “insulin deficient” — higher HbA1c, high-dose insulin or multiple oral hypoglycaemic agents, or prior DKA. Target glucose 140–180 mg/dL (7.8–10 mmol/L) per institutional protocol.

Potential harms of stopping SGLT2i

  • Small study (n = 64): increased total major perioperative complications (25% vs 7.6%, P = 0.04) and poorer glycaemic control after surgery in patients who withheld SGLT2i; trend to increased AKI (18.8% vs 4.6%, P = 0.05).
  • Cessation in patients receiving SGLT2i for heart failure may be associated with worsening heart failure.
  • Perioperative SGLT2i use is associated with ~31% reduction in postoperative AKI in large database studies, and lower MACE and mortality up to 180 days.
Balance Weigh the cardiorenal benefits of continuing SGLT2i against the risk of eDKA. In many lower-risk scenarios, the benefits likely outweigh the risks, especially when monitoring and dextrose/insulin rescue are available. In higher-risk scenarios (cardiac surgery with T2DM without HF/CKD, prolonged fasting, very low-carbohydrate diets), discontinuation is advised.

Perioperative insulin treatment Core practice

General

  • Insulin preparations differ little pharmacodynamically but mainly in pharmacokinetics — from (ultra-)short-acting to long-acting and mixed preparations, requiring one to several injections daily or continuous infusion via a pump.
  • The literature does not support one specific protocol. Adhere to your institutional protocol (and keep it updated), even though local protocols vary considerably between hospitals.

Fasting dose adjustments

  • Reduce long-acting insulin analogues to 75–80% of the usual dose.
  • Reduce intermediate-acting insulin by 50%.

When to switch to i.v. insulin

  • T1DM, and T2DM on insulin therapy, especially when the fasting period exceeds one missed meal, or when an insulin pump cannot be used.
  • Emergency surgery, or persistent hyperglycaemia.
  • Use a variable-rate i.v. insulin infusion (VRIII) or a combined glucose–insulin infusion.
Safety — always give substrate Patients should always receive a glucose infusion alongside any VRIII. Iatrogenic hypoglycaemia usually occurs because the glucose substrate was stopped while the insulin infusion continued.

Practical option: add 1/8 of the total daily insulin dose (minimum 6 units) to 500 mL glucose 5% infused at 83 mL/h. Historically potassium was added (GIK); however, postoperative potassium concentrations are comparable between glucose–insulin and GIK infusions, so GIK is not required for that purpose.

Glycaemic targets on the day of surgery

  • Emphasis is on avoiding hypoglycaemia rather than hyperglycaemia — under anaesthesia and in recovery the patient cannot report hypoglycaemic symptoms, and missed hypoglycaemia is more harmful than hyperglycaemia.
  • The lower target is raised to 5.6 mmol/L to prevent severe hypoglycaemia in fasting patients at risk.
  • Perioperative/ICU hypoglycaemia (glucose < 5.6 mmol/L) is usually iatrogenic and associated with poor outcomes.
  • Treat as an outpatient: i.v. glucose 15 g, recheck after 15 min (the 15–15 rule).
  • Never withhold insulin entirely in T1DM or highly insulin-dependent T2DM — insulin deficiency causes ketoacidosis.

Intraoperative monitoring & treatment

  • Measure glucose every 1–2 h, depending on stability.
  • Treat intraoperative hyperglycaemia (glucose > 10 mmol/L) with an i.v. correction bolus (sliding scale) or by adjusting the VRIII.
  • Avoid repeated subcutaneous boluses more often than every 2–3 h — unreliable subcutaneous perfusion and risk of insulin stacking.
  • Especially important in children with T1DM: obtain the child's own correction factor / insulin sensitivity factor (expected fall in glucose per 1 unit of insulin).
Quick calculation If the correction factor is unavailable: correction factor = 100 ÷ average total daily insulin dose → gives the expected glucose decrease in mmol/L per 1 unit of insulin.
Diabetes apparatus — pumps, CGMs & closed-loop Devices

Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) pumps

  • First pump developed by Dr Arnold Kadish in 1960; commercial availability took another 24 years; use grew after the DCCT trial in the 1990s showed intensive insulin treatment prevents diabetes-related complications.
  • Now > 50% of patients with T1DM use a CSII; use in T2DM is also increasing. T1D Exchange registry: pump use rose from 57% to 64% (2010–2018); the largest increase is in children, 60–70% of whom are now treated with a CSII.
  • Mimics physiological insulin secretion with continuous (ultra-)short-acting infusion plus mealtime boluses; both basal rate and boluses are easily adjustable → more flexibility.
  • Significantly improves glycaemic control without increasing hypoglycaemia, and improves patient satisfaction and quality of life.
Perioperative pump use Manufacturers state the device should not be used in the presence of imaging devices or electrocautery. However, switching to i.v. insulin or multiple daily injections increases the risk of ketoacidosis and hypoglycaemia from miscalculation or delayed initiation of i.v. insulin.

Adult and paediatric guidelines agree: an insulin pump may be used perioperatively unless there is an absolute contraindication — MRI or anticipated significant fluid shifts.

Continuous glucose monitors (CGM)

  • Use has risen in parallel with pumps — currently > 50% of patients under 12 yrs and around 30% of those aged 12–18 yrs use a CGM in daily life.
  • All subcutaneous sensors have comparable accuracy and have been used successfully in many paediatric patients.
Why CGM accuracy is unreliable perioperatively
  • Hypotension and hypothermia during surgery/anaesthesia may reduce subcutaneous perfusion.
  • Pressure on the device and underlying tissue can influence readings.
  • Some medications (e.g. i.v. paracetamol) can cause false high readings with certain sensors.
If a CGM is left in place, perform regular blood glucose checks to guide perioperative decisions. A small study of a flash glucose monitoring system showed promising results in critically ill patients, and the technology is evolving quickly.

Closed-loop systems ("artificial pancreas")

  • Combine CGM, CSII and escape glucagon infusions; the first hybrid closed-loop system became commercially available in 2016. Pumps adapt insulin infusion using CGM data, the target range and mealtime information entered by the patient.
  • Perioperatively, all the CSII/CGM caveats above apply.
  • In research, closed-loop systems have been used successfully during surgery and in hospital non-critical care, with additional hourly point-of-care testing to monitor functioning.
  • Only possible and advisable when all parties agree to continue it, know how it works, and have a proper backup plan.
Anaesthetic considerations — dexamethasone, magnesium, regional Anaesthesia

Dexamethasone

  • Commonly used as an antiemetic; as a potent glucocorticoid it causes dose-dependent hyperglycaemia, most notable on the first day after surgery, peaking 8–16 h after dosing.
  • PADDI trial (> 8800 patients randomised to dexamethasone 8 mg vs placebo): no difference in the primary endpoint of surgical site infection — 8.1% vs 9.1% (95% CI −2.1 to 0.3).
  • Diabetes subgroup (n = 1065): similar result — 11.2% vs 13.6% (95% CI −6.6 to 1.6).
  • PONV efficacy confirmed: 42% vs 54%, RR 0.78 (95% CI 0.75–0.82).
Bottom line Although dexamethasone increases perioperative hyperglycaemia, it does not increase surgical site infections — these results should reassure clinicians using dexamethasone for PONV in patients with diabetes.

Magnesium

  • Double-blinded RCT (n = 122) in diabetic patients: magnesium sulphate 15 mg/kg/h from 20 min before surgery until 24 h after surgery vs i.v. saline 0.9%.
  • Magnesium group had lower blood glucose and insulin requirements — mean postoperative glucose 8.8 (1.5) vs 9.7 (1.5) mmol/L.
  • Proposed mechanisms: increased insulin affinity to its receptors, increased pancreatic insulin secretion, potentiated insulin-mediated glucose uptake, regulation of hepatic glycogenolysis and glucose output, modulation of catabolic hormone release and glucose translocation into the cell.

Regional anaesthesia in diabetic neuropathy

  • Diabetic neuropathy is caused by multiple pathways, primarily persistent chronic hyperglycaemia → inflammation and oxidative stress → small-vessel changes, local ischaemia and reduced axonal conduction velocity.
  • Affected nerves are more sensitive to local anaesthetics → longer duration of nerve block.
  • Suggestions that local anaesthetics are more toxic to affected nerves are not conclusively evidenced — neuropathy should not be a reason to deny regional anaesthesia when there is a valid indication.
  • Caution with electrical stimulation alone: the threshold of affected nerves is significantly increased, theoretically increasing the risk of needle trauma.
  • With peripheral nerve catheters, be aware of an increased risk of catheter-related infection.
Future directions — carbohydrate loading & individualised targets Evolving

Preoperative carbohydrate loading (CHL)

  • Integrated into numerous ERAS guidelines; the principal objective is to prevent the fasting state, thereby inhibiting catabolic pathways.
  • Evidence suggests CHL reduces cortisol, interleukin-6 and insulin resistance, attenuating the surgery-associated inflammatory response, and improves comfort by alleviating hunger, thirst, malaise, anxiety and nausea.
  • Safety in diabetes is uncertain — CHL may potentially increase aspiration risk from delayed gastric emptying or gastro-oesophageal reflux.
  • However, small ultrasound studies found no difference in gastric volume between patients with T2DM and those without, 2 h before surgery. Small studies also showed improved glycaemic control after CHL in T2DM when insulin was prescribed to cover the carbohydrate load.

HbA1c-adjusted glycaemic targets

  • "One-fits-all" protocols are inadequate: a morbidly obese T2DM patient with very low insulin sensitivity is not comparable to a T2DM patient on metformin alone, or to a patient with T1DM.
  • Analogy with chronic hypertension: just as perioperative MAP targets are adjusted as a percentage of the patient's usual MAP, chronic hyperglycaemia should shift the upper and lower bounds of the target glucose range upwards.
  • No RCTs prove improved outcomes, but there is indirect evidence: the relationship between acute hyperglycaemia and ICU mortality dissolves in patients with pre-existing longer-term hyperglycaemia (HbA1c > 53 mmol/mol) — suggesting hyperglycaemia may be a risk factor mainly in patients who are usually well regulated.
  • The inpatient specialised diabetes team can be essential in managing diabetes perioperatively.
Postoperative management Post-op
  • Surgical site infection is the most commonly cited postoperative complication related to perioperative glucose control.
  • Surgical stress leads to hyperglycaemia and impedes postoperative glucose control in patients with DM, increasing the risk of complications — especially SSI.
  • A meta-analysis showed that postoperative VRIII with intensive monitoring in ICU resulted in lower mean glucose values and reduced SSI.
  • Small intervention studies on regular wards found a sliding-scale regimen led to higher mean glucose and more SSI compared with a basal–bolus regimen.
ADA recommendation Use a basal–bolus regimen on the postoperative ward in preference to sliding-scale insulin alone.

Definition: sliding scale = a protocol prescribing single bolus, correctional doses of short-acting insulin without changes to continuous or long-acting insulin doses.

  • Many hospitals still use sliding-scale regimens despite their ineffectiveness in achieving adequate in-hospital glucose control, possibly out of fear of hypoglycaemia.
  • Reassuringly, the risk of severe hypoglycaemia (< 2.3 mmol/L) is comparable between sliding-scale and basal–bolus regimens.
  • Once patients have adequate oral intake and are ready for discharge, they can return to their regular medication.
Diabetic emergencies — DKA & HHS Emergency
Feature Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) Hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar syndrome (HHS)
Typical patient Primarily T1DM (can also affect T2DM). Typically T2DM, especially older adults with hypertension and kidney disease.
Mechanism Insulin deficiency → hyperglycaemia, increased lipolysis and ketone production; body relies on fat breakdown → ketones accumulate → metabolic acidosis. Severe hyperglycaemia and extreme dehydration; insulin still circulates (preventing ketone production) but is insufficient for adequate glycaemic control.
Glucose ADA diagnostic criteria: glucose > 13.9 mmol/L. > 33.3 mmol/L.
Ketones / acid–base Ketones present; metabolic acidosis. Relative lack of ketones; pH > 7.3; hyperosmolarity from increased solute concentration.
Clinical clues Classical signs (polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss) but can mimic an upper respiratory chest infection (tachypnoea) or gastroenteritis (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting). Mental changes, marked dehydration.

Paediatric DKA — high index of suspicion Approximately one-third of children diagnosed with T1DM present with DKA at diagnosis, with a peak between ages 5 and 7. Conclusions Summary
  • Diabetes is one of the most common comorbidities in patients presenting for surgery.
  • Besides T2DM, other forms such as T1DM have specific clinical features and perioperative concerns.
  • Thorough preoperative evaluation of antihyperglycaemic treatment and long-term regulation (HbA1c), together with possible diabetes-related complications, is recommended.
  • Clinicians should adhere to local guidelines for perioperative antihyperglycaemic treatment and insulin schedules — and these should be updated frequently, as the evidence is evolving quickly.
  • GLP-1 RAs: the 2025 UK consensus recommends continuing these drugs perioperatively with aspiration risk stratification and mitigation.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: guidance is evolving. The 2025 UK consensus recommends omitting the day before and day of the procedure. The 2026 SPAQI consensus recommends a tailored, risk-stratified approach — continue in many lower-risk scenarios with monitoring, discontinue in higher-risk scenarios.
  • Shared decision-making is advised when there is a wish to continue using subcutaneous insulin pumps or continuous glucose monitoring devices intraoperatively.
  • A basal–bolus regimen on the ward leads to better glycaemic control than a sliding-scale regimen postoperatively.
