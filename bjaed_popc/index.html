1Definition & Clinical Burden▶

Definition

Pulmonary abnormalities after surgery that result in identifiable disease or dysfunction and adversely affect the patient's clinical outcome. Definitions vary between trials (a major source of heterogeneity), but the core composite is:

Core components

  • Respiratory failure
  • Pneumonia / respiratory infection
  • Atelectasis
  • Pleural effusion
  • Pneumothorax
  • Bronchospasm
  • Aspiration pneumonitis

Sometimes counted separately

  • ARDS
  • Pulmonary embolism
  • Pulmonary oedema
  • Exacerbation of pre-existing lung disease

Why it matters

PPCs are the most important independent determinant of 30-day mortality after non-cardiothoracic surgery, and are at least as common as cardiac complications. In elderly patients they may predict long-term mortality better than cardiac complications.

Incidence: up to 12.5% of elective postoperative patients; 5.8% after major abdominal surgery. Postoperative respiratory failure is the most common serious PPC; atelectasis is the most frequent.

Upper abdominal incision model (Scholes et al.)

Prospective cohort, n = 268, upper abdominal incisions only. Five independent risk factors:

  • Duration of anaesthesia
  • Surgical category
  • Respiratory co-morbidity
  • Current smoker
  • Predicted maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O₂max)

Score ≤2.02 → OR 8.41 (3.33–21.26) for PPC. Requires external validation before clinical use.

Bottom line: models have advanced understanding but lack of consensus and complexity mean they remain impractical for routine clinical use. Use them to inform — not replace — clinical judgement and consent.
4Pathophysiology — Why PPCs Happen▶

Onset: the moment consciousness is lost

Adverse respiratory effects of general anaesthesia begin immediately after induction and the respiratory system may take up to 6 weeks to return to its preoperative state after major surgery.

Mechanisms of atelectasis

1. Compression

Direct compression of lung tissue by the displaced diaphragm and reduced chest wall cross-sectional area.

2. Airway closure

FRC falls below closing volume → small airways collapse in dependent regions.

3. Absorption

Rapid gas absorption from alveoli behind narrowed/closed airways — exacerbated by high FiO₂.

Even 20% nitrogen in the inspired gas helps prevent alveolar collapse. Use of "hyperoxia" (FiO₂ 0.8) throughout GA for surgical site infection reduction does not increase postoperative atelectasis.

Postoperative contributors

Respiratory muscle dysfunction

  • Pain limits excursion; diaphragm splinting
  • Disruption of neurally mediated diaphragmatic function after visceral handling
  • Rapid shallow breathing, ↓ FRC and vital capacity

Impaired clearance

  • Opioids + anaesthesia inhibit cough reflex
  • Impaired mucociliary transport (persists postoperatively, worse with tracheal tube)
  • Dry gases → mucus plugging; sputum retention is common

Result

Reduced FRC + residual atelectasis + ineffective cough + abnormal respiratory control = ideal situation for PPCs to develop.

Key insight for weaning: conditions that promote atelectasis — high FiO₂ and low/zero PEEP — are often used during emergence. Current weaning practice can therefore counteract good intraoperative lung protection and create de novo atelectasis at extubation.

Smoking cessation

Cessation periodEffect on PPCs
<2 weeks before surgeryRisk similar to current smokers; rates may actually be higher — transient ↑ mucus production as bronchial irritation resolves and mucociliary activity improves

Maximise the cessation period. Surgery is a "teachable moment" — patients are almost 3× more likely to remain smoke-free at 12 months with intensive vs minimal cessation support.

Prehabilitation / inspiratory muscle training (IMT)

  • Technique: inspiratory threshold pressure loading — patient generates an inspiratory pressure against a chamber to open the valve.
  • Effect: inspiratory muscle strength improves significantly and is preserved early postoperatively → assists lung expansion and forceful expiratory manoeuvres for secretion clearance.
  • Evidence: preoperative aerobic exercise + IMT reduced PPCs (RR 0.4, 0.23–0.72) and LOS in cardiac and abdominal surgery — not joint replacement. A later meta-analysis: RR 0.48 (0.26–0.89). Cochrane review confirms reduced atelectasis and pneumonia, but quality of evidence is low–moderate (blinding impossible).
  • Practical: time-consuming and expensive (repeated physiotherapist supervision) — reserve for very high-risk patients.

Insufficient data for prehabilitation exercise programmes as a whole; Fair for optimisation of existing cardiorespiratory disease and early smoking cessation.

Protective ventilation in obese patients
  • VT 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight
  • PEEP ≥5 cm H₂O with appropriate recruitment manoeuvres
  • Opening pressure may need up to 55 cm H₂O
  • Respiratory rate to control CO₂ and maintain normal pH
  • Fluid loading before RM and high PEEP reduces cardiovascular compromise

Neuromuscular blockade

  • Poor use of NMBAs — failure to monitor correctly or excessive dosing — is associated with increased PPC risk.
  • Residual block (TOF ratio <0.9) frequently persists after GA despite reversal agents.
  • Quantitative monitoring (e.g. acceleromyography) reduces PPC risk and is superior to qualitative assessment.
  • Neostigmine reversal is associated with a dose-dependent increase in PPC risk — most likely because larger doses are used to reverse deep block. Proper use with quantitative monitoring is likely to reduce PPCs.
  • Sugammadex reliably reverses deep rocuronium/vecuronium block; no studies yet show it reduces PPCs. Linked to laryngospasm and negative-pressure pulmonary oedema if given early with a supraglottic airway.
  • Remifentanil reduces the need for muscle relaxation in appropriate cases, but no studies show reduced PPC incidence.

7Postoperative Strategies▶

Analgesia

  • Pain — especially from upper abdominal wounds — compromises pulmonary function, coughing and mobilisation → atelectasis and pneumonia.
  • Thoracic epidural analgesia may reduce respiratory muscle dysfunction and pain-related hypoventilation in upper abdominal surgery, facilitating early extubation and reducing respiratory failure risk.
  • Reduces PPCs by about one third in open AAA repair, coronary bypass and abdominal surgery.
  • Particularly beneficial in severe COPD having major abdominal surgery (improved analgesia, reduced opioid consumption).
  • Epidural opioids reduced atelectasis (RR 0.53, 0.33–0.85) but not pneumonia vs systemic opioids; epidural local anaesthetic reduced pulmonary infection (RR 0.36, 0.31–0.65) but not atelectasis.
  • Weigh risks: pneumothorax, phrenic nerve paralysis, nerve damage, epidural haematoma.
  • In known/suspected OSA, reduce opioid doses — opioid dose correlates with postoperative OSA severity.

I COUGH bundle — a working postoperative programme

I — Incentive spirometry

10× per hour (3–5 efforts per set) during waking hours until discharge. Device always within reach. Four-hourly documentation of volumes. Practise technique preoperatively.

C — Cough & deep breathe

Every 2 hours.

O — Oral hygiene

Twice-daily teeth brushing and mouthwash.

U — Understanding

Preoperative education via leaflet and video.

G — Get moving

Sit in a chair or head of bed elevated >30°; mobilise three times a day.

H — Head of bed

Elevated >30°.

Reduces pneumonia and unplanned reintubation in general and vascular surgical patients. A combination of physiotherapy, mobilisation and oral hygiene is more beneficial than any single component.

Nasogastric tubes

  • Identified repeatedly as a risk factor for PPCs.
  • Abdominal surgery patients are 5–8× more likely to have a PPC if an NGT is used perioperatively.
  • Meta-analyses show no benefit from routine NGT placement after elective or emergency open abdominal surgery.
  • Reserve for symptom relief or specific surgical reasons.
8Weaning from Mechanical Ventilation in the Operating Room▶
Why this matters: most patients are extubated in the operating room, yet there is no definitive standard for weaning. Expert consensus (2019) gave only weak recommendations for weaning and extubation. Poorly chosen emergence settings (high FiO₂, zero PEEP) can undo good intraoperative lung protection.

Systematic review: 14 RCTs, 1719 patients — 7 studies addressed the weaning phase exclusively, 7 addressed weaning plus induction/maintenance. Overall quality of evidence: low.

Mechanisms supporting this approach

  • PSV increases driving pressure during inspiration → tidal recruitment, promotes lung expansion, reduces work of breathing. Level of support can be titrated to match ventilatory and oxygenation requirements.
  • PEEP counters airway closure and maintains end-expiratory lung volume, preventing or reversing atelectasis during emergence.
  • Low FiO₂ avoids absorption atelectasis and surfactant impairment.
  • Resumption of spontaneous ventilation without ventilatory support during emergence decreases lung volumes — hence the need to actively recruit and maintain volume through the weaning phase.
Recruitment manoeuvres: evidence does not consistently support their clinical utility during weaning, and they are not safe across all scenarios (ART trial showed harm in acute respiratory failure). Bag-squeezing is discouraged; if used, ventilator-driven alveolar recruitment should be performed with the shortest effective time, lowest effective pressure and fewest number of breaths.
Bottom line: based on low-quality evidence, wean patients after surgery with a combination of PSV, individualised PEEP and low FiO₂, pending more definitive studies.
9Bedside Checklist▶

Preoperative

  • Screen for PPC risk: age, ASA, functional status, COPD/CHF, smoking, recent URTI, OSA, SpO₂ on air, albumin, Hb
  • Optimise COPD/asthma — continue inhalers, consider short-term steroids
  • Advise smoking cessation ≥4 weeks (ideally ≥8 weeks) before surgery
  • Delay elective surgery if symptomatic URTI or on antibiotics
  • Identify and treat anaemia (target Hb ≥100 g/L)
  • Consider preoperative IMT/prehabilitation for very high-risk patients
  • Start CPAP preoperatively in severe OSA; assess compliance
  • Antibiotics only if infection is present — not prophylactically for PPC prevention

Intraoperative

  • Set VT 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight
  • PEEP: avoid zero; start low in healthy lungs, titrate up for obesity/pneumoperitoneum/lung disease
  • Keep FiO₂ as low as compatible with SpO₂ ≥92% — avoid 1.0
  • Recruitment manoeuvre if atelectasis suspected, before increasing PEEP
  • Quantitative neuromuscular monitoring if NMBA used
  • Ensure full reversal — TOF ratio ≥0.9 before extubation
  • Avoid neostigmine unless needed and guided by monitoring
  • Goal-directed fluid therapy — avoid overload
  • Nasogastric tube only if indicated — not routine
  • Consider neuraxial technique where appropriate
  • Minimise operative time, especially in high-risk patients

Weaning & emergence

  • Wean on PSV, not unsupported spontaneous ventilation
  • Individualised PEEP during weaning — avoid zero PEEP
  • Low FiO₂ during emergence — avoid 1.0
  • Confirm full neuromuscular recovery before extubation
  • Suction and clear secretions; encourage cough at extubation

Postoperative

  • Effective analgesia — consider thoracic epidural for upper abdominal/open AAA/cardiac surgery
  • Reduce opioid doses in known/suspected OSA
  • Head of bed >30°; sit out of bed; mobilise 3× daily
  • Incentive spirometry 10×/hour while awake; document volumes 4-hourly
  • Deep breathe and cough every 2 hours
  • Oral hygiene — teeth brushing and mouthwash twice daily
  • Consider CPAP for postoperative hypoxaemia (especially OSA or unable to do spirometry)
  • Avoid routine nasogastric tube; remove as soon as clinically appropriate
