Quick reference · Anaesthetists & Intensivists

Peri-operative glucocorticoids in adrenal insufficiency

Association of Anaesthetists · Royal College of Physicians · Society for Endocrinology UK — 2020 guidelines, including published correction

Adult — major surgery

Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction, then 200 mg/24 h continuous IV infusion until double the usual oral dose is tolerated, then taper to maintenance (usually ≤48 h; up to 1 week if major/complicated).

Child — major surgery

Hydrocortisone 2 mg/kg IV at induction, then weight-based infusion or 2 mg/kg 4-hourly IV/IM. Double oral dose for 48 h once enteral, then taper over up to a week.

Obstetric — delivery

Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at onset of labour, then 200 mg/24 h infusion or 50 mg IM 6-hourly. Rapid taper over 1–3 days after uncomplicated delivery.

Golden rule

If in doubt — give. There are no long-term adverse consequences of short-term glucocorticoid administration. Delays may prove fatal.

▶ 1 · Who is at risk? Core

Two classical categories

  • Primary adrenal insufficiency — disease of the adrenal gland itself (e.g. Addison's disease / autoimmune adrenalitis, congenital adrenal hyperplasia). Cortisol and aldosterone deficiency.
  • Secondary adrenal insufficiency — deficient ACTH from the pituitary, or deficient CRH from the hypothalamus. Cortisol deficiency, but aldosterone continues to be secreted in response to renin.

The big one: prescribed glucocorticoids (tertiary adrenal insufficiency)

Recommendation 1 Prescribed glucocorticoid therapy — prednisolone ≥5 mg/day in adults, or a hydrocortisone-equivalent dose of 10–15 mg·m⁻²/day in children — across all routes (oral, inhaled, topical, intranasal, intra-articular), can suppress the hypothalamo–pituitary–adrenal axis and is the most common cause of adrenal insufficiency that anaesthetists will encounter.
  • Suppression may occur after 1 month or more of such therapy.
  • Approximately 7 in 1000 people are prescribed long-term oral corticosteroids — roughly 100× the number with intrinsic deficiency.
  • Inhaled corticosteroids commonly suppress the adrenal response to ACTH, in a dose-dependent manner, even within recommended dose ranges.
  • All steroid-dependent patients are at risk of adrenal crisis.

Paediatric epidemiology

  • Primary adrenal insufficiency: ~1 in 8,000–10,000 children (congenital adrenal hyperplasia most common, ~1 in 15,000 live births).
  • Secondary (ACTH deficiency): ~1 in 10,000 children — congenital hypothalamic/pituitary disorders, or acquired (brain tumours and their treatment).
  • Glucocorticoid treatment for asthma, renal disease, juvenile chronic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease and Duchenne muscular dystrophy accounts for a significant proportion of cases.
Recommendation 2 All glucocorticoid-dependent patients are at risk of adrenal crisis as a consequence of surgical stress or illness. Be able to recognise and diagnose this medical emergency. If in doubt about the need for glucocorticoids, give them — there are no long-term adverse consequences of short-term administration.
▶ 2 · Adult — the core regimen Do this
Recommendation 4 Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction, followed by a continuous infusion of hydrocortisone 200 mg·24 h⁻¹ until the patient can take double their usual oral glucocorticoid dose by mouth. This regimen is preferred above others due to enhanced safety.
  1. Induction: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV.
  2. Immediately start a continuous IV infusion of hydrocortisone 200 mg/24 h.
  3. Continue the infusion while nil by mouth.
  4. Convert to enteral glucocorticoid at double the usual oral replacement dose once taking orally.
  5. Taper back to the appropriate maintenance dose — in most cases within 48 h; up to a week if surgery is more major or complicated. Use clinical judgement.
Worked example from the guideline If the usual replacement dose is 10–5–5 mg hydrocortisone, double it to 20–10–10 mg post-operatively, then taper back to maintenance.

If IV infusion is impractical

Intramuscular administration may be prescribed: hydrocortisone 50 mg IM every 6 h is an accepted alternative to the 200 mg/24 h infusion.

Critical illness and complications

Recommendation 5 Major complications and critical illness excite a prolonged stress response. Any glucocorticoid supplementation should reflect this pattern — continue the continuous IV infusion while the patient remains unwell in critical care, rather than stepping down to oral replacement.

Dexamethasone

Recommendation 6 Dexamethasone is not adequate as glucocorticoid treatment in patients with primary adrenal insufficiency, as it has no mineralocorticoid activity.

Where dexamethasone 6–8 mg IV has been used (e.g. for other indications), it will suffice for 24 h of steroid cover — equivalent to approximately 200 mg of hydrocortisone in the form of a long-acting glucocorticoid (corrected value; see Correction notice below).

Key principle Adrenal replacement dosing following surgical stress or illness is in addition to the patient's usual steroid treatment — not instead of it.

▶ 4 · Children Paediatric
Recommendation 7 Children with adrenal insufficiency are more vulnerable to problems with glycaemic control than adults and require frequent blood glucose monitoring. They can be treated with a bolus of hydrocortisone at induction followed by an immediate continuous infusion, or alternatively a bolus at induction followed by 4-hourly IV boluses post-operatively. Minimise fasting and prioritise these patients on routine operating lists.

Paediatric safety points

  • No child with adrenal insufficiency should be fasted for more than 6 h.
  • Start hourly blood glucose checks if pre-operative fasting exceeds 4 h.
  • After surgery, check blood glucose every hour until enteral intake resumes.
  • Children should continue their regular hydrocortisone doses until the time of surgery — or receive hydrocortisone intravenously.
  • All children with known glucocorticoid deficiency, or at risk of it (significant exogenous glucocorticoid >10–15 mg·m⁻²/day), should receive IV hydrocortisone at induction.
  • Diabetes insipidus + adrenal insufficiency: cortisol is required to excrete a water load. With DDAVP treatment, inadequate peri-operative cortisol risks water intoxication and hyponatraemia. Strict fluid balance with adequate cortisol replacement is mandatory.
▶ 5 · Obstetrics Pregnancy
Recommendation 8 Women may require a higher maintenance dose during the later stages of pregnancy (from the 20th week onwards), and stress-dose supplementation using hydrocortisone 100 mg at the onset of labour, then either a continuous IV infusion of hydrocortisone 200 mg·24 h⁻¹ or 50 mg IM every 6 h until after delivery.
  • Adrenal insufficiency is rarely encountered for the first time in pregnancy.
  • Pregnancy does not influence the severity of the disease, but may delay diagnosis — signs and symptoms may be misattributed to pregnancy itself.
  • Serum total and free cortisol are normally increased by 20–40% in pregnancy, so a higher maintenance dose may be advisable in later pregnancy.
  • Peri- and post-operative surgical stress doses in pregnancy are the same as for other adults.
  • Timing of the first dose: 100 mg hydrocortisone at the onset of active labour — contractions every 5 min for the last hour, or cervical dilatation >4 cm.
  • After an uncomplicated delivery, rapid taper (over 1–3 days) back to the regular replacement dose.
▶ 6 · Patients on therapeutic glucocorticoids Nuance

In general there is little or no conclusive evidence on which to base recommendations in this population. A pragmatic, individualised approach is required — blanket recommendations are not appropriate.

What the evidence shows

  • A daily glucocorticoid dose equivalent to prednisolone ≥5 mg for longer than 1 month represents an adrenal-suppressive dose in a proportion of adults.
  • Short synacthen test data in patients on 5–20 mg prednisolone daily show that roughly one third to a half do not achieve the target cortisol concentration.
  • A precise dose–response relationship could not be demonstrated — but a 5 mg prednisolone dose (or standard-dose inhaled glucocorticoid) can be associated with inadequate adrenal cortisol reserve in a significant number of patients.
  • Systematic review (Marik & Varon) and Gibbison et al. found no evidence that routine peri-operative doses higher than the therapeutic dose improve haemodynamic or mortality outcomes — they are "probably not required". However the evidence base is weak, heterogeneous and small.
Guideline position Given the risks of an inadequate glucocorticoid response — and the lack of long-term harm from supplementation — the guideline supports balanced, individualised glucocorticoid supplementation in addition to ongoing treatment during the peri-operative period (and by extension whenever patients are subjected to any physiological stress).

Factors to weigh

  • Dose and duration of glucocorticoid therapy
  • The condition being treated
  • The degree of physiological stress anticipated

Individual patients may therefore need no supplementation, a single dose, or a regimen delivered over a number of days. See Table 2 for broad recommendations.

Again If in doubt about the need for glucocorticoids, give them — there are no long-term adverse consequences of short-term glucocorticoid administration.
▶ 7 · Adrenal crisis — recognition Emergency
Critical Patients with previously undiagnosed adrenal insufficiency sometimes present for the first time following the stress of surgery. Anaesthetists must be familiar with the symptoms and signs of acute adrenal insufficiency so that inadequate supplementation or undiagnosed adrenal insufficiency is detected and treated promptly. Delays may prove fatal.

Recognise the traps

  • Relative adrenal insufficiency — symptoms and signs of adrenal crisis can occur in physiologically stressed patients while plasma cortisol levels are normal, or even high.
  • Plasma sodium is often, but not always, low.
  • C-reactive protein may be raised, but is of limited value in the post-operative period.
  • Persistent pyrexia may be due to adrenal insufficiency, but is usually attributed to post-operative sepsis and treated with antimicrobials. Steroid supplementation should not be reduced or withdrawn while the patient is pyrexial.
  • Around half of patients affected by adrenal crisis report a previous crisis — usually precipitated by gastroenteritis or fever, but also by surgical episodes, pregnancy, emotional distress and other triggers.
  • Patients with comorbidities (notably asthma and diabetes) are more vulnerable. Patients with mineralocorticoid or vasopressin dependency are less stable than secondary adrenal patients with intact mineralocorticoid function.

Prevention on the ward

  • In a British survey, 8.6% of patients with chronic adrenal insufficiency reported a previous adrenal crisis caused by insufficient glucocorticoid medication during an inpatient stay (e.g. for elective surgery). Some were due to inadequate dosing; others to medication errors and omissions on the wards.
  • Many patients with a long-standing diagnosis report that ward staff dismiss their observations about warning signs of under-replacement during illness or after surgery.
  • It is vital that physiological replacement therapy is not interrupted, and that the daily dose is increased at times of physiological stress.
For crisis management detail See the Society for Endocrinology Emergency Guidance: Emergency management of acute adrenal insufficiency (adrenal crisis) in adults (Arlt W, SfE Clinical Committee, 2016) — cited as reference 5 in the source guideline.
▶ 8 · Practical peri-operative checklist Checklist

Before the list

  • Take a careful history. Ask specifically about glucocorticoid self-management, previous episodes of adrenal crisis, and how practised the patient is at adjusting medication for illness, injury or post-operative recovery.
  • Do not assume competence. Some patients have never been taught sick-day rules; others are extremely well informed — possibly more so than their anaesthetist.
  • Look for a steroid emergency card, medical alert bracelet/necklace, or a self-administered hydrocortisone injection kit brought to hospital.
  • Collaborate with the endocrinologist when planning scheduled surgery — especially for patients with multiple risk factors (age, comorbidities).
  • Put the patient first on the list to minimise fasting and dehydration, which they tolerate poorly.

Day case surgery

  • Patients having body surface surgery need not be denied day case surgery.
  • It is particularly important to ensure they have adequately recovered and are not suffering from nausea or vomiting before discharge home.
  • Instruct them to return to hospital if they feel unwell or develop nausea or vomiting.
  • Laparoscopic surgery as a day case can be considered, but a 23-h stay protocol is recommended where available.
  • Ensure the patient is familiar with sick day rules for febrile illness, has an injection kit in case of vomiting, and that a companion has been trained to use it.

Feeding and fluids

  • Minimise fasting; convert to enteral glucocorticoid as soon as the patient is taking orally.
  • Maintaining peri-operative fluid balance matters — sodium and water retention secondary to supplementation is a potential concern, but short-term supplementation during uncomplicated surgery carries minimal risk.
  • Glycaemic control is easily achieved; glucocorticoid-induced glycaemia should not deter supplementation.

Where to manage

  • Patients with adrenal insufficiency who develop complications of surgery should be managed in the critical care environment.
  • Continue stress-dose coverage by continuous IV infusion while the patient remains unwell.
Background literature — not a formal guideline recommendation The guideline's reference list includes data on etomidate causing adrenocortical suppression, including after a single induction dose. Worth considering when selecting an induction agent in patients with, or at risk of, adrenal insufficiency.
▶ 9 · Key numbers & evidence base Evidence

Outcomes in adrenal insufficiency

  • Mortality: in a Swedish cohort of 1,675 patients, the risk ratio for all-cause mortality was 2.19 for men and 2.86 for women, with narrow confidence intervals. Excess mortality was attributable to cardiovascular, malignant and infectious diseases.
  • Crisis frequency: adrenal crises requiring hospital treatment occur about 6–8 times per 100 patient-years among patients with primary or secondary adrenal insufficiency.
  • Prospective data: in 423 patients with primary and secondary adrenal insufficiency, the incidence of adrenal crisis was 8.3 per 100 replacement years; two patients died during adrenal crisis over a 2-year follow-up.
  • In-hospital harm: 8.6% of patients with chronic adrenal insufficiency reported a previous crisis caused by insufficient glucocorticoid medication during an inpatient stay.

Physiology

  • Cortisol is produced in the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex; up to 20 mg is released each day.
  • Reference ranges: 140–700 nmol·L⁻¹ at 09:00, 80–350 nmol·L⁻¹ at midnight.
  • Cortisol is highly protein bound — 60–80% to cortisol-binding globulin, 15–35% to albumin; the free active fraction is ~5%.
  • Uncomplicated major elective surgery produces a ~5-fold increase in secretion, to about 100 mg of cortisol per day; plasma cortisol typically returns to baseline within 24–48 h.
  • During protracted critical illness, reduced metabolism and clearance contribute to hypercortisolaemia.

Why these guidelines exist

In 2012 the Association of Anaesthetists, the Society for Endocrinology UK, the Royal College of Anaesthetists and the Royal College of Physicians received a "Rule 43" letter (now a "Report to Prevent Future Deaths") from HM Coroner Sheffield, expressing concern about standards of care for patients with adrenal insufficiency undergoing surgery. This is the first national guidance on the topic integrating endocrinology expertise with the practicalities of anaesthetic and surgical service delivery, covering both adults and children.

Previous guidance The 2005 Addison's Self-Help Group "Surgical guidelines for Addison's Disease and other forms of adrenal insufficiency" were endorsed by NICE Clinical Knowledge Summaries in 2010 and by a European expert consensus statement in 2013. A Cochrane review (2009, revised 2012) was withdrawn in 2012 as the evidence base was very poor, limited in size and quality, and focused mainly on minor dental procedures.
