Smoking & Anaesthesia

Quick reference for anaesthetists & intensivists · based on BJA Education 2019;19(1):1–6

Key clinical points
  • Smoking increases the risk of perioperative morbidity and mortality in a dose-dependent manner.
  • RCoA advice: people who smoke should quit several weeks before surgery and should be especially encouraged not to smoke on the day of operation.
  • There are >4500 chemicals in cigarette smoke; the majority have detrimental effects on human body systems.
  • The preoperative assessment clinic is an opportunity to discuss and encourage smoking cessation.
  • E-cigarettes are used by 2.3 million adults in the UK, but the long-term harm caused is unknown.

Smoke constituents & pathophysiology

Carbon monoxide (gaseous phase)

  • Main component of the gaseous phase; directly impairs oxygen delivery to tissues.
  • Inhalation → increased carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb): 2–12% in smokers vs <1.5% in non-smokers.
  • CO has ~300-fold greater affinity for haemoglobin than oxygen, so COHb formation is favoured over oxyhaemoglobin.
  • The COHb dissociation curve is shifted far to the left of the oxyhaemoglobin curve — P50 is greatly reduced.
  • COHb also causes a left shift of the oxyhaemoglobin curve (partly via reduced 2,3-DPG), further impairing oxygen unloading in tissues.
  • Net effect: hypoxaemia and reduced physiological reserve to maintain PO₂ during physiological stress.

Pulse oximetry pitfall: most saturation monitors cannot distinguish oxyhaemoglobin from COHb and will read an erroneously high SpO₂ in the presence of significant COHb. Use co-oximetry (arterial blood gas analyser) to quantify COHb.

Nicotine (particulate phase)

  • Main component of the particulate phase; a typical cigarette contains ~2 mg.
  • Readily absorbed across the alveolar membrane; crosses the blood–brain barrier and reaches the cerebral circulation within 20 s.
  • Stimulates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors → release of noradrenaline, adrenaline, vasopressin, serotonin, dopamine and β-endorphin.
  • Increases cardiac output and the risk of tachyarrhythmias; at higher doses the stimulant effect diminishes and a sedative/depressant effect predominates.
  • Half-life 30 min; metabolised by CYP2A6/CYP2B6 to metabolites including cotinine (active, detectable up to 20 h).
  • Highly addictive in humans.

Tar

  • The nicotine-free remainder of the particulate phase.
  • Composition and toxicity vary widely between tobacco sources, so tar yield is only a crude measure of toxic potential.
  • Machine-measured yields (high/medium/low) poorly simulate human smoking — smokers can increase intake by blocking ventilation holes or taking deeper/more frequent puffs.

Systemic clinical effects relevant to the perioperative period

Cardiovascular

  • Smoking is the largest preventable cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.
  • 3–4× increase in coronary heart disease.
  • Sympathomimetic effects of nicotine plus CO-mediated reduction in oxyhaemoglobin upset the myocardial oxygen supply/demand balance.
  • Increased incidence of cardiac dysrhythmias with arterial COHb as low as 4–5%.
  • Adverse lipid profile, endothelial injury and development of atherosclerotic plaques.
  • Independent risk factor for peripheral vascular disease, thromboembolic disease and stroke.
  • Strong positive dose-dependent correlation with subarachnoid haemorrhage — association appears greater in women than men.
  • Risk falls in a dose-dependent manner with cessation.

Respiratory

  • Cause of 90% of lung cancers; around 20% of smokers develop COPD.
  • COPD: small airway obstruction, reduced FEV1, emphysematous and bullous change.
  • Acute reduction in airway diameter (bronchoconstrictive reflex to inhaled particles).
  • Premature small airway closure in expiration → increased closing volume and altered V/Q relationships.
  • Hypersensitive airway reflexes → increased perioperative cough, breath-holding, laryngospasm.
  • Increased mucus production and viscosity with impaired mucociliary clearance → sputum retention, pneumonia and respiratory failure.

Gastro-intestinal

  • Relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter → increased GORD and peptic ulcer disease.
  • Reduced postoperative nausea and vomiting in smokers — possibly increased metabolism of volatile agents via CYP2E1.
  • Increased incidence of Crohn's disease; reduced incidence of ulcerative colitis.

Other systems

  • Impaired immune function → poorer wound healing and increased wound infection rates.
  • Abnormal bone metabolism → fracture healing may be delayed.

Passive / involuntary smoking

  • WHO definition: exposure to second-hand tobacco smoke — a mixture of exhaled mainstream smoke and sidestream smoke from a smouldering cigarette, diluted with ambient air.
  • Involves inhaling carcinogens and other toxic components; effects are not limited to the smoker.
Pharmacology & drug interactions
  • Nicotine and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons induce the cytochrome P450 system, particularly CYP1A1, CYP1A2 and CYP2E1.
  • Altered metabolism of: theophylline, caffeine, haloperidol, propranolol and volatile agents.
  • Consistently greater postoperative opioid requirements in patients who smoke — mechanism poorly understood and not simply increased substrate metabolism. Likely contributors: altered pain thresholds and receptor-mediated effects.
  • Chronic nicotine use may alter the number and sensitivity of postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.
  • Neuromuscular blocking agents: small studies show inconsistent results; there is currently no clear evidence that patients who smoke require altered dosing.
Perioperative complications

Intraoperative

In a study of >26,000 patients (26% smokers), there was an increased incidence of all specific respiratory adverse events in smokers: reintubation after planned extubation, laryngospasm, bronchospasm, aspiration, hypoventilation and hypoxaemia, and pulmonary oedema.

RR 1.8Any respiratory complication — all smokers
RR 2.3Younger patients who smoke (16–39 yr)
RR 6.3Young, obese patients who smoke
RR 25.7Perioperative bronchospasm — younger smokers with chronic bronchitis

Postoperative

  • Meta-analyses show increased postoperative mortality and increased rates of all cardiac, pulmonary and septic complications.
  • Clear dose–response relationship between amount smoked and morbidity.
  • Current smokers are 1.38× more likely to die within 30 days (95% CI 1.11–1.72).
  • Findings consistent across cardiac, vascular, thoracic, general, urology, orthopaedic and plastic reconstructive surgery.
  • In general surgery, smoking is an independent risk factor for anastomotic breakdown.
  • Also associated with increased ICU admission, emergency hospital readmission and longer inpatient postoperative stay.

Smoking cessation — timing, benefits & interventions

Perioperative timing

  • Stopping before surgery reduces postoperative complications.
  • 1980s cardiac surgery studies suggested quitting within 8 weeks increased pulmonary complications; a more recent meta-analysis found no increase in complications in those quitting within 2 months of surgery.
  • Trials of at least 4 weeks cessation showed a significantly larger treatment effect on perioperative morbidity and mortality than shorter trials — the longer the period of cessation, the greater the benefit.
  • Even brief abstinence helps: carbon monoxide half-life is 4 h (breathing air) and nicotine half-life 30 min, so short abstinence avoids some acute cardiovascular effects.
  • It is not clear whether cessation within hours or days reduces complications, but there is no clear evidence of harm — cessation should be encouraged at any time.

Effects of cessation

  • Cough and wheeze decrease within weeks; mucociliary clearance starts to improve after a week, but lung inflammation takes much longer to subside.
  • Goblet cell hyperplasia regresses and alveolar macrophages decrease; however alveolar destruction, smooth muscle hyperplasia and fibrosis may be permanent.
  • All-cause mortality in patients with coronary artery disease falls by approximately one third; this takes several months to accrue.
  • Risk of coronary heart disease and cerebrovascular disease approaches that of never-smokers within 10–15 years.
  • Rate of decline of FEV1 slows — the younger the patient at quitting, the slower the decline, eventually approaching never-smokers.
  • Largest survival benefit in those quitting under 30 yr; even quitting at 60 yr may give up to 3 years of survival benefit.

Very Brief Advice (VBA) — Ask, Advise, Act

  • ASK and record smoking history. Typically reported in pack-years = packs per day × years smoked (1 pack-year = 20 cigarettes/day for 1 yr).
  • ADVISE that the most effective way to quit is a combination of medication and specialist support.
  • ACT on the patient's response: give information, refer and prescribe.

NICE guidance: when patients who smoke access secondary care services they should be identified and offered intensive support to quit. Combined behavioural support + pharmacotherapy is more effective than either alone. Refer to the local NHS Stop Smoking Service.

Brief advice from a physician increases quit rates. Preoperative intensive cessation interventions produced a 10-fold relative risk of quitting compared with usual care — surgery is a powerful “teachable moment”.

Bupropion (oral) Nicotinic receptor antagonist with dopaminergic and adrenergic actions May work by blocking nicotine effects, relieving withdrawal, or reducing depressed mood
Varenicline (oral) Nicotinic receptor partial agonist; binds less effectively than nicotine Proven efficacy

Electronic cigarettes

  • Used by 2.3 million adults in the UK; a battery heats a solvent and disperses an aerosol containing nicotine, water and sometimes flavouring — nicotine delivered without combustion.
  • Current opinion: e-cigarettes are likely less harmful than cigarettes.
  • However, the aerosol has been found to contain heavy metals, polycyclic hydrocarbons, nitrosamines, volatile organic compounds and inorganic compounds.
  • E-cigarettes help some people to quit, but no RCTs have compared their effectiveness with established pharmacotherapy for promoting and maintaining cessation.
