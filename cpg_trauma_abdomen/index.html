Key epidemiological facts

  • WHO 2024: 4.4 million injury-related deaths worldwide each year; ~1/3 from road traffic accidents.
  • Malaysia: 402,626 road accidents Jan–Sep 2022 (↑58% from 2021); 4,379 deaths (↑~32%).
  • Malaysia Road Fatalities Index: average 18 lives lost per day.
  • Most common intra-abdominal injuries: liver and spleen.
Anaesthetist's perspective: This guideline repeatedly emphasises the golden hour, haemorrhage control before imaging in unstable patients, and balanced resuscitation. Your intraoperative decisions (induction agent, fluid strategy, lung isolation) directly influence the two primary outcomes: time to haemostasis and prevention of the lethal triad.
Key Recommendation 1 — Pre-hospital care
  • Attending team should have adequate training in providing basic trauma care.
  • Vital signs should be monitored regularly.
  • Fluid resuscitation should be done judiciously.
  • Tranexamic acid should be administered in suspected bleeding patients without known contraindications.
  • All severely injured trauma patients should be transferred to the nearest appropriate hospital within 60 minutes from time of injury (trauma bypass system).

Pre-hospital blood transfusion

  • Multicentre RCT (RePHILL): no significant differences between pre-hospital blood products (PRBC–LyoPlas) and 0.9% sodium chloride in mortality, tissue perfusion improvement, or transfusion-related complications in first 24 hours after ED arrival.
  • Decision must consider healthcare system readiness — hospital transfusion capacity, EMS transport and administration capability.
  • Best practice: well-planned and resourced, multiple safeguards, adequate training and credentialing, responsible stewardship to minimise waste.

Tranexamic acid (TXA) — pre-hospital

Indication: administer within 3 hours from time of injury in patients with:
  • Suspected haemorrhage
  • SBP < 90 mmHg
  • GCS ≤ 12
Dosage: Loading dose 1 g over 10 minutes, followed by maintenance 1 g infusion over 8 hours.

Evidence: CRASH-2 — TXA reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.97) and risk of death due to bleeding (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.76–0.96). Cohort data in traumatic haemorrhagic shock: reduced 28-day mortality (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.21–0.80), less total blood product transfused (median difference 2 units), shorter hospital LOS (4 days) and ICU LOS (1 day). No significant difference in adverse events.

Transfer — golden hour & trauma bypass

ConceptKey dataCase example
Golden hour Definitive resuscitative care should be initiated within first 60 minutes from time of injury. HI 32-year-old, pedestrian vs car. Scene time 15 min, transport 25 min, ED arrival 40 min. OT within 90 min.
Asian cohort No association between overall pre-hospital time and 30-day mortality, but 6% increase in odds of poor functional outcome per 10-minute delay (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.04–1.08). MED 20-minute delay for extrication → higher risk of poor functional outcome, not necessarily death.
Direct transfer Major trauma centre vs trauma unit: shorter time to surgery (4.37 vs 5.37 h), shorter time to CT (2 vs 3.15 h), lower odds of death (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.72–0.82). HI Bypass district hospital with no surgeon; go direct to specialist centre 45 min away.
Anaesthetic relevance: Many patients arrive with a pelvic binder in place, a supraglottic airway or endotracheal tube already inserted, and having received up to 1 L of crystalloid and TXA. Document what was given and when — this changes your induction and transfusion planning.

Primary survey (ATLS)

  • Cervical spine protection with rigid collar; pelvic binder if pelvic fracture suspected.
  • Adjuncts: vital sign monitoring, continuous ECG, ABG including lactate, chest and pelvic X-rays, FAST/e-FAST, DPL.
  • Gastric and urinary catheters assist diagnosis and monitoring.
  • Specialist-led multidisciplinary team (Trauma Team Activation — Emergency, Surgical, Anaesthesiology) should be initiated early on arrival.

Shock Index (SI) & Delta Shock Index (DSI)

Shock Index (SI) = Pulse rate ÷ Systolic blood pressure
< 0.6 = no shock · 0.6–<1.0 = mild · 1.0–<1.4 = moderate · ≥ 1.4 = severe

Delta Shock Index (DSI) = ED SI − Pre-hospital SI
Assume occult shock if DSI > 0.1
StudyFindingCase example
PATOS (DSI > 0.1 vs ≤ 0.1) Higher in-hospital mortality (OR 2.82, 95% CI 2.08–3.84); higher ICU admission (OR 2.02, 95% CI 1.76–2.32); higher massive transfusion (OR 5.24, 95% CI 3.10–8.85). HI Pre-hospital SI 0.7, ED SI 0.95 → DSI 0.25. Occult shock. Prepare blood products early.
Japan Trauma Data Bank DSI > 0.1 → higher incidence of emergency surgery (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.08–1.38). MED DSI 0.2 in a patient with abdominal pain — higher likelihood of needing laparotomy.
Key Recommendation 2 — Abdominal trauma patients with Shock Index ≥ 1.0 should be given priority and monitored closely.

Fluid resuscitation

Key Recommendation 4
  • Initial bolus fluid resuscitation (including pre-hospital phase) in abdominal trauma should not exceed 1 L (15 ml/kg) except when necessary to maintain a systolic blood pressure of 80–90 mmHg.
  • Balanced crystalloid is the preferred fluid in abdominal trauma resuscitation.
  • Hypotonic solution should be avoided in all abdominal trauma resuscitation, especially with concurrent traumatic brain injury.
  • Transfusion of packed red blood cells and plasma (FFP) should be considered early in haemorrhagic trauma patients.
StudyFindingCase example
Level I trauma centre, moderate–severe trauma High-volume (≥ 2 L) crystalloid in ED → increased overall mortality vs low-volume (< 2 L), OR 1.88 (95% CI 1.18–3.01). Subgroup: elderly OR 2.56 (95% CI 1.01–6.45); blunt injury OR 2.11 (95% CI 1.28–3.48). HI 72-year-old given 3 L crystalloid in ED for SBP 85 — higher mortality risk. Switch to blood products early.
Severely injured blunt trauma with haemorrhagic shock High-volume (> 15 L) crystalloid in first 24 h → acute lung injury/ARDS OR 3.4 (95% CI 1.5–7.9); multiple-organ failure OR 2.9 (95% CI 1.3–6.1); abdominal compartment syndrome OR 8.7 (95% CI 2.6–28.9). HI Massive crystalloid resuscitation → abdominal compartment syndrome. Monitor IAP and bladder pressure.
Critically ill trauma patients Non-survivors had higher risk of positive fluid balance (OR 1.236, 95% CI 1.008–1.515) and volume of fluid infusion (OR 4.10, 95% CI 1.030–16.317). MED Aim for zero or negative fluid balance after initial resuscitation.

Clinical diagnosis

Key Recommendation 5
  • Assessment should include clinical history, mechanism of injury and physical examination.
  • Trauma patients with 'seatbelt sign' should raise suspicion of intra-abdominal injury and further evaluation is warranted.

Computed tomography (CT)

Key Recommendation 8 — All abdominal trauma patients undergoing CECT should have at least arterial and portovenous phases, obtained by either multiphasic or split-bolus protocol.

Key Recommendation 10 — CECT should be performed in haemodynamically stable abdominal trauma patients with suspected intra-abdominal injury to guide treatment.

Laparotomy vs laparoscopy

Key Recommendation 12
  • Laparotomy is the preferred option in stable abdominal trauma patients requiring surgical intervention.
  • Laparoscopic surgery may be offered in abdominal trauma patients who are haemodynamically stable and without traumatic brain injury.
  • Laparoscopic surgery should be performed by surgeons experienced and competent in trauma laparoscopy.
ComparisonFindingCase example
Therapeutic laparoscopy vs laparotomy No significant difference in median operating time (105 vs 98 min). Laparoscopy: lower post-op complications (1 vs 10), opiate requirements (34 vs 136 mg MEQ), hospital stay (4 vs 9 days). MED Stable patient with anterior stab wound — laparoscopy reduces pain and LOS.
Diagnostic laparoscopy vs non-therapeutic laparotomy Lower mortality (OR 0.10, 95% CI 0.04–0.29), lower major complications (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.29–0.50), shorter LOS (p < 0.001). MED Negative CT but persistent pain — diagnostic laparoscopy avoids unnecessary laparotomy.
Meta-analysis (BAT) Less blood loss (SMD 0.28, 95% CI 0.51–0.05), shorter hospital stay (SMD 0.67, 95% CI 0.90–0.43). No difference in mortality or operation time. MED Blunt trauma with equivocal findings — laparoscopy reduces morbidity.
Hollow viscus / mesenteric injury Laparoscopy: shorter LOS (11.0 vs 17.6 days, p < 0.001), fewer wound infections (5.1% vs 16.1%, p = 0.049). MED Suspected bowel injury — laparoscopic repair reduces wound infection.

Non-operative management (NOM)

Key Recommendation 13
  • NOM in abdominal trauma should only be performed in haemodynamically stable patients.
  • It should be undertaken in centres with 24-hour availability of surgical services, operating theatres, intensive care units and other supporting resources.
  • Patients treated non-operatively should be closely monitored for clinical deterioration (haemodynamic instability and peritonitis) which warrants urgent intervention.
Prerequisites: 24-hour access to surgeon, OT, ICU, CT, continuous monitoring. Care led by a surgeon. Haemodynamically stable. No peritonitis. Full extent/severity determined by CECT. In polytrauma, consider extra-abdominal injuries.

Monitoring: Vital signs (BP, PR, RR, SpO₂, temperature, pain score) and GCS · fluid balance and urine output · clinical parameters (peritonitis) · blood results (ABG, Hb, coagulation, renal, LFT).

Termination of NOM: Haemodynamic deterioration · onset of peritonitis · increased blood transfusion requirement · radiological evidence of complications on reimaging.

These cases integrate the CPG recommendations into realistic anaesthetic scenarios. Each includes vitals, key findings and the teaching point.

1 The "normal vitals" trap — occult shock in a young patient

24-year-oldMVCHR 98SBP 118SI 0.83GCS 15

Scenario: Motorcyclist hit by car. Initial vitals look reassuring. Pre-hospital SI was 0.55; ED SI is 0.83 → DSI 0.28 (occult shock). Abdomen soft but seatbelt sign present. e-FAST shows free fluid in RUQ.

Action: Do not be reassured by normal HR/BP. DSI > 0.1 indicates occult hypoperfusion. Proceed to CECT (stable) or OT if unstable. Prepare blood products early.

Teaching point: Shock Index and Delta Shock Index detect occult shock before conventional vital signs deteriorate. PATOS: DSI > 0.1 → mortality OR 2.82, massive transfusion OR 5.24.

2 The unstable patient — do not scan, go to theatre

45-year-oldBlunt abdominal traumaHR 132SBP 78SI 1.69e-FAST positive

Scenario: Fall from height. SBP 78 despite 1 L crystalloid. e-FAST shows free fluid in Morison's pouch. Surgeon asks for CT to "define anatomy."

Action: Refuse CT. This is a Class III recommendation — do not scan persistently unstable patients. Proceed to emergency laparotomy within 90 minutes of ED arrival. Activate MTP.

Teaching point: CT before laparotomy in unstable patients increases mortality (OR 1.71). Every 10-minute delay to laparotomy increases 24-hour mortality (HR 1.50). The anaesthetist is a key advocate for the OT.

3 RSI in the haemodynamically unstable trauma patient

35-year-oldStab wound abdomenHR 128SBP 82GCS 14SI 1.56

Scenario: Penetrating abdominal trauma with omental evisceration. Needs emergency intubation for laparotomy. You have propofol, ketamine, etomidate, rocuronium and suxamethonium available.

Action: Use video laryngoscopy (Class I recommendation). Choose an induction agent that maintains haemodynamics — ketamine (reduced dose) or etomidate. Avoid propofol in this unstable patient. Have vasopressors drawn up. Use rocuronium or suxamethonium for rapid sequence.

Teaching point: Ketamine and etomidate are associated with higher vasopressor need at 24 h vs propofol. Ketamine in high SI (> 0.9) causes hypotension in 26% within 9 min. Have phenylephrine/noradrenaline immediately available.

4 The TBI + abdominal trauma dilemma

52-year-oldMVCGCS 8SBP 85HR 118Right pupil dilated

Scenario: Both head and abdominal injuries. FAST positive. Neurosurgery wants to take the patient for craniotomy; general surgery wants laparotomy. SBP is 85.

Action: Surgical haemostasis first (Recommendation 18). Hypotension worsens secondary brain injury. Laparotomy to control abdominal haemorrhage and restore BP, then address the head injury. Do not use laparoscopy — pneumoperitoneum increases ICP.

Teaching point: In haemodynamically unstable patients with head injury, abdominal haemostasis takes priority. Patients who have simultaneous head and torso operations have extremely low survival rates. Target MAP ≥ 80 mmHg if TBI suspected.

5 Damage control surgery — the lethal triad

38-year-oldCrush injuryTemp 34.2°CpH 7.12Lactate 9.4INR 2.1

Scenario: Massive abdominal trauma with liver and splenic injuries. Already received 10 units PRBC. Hypothermic, acidotic, coagulopathic. Surgeon asks whether to do definitive repair or DCS.

Action: Damage control surgery. Indications: acidosis, hypothermia (< 34°C), shock, > 10 units PRBC, ISS > 25. Abbreviated surgery: pack liver, splenectomy if needed, temporary abdominal closure. ICU resuscitation to correct physiology. Return to OT in 24–48 h for definitive repair.

Teaching point: Your role: prevent further heat loss, warm all fluids, correct coagulopathy with blood products and TXA, communicate physiological endpoints with the surgeon. Recognise when to stop.

6 The stable splenic injury — NOM with a plan

19-year-oldSports injuryHR 88SBP 122Grade III splenic lacerationNo blush

Scenario: Stable patient with isolated grade III splenic laceration on CT. No active extravasation, no peritonitis. Surgeon plans non-operative management.

Action: NOM is appropriate — haemodynamically stable, no peritonitis, 24-hour surgical/ICU/OT availability. Monitor closely (vitals, GCS, pain score, fluid balance, bloods). Start VTE prophylaxis within 48 hours if no active bleeding (Recommendation 17). Watch for NOM failure: haemodynamic deterioration, peritonitis, increasing transfusion requirement.

Teaching point: NOM success rate 80–95%. Risk factors for failure: age > 40, ISS > 25, grade > 3, contrast blush, > 1 unit transfusion. Know when to escalate — the anaesthetist may be called for emergency laparotomy if NOM fails.

7 Penetrating abdominal trauma — stab vs gunshot

28-year-oldStab wound RUQHR 92SBP 124Localised painNo peritonitis

Scenario: Anterior abdominal stab wound. Haemodynamically stable. No evisceration, no peritonitis. Wound is small and pain is localised.

Action: Determine peritoneal violation via local wound exploration, CT or diagnostic laparoscopy (Recommendation 14). If fascia breached → laparoscopy or laparotomy. If no breach → wound debridement. NOM may be considered in stab wounds, but not gunshot wounds.

Teaching point: For gunshot wounds with transperitoneal trajectory, laparotomy is required even if stable. For stab wounds, selective NOM with serial clinical examination (every 6 hours) is acceptable in the right patient.

8 Pelvic fracture + abdominal trauma — the double bleed

42-year-oldMVCHR 138SBP 76Pelvic binder appliedFAST positive

Scenario: Unstable pelvic ring fracture with positive FAST. Patient remains hypotensive despite pelvic binder and 1 L crystalloid. Pelvic X-ray shows Type C fracture.

Action: Haemodynamic instability + unstable pelvic ring → pelvic stabilisation and haemorrhage control (Recommendation 19). Options: external fixation with pre-peritoneal packing, or pelvic binder with angiography. Positive FAST → exploratory laparotomy for intra-abdominal bleeding. Coordinate with orthopaedics and interventional radiology.

Teaching point: Mortality ~50% if open pelvic fracture or major vessel injury. FAST has high sensitivity (85.4%) and specificity (98.7%) for intra-abdominal free fluid in pelvic fractures. Resuscitate with blood products, not crystalloid.

9 The geriatric trauma patient — frailty and anticoagulants

81-year-oldFall from standingHR 92SBP 108Age SI 69On apixabanCFS 6

Scenario: Elderly patient on DOAC for AF. Low-energy fall. Vitals look "normal" for age but HR 92 and SBP 108 meet geriatric triage thresholds (HR > 90, SBP < 110). CFS 6 (moderate frailty).

Action: Do not under-triage. Age SI = 81 × 0.85 = 69 (cut-off 49 → high mortality risk). Proceed to CT — low threshold in geriatric patients. Check coagulation (PT/INR, aPTT). Consider reversal agents if bleeding. Involve geriatric services if available. Discuss goals of care with frailty in mind.

Teaching point: Geriatric patients can have major trauma from low-energy mechanisms. Frailty (CFS 5–6: OR 5.68; CFS 7–9: OR 10.38 for 30-day mortality) and anticoagulation are key risk modifiers. DOACs have lower mortality than warfarin but still require attention.

10 Abdominal compartment syndrome — the silent killer

33-year-oldPost-laparotomyIAP 24 mmHgUrine output 10 ml/hPeak airway pressure 38 cmH₂OLactate 5.8

Scenario: Patient had damage control laparotomy with temporary abdominal closure 24 hours ago. Now in ICU: IAP 24 mmHg (Grade III), rising creatinine, hypoxia, high airway pressures, lactic acidosis.

Action: Abdominal compartment syndrome. Stop enteral nutrition. Deepen sedation, consider neuromuscular blockade. Aggressive negative fluid balance (diuresis). Gastric decompression, prokinetics. If organ dysfunction persists → decompressive laparotomy / laparostomy. Monitor IAP every 4–6 hours.

Teaching point: IAH/ACS affects respiratory mechanics (reduced compliance), cardiovascular function (reduced preload) and renal perfusion. Your ventilation strategy should avoid high airway pressures. Fluid management should target zero or negative balance once resuscitation is complete. Overzealous fluid resuscitation is the commonest cause.
