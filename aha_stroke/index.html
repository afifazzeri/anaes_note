1 · Core clinical orientation

Definition, Epidemiology & Anaesthetic Imperatives Start here
Definition Perioperative stroke is a cerebrovascular event occurring during surgery or within 30 days postoperatively. Events are predominantly ischemic rather than hemorrhagic and carry substantial morbidity, loss of functional independence, prolonged hospitalization, and markedly higher perioperative mortality.
  • Rising clinical burden: Driven by an aging surgical population with multimorbidity increasingly undergoing complex cardiac, vascular, and neurosurgical interventions.
  • Covert (silent) strokes: Clinically silent infarcts detected on postoperative diffusion-weighted MRI occur in up to 10% of noncardiac, non-neurological surgeries, 5%–10% after CEA, 30%–50% after CAS, and 15%–45% after neurosurgical procedures.
  • Early vigilance is decisive: Roughly 50% of perioperative strokes manifest within the first 24 hours postoperatively, when residual anaesthesia, prolonged intubation sedation, analgesia, and stroke-induced consciousness changes obscure deficits.
  • Immediate systems trigger: Suspected stroke requires instant activation of the institutional stroke team and urgent neurology assessment—mechanical thrombectomy is preferred for large vessel occlusion (LVO) due to superior efficacy and lower bleeding risk than systemic thrombolysis.

3 · Pathophysiology & timing mechanisms

Embolic, Hypoperfusion, Thrombotic & Hemorrhagic Pathways (Table 3) Scrollable Table 3

1. Embolic Mechanisms (Predominant)

  • Direct dislodgement: Intraoperative manipulation of the heart, aorta, or great vessels frees atheromatous plaque or thrombi.
  • Atrial fibrillation: New-onset postoperative AF or chronic AF with held anticoagulation promotes left atrial thrombus formation.
  • Paradoxical embolism: Perioperative DVT combined with sudden rises in intrathoracic pressure across a patent foramen ovale.

2. Hypoperfusion & Prothrombotic State

  • Watershed / global ischemia: Intraoperative hypotension, large-artery clamping, or excessive anaesthetic depth critically reduce CBF in patients with arterial stenosis or poor collaterals.
  • Hypercoagulability: Surgical tissue trauma and CPB trigger systemic inflammation and transient prothrombotic activation, compounded by interrupted antithrombotics.
  • Hemorrhagic stroke (rare): Linked to surgical manipulation, anticoagulation, postoperative hypertension, or preexisting intracranial lesions.

4 · Preoperative assessment & optimization

Surgical Timing, Carotid / Aortic Evaluation & Antithrombotic Management Pre-op Checklist

Surgical Timing After Cerebrovascular Events

Elective Deferral Rule Ideally delay elective noncardiac surgery for at least 6 months — and optimally 9 months — after an ischemic stroke or TIA, as recurrence risk declines substantially over this interval.
Exceptions: Urgent carotid revascularization, acute coronary interventions, and surgery for infective endocarditis. If stroke risk is extreme and surgical benefit modest, reconsider elective surgery.

Carotid & Aortic Disease Workup

  • Symptomatic carotid stenosis (>70%): Consider carotid revascularization prior to other major elective surgeries.
  • Asymptomatic carotid stenosis: Routine prophylactic carotid revascularization is generally discouraged unless specific scenarios exist (e.g., contralateral occlusion). Optimal medical vs procedural management awaits CREST-2 trial outcomes.
  • Focused screening only: Preoperative neurology evaluation and carotid ultrasound/MRA are justified in high-risk patients (prior stroke or carotid bruit). Routine carotid screening in low- or average-risk asymptomatic patients is not recommended.
  • Aortic atherosclerosis / aneurysms: Use preoperative CTA, MRA, or TEE to identify high-risk aortic plaques and guide intraoperative clamp/cannula placement.

5 · Intraoperative management

Hemodynamics, Cerebral Perfusion Targets, Surgical Techniques & Neuromonitoring Theatre
Primary Intraoperative Objective Maintain stable cerebral perfusion and minimize embolic burden—especially during anaesthetic induction, major arterial clamping, and aortic/cardiac manipulation.

Hemodynamic Monitoring & Blood Pressure Targets

  • Avoid profound and prolonged hypotension: Hypotension contributes directly to watershed and global cerebral ischemia.
  • Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) target: Current guidelines recommend prompt correction of hypotension to maintain MAP 60–65 mmHg during cardiac bypass.
  • Individualize for cerebrovascular disease: Target higher MAP thresholds in patients with known carotid/intracranial stenosis, impaired autoregulation, or poor collateral flow.
  • Evidence nuance: Randomized trials targeting uniformly higher intraoperative BP with IV vasopressors (e.g., POISE-3 hypotension- vs hypertension-avoidance; CPB high vs low BP targets) showed neutral results or harm—focus on preventing significant drops rather than aggressive routine hypertension.

Surgical Techniques & Positioning

  • Aortic no-touch technique: Off-pump CABG combined with strict aortic no-touch consistently lowers stroke incidence by avoiding atheroma dislodgement.
  • Imaging-guided cannulation/clamping: Use CTA or intraoperative TEE / epiaortic ultrasound to choose plaque-free zones for aortic clamps and cannulas.
  • Embolic protection & flow reversal: Embolic protection devices and dynamic flow reversal (used in TCAR via proximal carotid clamping) divert embolic debris away from the cerebral circulation.
  • Positioning hazard — Beach chair position: Frequently used in shoulder surgery; significantly lowers cerebral perfusion pressure and risks watershed stroke in patients with impaired autoregulation or cerebrovascular stenosis. Consider alternative positioning or cerebral monitoring in high-risk patients.

Intraoperative Neurophysiological Monitoring

Valuable during high-risk procedures (ascending aorta, aortic arch, thoracoabdominal aortic repairs, carotid/neurosurgery) to detect cerebral or spinal cord ischemia and microemboli early enough for immediate intervention:

6 · Postoperative surveillance, AF & antithrombotic resumption

Neurological Checks, Postoperative AF & Restarting Antithrombotics ICU / Ward

Structured Neurological Surveillance

  • Critical first 24 hours: Approximately 50% of all perioperative strokes occur within 24 hours of surgery. Residual anaesthetics, prolonged intubation sedation, and opioids mask focal deficits.
  • Minimum standard: Basic neurological assessment at every routine nursing shift and physician round.
  • High-risk protocol: Perform focused neurological exams every 2–4 hours during the first 24 hours in elderly patients, those with prior stroke/TIA, high-risk surgery, or multiple vascular comorbidities, tapering as the high-risk window passes.
  • Standardized bedside tools: Train surgical and ICU staff in FAST (Face, Arm, Speech, Time) and modified NIHSS adapted for rapid bedside screening. Suspect stroke → immediate stroke team activation and urgent neurology consult.

Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation (POAF) Surveillance

  • Incidence: Occurs in 30%–60% of cardiac surgeries and roughly 3% of major noncardiac surgeries; driven by age, CHF, hypertension, CAD, hypovolemia, hypotension, anemia, surgical trauma, and metabolic disturbances.
  • Telemetry risk stratification: Use validated scores—CHA2DS2-VASc, HATCH, POAF, and COM-AF—to select patients for continuous telemetry or prolonged rhythm monitoring.
  • Surgical prophylaxis & anticoagulation: Left-sided posterior pericardiotomy may reduce POAF in cardiac surgery. Patients with newly diagnosed POAF lasting >48 hours may benefit from anticoagulation for stroke prevention.

Preferred Intervention

Mechanical Thrombectomy

Superior efficacy & lower bleeding risk vs systemic or intra-arterial thrombolysis. Recanalization rates >80% with improved functional outcomes. Eligible up to 24 hours post-onset (and selected large ischemic cores).

Endovascular Alternative

Intra-Arterial Thrombolysis (Select Cases)

If systemic thrombolysis carries excessive bleeding risk and mechanical retrieval is incomplete, cautious intra-arterial alteplase may be considered via specialized neurointerventional teams.

No LVO · or MT Not Feasible
Intracranial / Intraspinal Surgery <3 Months

IV Thrombolysis Strictly Contraindicated

Absolute contraindication within 3 months of intracranial or intraspinal surgery. Start early antiplatelet therapy within 24 hours (barring contraindications).

Major Surgery Within 14 Days

Generally Contraindicated · Multidisciplinary Review at 10–14 d

IV thrombolysis is generally contraindicated <14 days, but may be carefully weighed especially after 10 days via multidisciplinary review (surgical-site hemorrhage ~7%, serious ~3%, rarely fatal). Otherwise initiate antiplatelet therapy within 24 hours.

>14 Days Post-Op or Minor Surgery

Consider IV Thrombolysis or Early Antiplatelets

If within the standard IV thrombolysis window and surgical hemostasis is secure, administer IV thrombolysis with multidisciplinary sign-off. If ineligible, start antiplatelet therapy within 24 hours.

Post-Acute Comprehensive Care

Secondary Prevention, Mobilization & Multimodal Rehabilitation

Maintain cerebral perfusion, initiate early mobilization and individualized rehabilitation, and optimize secondary stroke prevention. Investigational neuroprotective therapies targeting excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation remain under clinical study.
