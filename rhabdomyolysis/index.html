Rhabdomyolysis · Quick Reference

For anaesthetists and intensivists — recognition, investigation and management

Searchable · Collapsible
1Key points
  • Rhabdomyolysis is characterised by breakdown of skeletal muscle with release of myoglobin and other intracellular proteins and electrolytes into the circulation.
  • Creatine kinase (CK) concentration is the most useful indicator of muscle damage.
  • Hyperkalaemia can be life-threatening and needs prompt management.
  • Acute kidney injury (AKI) is common.
  • Fluid resuscitation is the mainstay of management.
2Definition & overview

Rhabdomyolysis is characterised by skeletal muscle disintegration and the release of myoglobin and other intracellular proteins and electrolytes into the circulation. Typically CK levels are markedly elevated, and muscle pain and myoglobinuria may occur.

The condition ranges from asymptomatic to life-threatening, with associated hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury (AKI).

Historical note

Bywaters and Beall first associated AKI with crush injury during the London Blitz. They described four air-raid casualties with oliguria and pigmented casts after crush injury — all four developed AKI and died within about a week.

3Causes

Rhabdomyolysis can be traumatic or non-traumatic.

Traumatic
  • Crush injury and trauma — earthquakes, collapsed buildings, road traffic collisions
  • Compartment syndrome — alcohol-associated immobility, prolonged collapse, perioperative positioning, prolonged tourniquet use
  • Electrocution
Non-traumatic
  • Exertional — strenuous exercise, seizures
  • Body temperature changes — malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, hypothermia
  • Genetic defects — from disorders of glycolysis or gluconeogenesis to mitochondrial disorders
  • Drugs or toxins — statins, alcohol, heroin, cocaine
  • Infections — influenza A and B, EBV, HIV, Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus
  • Metabolic and electrolyte disorders — non-ketotic hyperosmotic state, diabetic ketoacidosis
  • Idiopathic

One review of 26 ICU patients found the cause was ischaemic by vascular obstruction (50%), crush injury by trauma (23%), sepsis (11.5%), heat stroke/hyperthermia (11.5%), and hyponatraemia in a single patient.

Anaesthetic & critical care relevance

  • Alcohol is commonly associated. Coma — from alcohol or other causes — leads to prolonged muscle compression and ischaemia. Alcohol also damages the sarcolemma directly and can cause myopathy, leading to non-traumatic rhabdomyolysis. Chronic abuse renders muscle vulnerable to further damage.
  • Recreational drugs: 24% of patients presenting with cocaine-related disorders in one emergency department developed rhabdomyolysis. In another study, 60% of ED patients with rhabdomyolysis tested positive for cocaine or amphetamine, and 45% were clinically intoxicated.
  • Statins: widespread use; can cause rhabdomyolysis secondary to myopathy. Cerivastatin was withdrawn from the USA in 2001 (rhabdomyolysis rate 16–80× higher than other statins). Fatal rhabdomyolysis with statins is rare — fewer than one death per million prescriptions.
  • Anaesthesia obscures the pain and paraesthesia of prolonged muscle compression that would disturb a conscious patient. Risk factors: prolonged immobility, tourniquets, non-invasive arterial pressure cuffs, poor perioperative positioning. Compression of discrete muscle groups may occur in the lower limbs in the lithotomy position, or at pressure points during pronation. Take particular care to prevent localised pressure effects in these at-risk patients.
  • Disasters: incidence rises after wars or natural disasters. The term “renal disaster” describes a disaster where a substantial number of victims survive with major traumatic lesions, resulting in crush syndrome and renal failure.
4Pathophysiology

Rhabdomyolysis involves either direct sarcolemmic injury (usually trauma) or depletion of ATP within the myocyte.

  1. ATP stores maintain the ionic gradients across the cell membrane, including the calcium ATPase pumps.
  2. ATP depletion impairs pump function → increased sarcoplasmic calcium.
  3. Raised intracellular calcium → persistent contraction of the myofibril.
  4. Sustained contraction → further energy depletion, activation of calcium-dependent neutral proteases and phospholipases → eventual disintegration of the myocyte.
5Metabolic derangements

After myocyte injury, intracellular contents are released into the circulation. Hyperkalaemia, hyperuricaemia and hyperphosphataemia can all develop rapidly.

  • Phosphate binds calcium → calcium-phosphate deposition in soft tissue.
  • Calcium is lost from serum by entering damaged muscle cells → hypocalcaemia.
  • Ischaemic muscle uses anaerobic metabolism (pyruvate → lactate) → metabolic acidosis.
  • Hyperuricaemia results from metabolism of purines from disintegrated cell nuclei.
High-risk combination

The combination of hyperkalaemia + hypocalcaemia + acidosis is particularly dangerous.

Recovery phase

About one-third of patients develop hypercalcaemia during recovery. This may occur due to release of vitamin D stores from injured muscle, providing substrate for excess 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 production. Hypercalcaemia may be exacerbated if excess calcium was administered during the acute hypocalcaemic phase.

6Myoglobin & acute kidney injury

Myoglobin handling

Myoglobin is a dark red, haem-containing protein that stores and transports oxygen in muscle. Only small levels are normally present in plasma — this small amount is bound to plasma proteins and very little crosses the glomerulus. Myoglobin has a small molecular weight and is easily filtered. When this transport mechanism is overwhelmed, the renal threshold for free myoglobin is exceeded and myoglobin appears in the urine.

Incidence

During intensive care, the reported incidence of AKI after rhabdomyolysis may be as high as 65%.

Mechanisms

  • Tubular obstruction — within the renal tubules, myoglobin interacts with the protein Tamm–Horsfall to form brown granular casts. This process is favoured when the urine is acidic; myoglobin may have no nephrotoxic effect when the urine is alkaline. Obstruction may be compounded by urate deposition from hyperuricaemia.
  • Direct and ischaemic tubular injury — the haem group from myoglobin has been implicated in lipid peroxidation, with accompanying cytotoxic effects.
  • Intrarenal vasoconstriction — scavenging of the vasodilator nitric oxide by myoglobin causes a deficit, compounding kidney injury. Renal blood flow is further reduced by hypovolaemia, activation of the renin–angiotensin system and additional vascular mediators.
RIFLE criteria study

In a 44-month retrospective study of 135 consecutive patients with rhabdomyolysis, RIFLE criteria stratified AKI severity. At admission: 44% no AKI, 15% risk, 24% injury, 17% failure. These categories were associated with increasing magnitude of volume depletion, potassium, phosphate, urea and anion gap, and predicted differences in length of stay, dialysis, discharge creatinine, and rate of normalisation of admission creatinine.

7Clinical manifestation

A high level of suspicion is required in patients suffering trauma, crush injury, or prolonged immobilisation.

  • Specific muscle group affected: history of weakness, myalgia and swelling in the affected limbs. Severe pain may limit passive limb movement and there may be reduced range of active movement.
  • Compartment syndrome or crush injury: muscles may be tense and swollen, leading to nerve injury with sensory loss.
  • Diffuse muscle injury (e.g. drugs): general malaise with diffuse myalgia and non-specific deterioration.
  • Urine colour: rarely, patients volunteer that their urine has changed to a red or brown colour.
  • Drugs: commonly offending drugs — statins, alcohol, heroin, cocaine — should be identified and withdrawn.
Watch for late deterioration

Clinical observations may initially be normal, or normalise after fluid resuscitation, only to deteriorate hours later as the condition progresses. Regular observations, including hourly urine output, are required to detect deterioration promptly.

Creatine kinase (CK)

  • Rhabdomyolysis is typically diagnosed when CK > 5000 units litre−1 — five times the normal upper limit.
  • Myoglobin levels peak before CK rises, but myoglobin is metabolised rapidly at sites outside the kidney, making CK a more reliable marker.
  • CK is predominantly the skeletal muscle (MM) fraction rather than the myocardial (MB) fraction. A rise in CK-MB represents the small amount found in skeletal muscle, not cardiac injury.
  • CK typically peaks 24–72 h after onset. If CK continues to rise beyond this period, consider an undiagnosed compartment syndrome.

Myoglobin

  • Serum and urine myoglobin can be measured directly, but not all laboratories offer this.
  • Detectable in urine when serum concentration exceeds the renal threshold of 0.5–1.5 mg dl−1; directly visible as reddish-brown urine when concentration exceeds 100 mg dl−1.
  • The rise in serum myoglobin precedes CK, and myoglobin is metabolised rapidly — levels may be undetectable by the time urine is tested. The assay for myoglobinuria is also insensitive, reducing detection frequency.
  • Absence of myoglobinuria does not exclude rhabdomyolysis — in a study of 475 patients diagnosed by CK levels, myoglobinuria was detected in only 19%.

Urinary dipstick

  • Positive for blood in the presence of myoglobin, with a sensitivity of 81% for detecting rhabdomyolysis; far more frequent than a positive assay for myoglobinuria.
  • False positives occur if urinary red cells are present (e.g. trauma), impairing reliability.
  • A rapid bedside test that can be used serially to assess resolution of myoglobinuria — a useful endpoint for fluid resuscitation.

Other

  • A metabolic acidosis with a high anion gap is commonly reported in rhabdomyolysis with associated AKI.
  • Arterial blood gas allows monitoring of trends in pH and serum lactate to guide fluid resuscitation. Base excess and potassium can be used to make decisions about renal replacement therapy.
  • Coagulation studies should be taken to exclude disseminated intravascular coagulation, which may occur in the more severe spectrum of rhabdomyolysis.
9Management
Mainstay

Early fluid resuscitation is the most important measure in the prevention of AKI. Large volume depletion occurs due to sequestration of water by injured muscle.

Monitoring

  • Bladder catheterisation is required to monitor urine output accurately.
  • Invasive monitoring — arterial and central venous pressure monitoring — should be considered to guide fluid resuscitation.
  • More advanced flow monitoring should be decided on the individual unit's preference and local availability.
  • Most studies target urine output of 3 mL kg−1 h−1 or >300 mL h−1.

Fluid choice

Normal saline, compound sodium lactate (Hartmann's) and sodium bicarbonate have all been used in the literature as resuscitation fluids, with comparative merits and disadvantages:

  • Compound sodium lactate may aid urinary alkalinisation but should probably be avoided because it contains potassium.
  • Normal saline is devoid of potassium and is more appropriate, but may contribute to hyperchloraemic acidosis.

Volume resuscitation with polyuria should be continued until myoglobinuria is cleared, supported by a negative urinary dipstick for blood.

Urinary alkalinisation — sodium bicarbonate

  • Theoretical advantage: reduced precipitation of Tamm–Horsfall protein complexes in the alkaline urinary environment.
  • Disadvantage: alkalinisation reduces ionised calcium, which may further exacerbate hypocalcaemia induced by phosphate–calcium precipitation.
  • Administration: boluses of 50–100 mmol, or as 1.26% in volume resuscitation. The advantage of 1.26% sodium bicarbonate is avoidance of the hyperchloraemic acidosis associated with large volumes of sodium chloride infusion.
  • Target urinary pH > 6.5.
  • Evidence: lacking — a recent systematic review found no level 1–3 evidence to support its use in preventing AKI in rhabdomyolysis.

Mannitol

  • Theoretical benefits: flushes nephrotoxic agents through the tubules, extracts fluid accumulated in injured muscle, and acts as a free radical scavenger.
  • Caveat: may exacerbate hypovolaemia — therefore reserve for when urine output remains low despite normovolaemia.
  • Monitoring: plasma osmolality and osmolal gap should be monitored. Discontinue if there is no improvement in urine output, or if the osmolal gap increases above 55 mOsm kg−1.
  • Other diuretics are not appropriate in this setting.
  • Evidence: again, no level 1–3 evidence to support mannitol for prevention of AKI.

Hyperkalaemia

Most life-threatening electrolyte abnormality

Of all the electrolyte abnormalities in rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia is the most life-threatening and should be monitored and treated promptly.

  • Hyperkalaemic ECG changes: treat with calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10%.
  • Potassium > 6.5 mmol L−1: give insulin 10 IU in 50 mL of 50% dextrose over 15 min and nebulised salbutamol 10 mg.

Hypocalcaemia

Should not be treated unless the patient becomes symptomatic or in the treatment of hyperkalaemia, as normocalcaemia usually occurs spontaneously.

Renal replacement therapy

Reserved for the management of hyperkalaemia, acidosis, or volume overload.

Crush victims / mass disasters

The European Renal Best Practice and Renal Disaster Relief Task Force of the International Society of Nephrology has produced recommendations for the management of crush victims in mass disasters. As the evidence base is sparse, these are based on retrospective analyses, case reports, position statements and expert judgement — representing a low grade of evidence. They describe basic life-saving interventions in the disaster field: early fluid resuscitation, extrication, and the medical and logistical recommendations that apply to major disasters.

10Compartment syndrome

Where compartment syndrome is suspected, initial management should focus on prompt diagnosis and relief of compartmental pressures.

  • Remove external pressure — dressings, casts, or rubble at trauma scenes.
  • Oxygen should be administered.
  • Analgesia — offered as pain often appears out of proportion to the injury sustained.
  • Avoid hypotension — it further reduces limb perfusion.
  • Urgent fasciotomy is commonly the definitive therapy and should be performed early. Delays in performing fasciotomy increase morbidity, including the need for amputation.
After fasciotomy

Patients should be closely monitored with serial CK levels and hourly urine output to detect impending rhabdomyolysis and AKI. High-risk patients with multiple injuries should be cared for in a critical care environment. Medical and surgical management of patients with established rhabdomyolysis should occur simultaneously.

11Prognosis
  • Intensive care mortality from rhabdomyolysis has been reported as 22% without AKI and 59% with AKI.
  • However, the majority of survivors recover good renal function.
