Part A — Systemic Sclerosis (SSc) ANAESTHESIA

Definition SSc is a rare autoimmune, inflammatory, progressive multisystem connective tissue disease characterised by small-vessel vasculopathy and abnormal collagen deposition → fibrosis of skin and internal organs.
  • Global incidence ≈ 1:10,000. European prevalence 7–33 per 100,000; almost 4× higher in women.
  • Peak onset 3rd–5th decade. Highest mortality of all rheumatic diseases — 10-year survival ≈ 66% (5-year ≈ 86%, 10-year ≈ 72% in newer estimates).
  • Leading causes of death: pulmonary fibrosis/ILD 19%, myocardial disease 14%, PAH 14%.
  • PAH is an independent predictor of 30-day postoperative pneumonia, congestive heart failure and all-cause mortality.
  • Of all major adverse cardiac events (MACE), only myocardial infarction is independently associated with SSc.
  • Postoperative pneumonia accounts for more than one-third of 30-day mortality.
  • Involvement of a rheumatology specialist is recommended to gauge severity, optimise therapy and help manage postoperative complications.
Why it matters to us These patients present for disease-specific elective surgery (soft-tissue calcinosis debulking, sympathectomy, lung transplant), unrelated elective/emergency surgery, out-of-theatre resuscitation and obstetric delivery. Subclinical organ involvement can cause unanticipated difficulty.
  • Risk factors: female sex, silicone breast implants, Native American / African American race, previous chemotherapy (bleomycin, taxanes, gemcitabine), radiotherapy, gadolinium (in renal failure), silica dust, vinyl chloride, organic solvents.
  • Scleroderma renal crisis (SRC) was historically the main cause of death — attenuated by ACE inhibitors.
  • Cardiac involvement and interstitial lung fibrosis emerge in ≈50% within the first 5 years.
  • SSc carries a 3-fold increased risk of myocardial ischaemia; symptomatic cardiac involvement → 75% mortality at 5 years.
  • PAH confers a 3× higher mortality risk than SSc alone. Up to 31% have sleep-disordered breathing.
  • Patients with SSc undergo twice the number of thoracic, breast and vascular operations; transplant surgery is 6× more frequent (1.9% vs 0.3%).

The core triad
1
Endothelial cell injury / apoptosis (unknown trigger in a genetically susceptible host)
2
Immune activation — T & B lymphocytes, monocytes, macrophages → autoantibodies, cell-mediated autoimmunity, cytokine overproduction (ET-1, TGF-β, PDGF, IL-6)
3
Fibroblast → myofibroblast transformation → excess collagen & matrix deposition → fibrosis + profibrotic cytokine loop

Three amplifiers keep the loop running

Tissue hypoxia Mechanical tissue stress Dysregulated cytokine production (TGF-β, ET-1, PDGF)

Clinical consequences of ischaemia–reperfusion small-vessel injury

  • Skin — Raynaud's phenomenon, digital ulceration, gangrene
  • Stomach — gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE) → silent blood loss
  • Kidney — scleroderma renal crisis
  • Pulmonary vasculature — PAH
  • Heart — "myocardial Raynaud's phenomenon": small-vessel disease and coronary vasospasm → MI with no epicardial atherosclerotic change
Modern concept SSc is better understood as dysregulated tissue healing rather than primary fibrosis. IL-6 overexpression (mainly from activated macrophages) is linked to skin progression and worse long-term survival.

Pathognomonic / suggestive signs

  • Scleroderma ("hard skin") and sclerodactyly — the hallmark
  • Calcinosis cutis, widespread telangiectasia
  • Terminal tuft resorption (late)
  • Subjective "puffy hands" in early disease — prevents pressing palms and fingers tightly together
  • Raynaud's phenomenon — consistent finding, may precede other features by years
Features of "pathological Raynaud's" New adult onset · unusual triggers (spicy food, emotion, anxiety) · asymmetrical finger/hand involvement · severe or prolonged episodes · digital scars, pits or dystrophic changes.

ICU & transplant outcomes

  • Lung transplantation in SSc: 48% relative increased risk of death at 1 year vs non-SSc ILD (but similar to non-SSc PAH).
  • ICU admission reasons: respiratory failure 66%, acute renal failure 15%. Baseline imaging showed pulmonary fibrosis in 72%; PAH 32%; ILD 72%. dcSSc phenotype predominates.
  • 39% require renal replacement therapy.
  • 30-day mortality 31%, rising at 6 months and 1 year. Respiratory complications and infection are the main causes of ICU death.
  • Invasive mechanical ventilation in ICU → 85% in-hospital mortality.
  • Chronic pain is very common (93%); 45% daily. Neuropathic pain in 26% (marker of more severe disease).
Golden rules
  • There is no high-level evidence for optimal perioperative management — practice is expert opinion/consensus.
  • Use a multidisciplinary approach: surgeon, rheumatologist, cardiologist, respiratory physician, coordinated by the anaesthesia team.
  • Frequency of specialist review (>twice/year) is itself an indicator of disease severity and internal organ involvement.
  • An organ-system approach may uncover unrecognised subclinical involvement that the perioperative stress response could unmask.

Skin & musculoskeletal

  • Note the extent of skin involvement (above/below knees and elbows) → indicates limited vs diffuse subtype.
  • Extensive or rapidly progressive skin disease = marker of severity, usually dcSSc, associated with cardiac and lung involvement.
  • Ulceration, subcutaneous calcinosis and superinfection → chronic pain, loss of movement/function (especially hand).
  • Ischaemia and necrosis → autoamputation of digital tufts, and in extreme cases phalanges.
  • In late disease skin may soften and become fragile — handle gently.

Cardiac & respiratory

  • Fibrosis + microcirculatory failure → decreased cardiac reserve and decreased left ventricular mass.
  • Subclinical cardiac disease is common — keep a low threshold for stress testing and advanced/invasive investigations.
  • Absence of respiratory symptoms is NOT reassuring — ILD may be detectable years before symptoms.
  • PAH definition now uses mPAP ≥ 20 mmHg at rest (older data used ≥ 25 mmHg) — beware interpreting historical data.
  • Consider time since PAH diagnosis and current treatment as indicators of progression/severity.
  • Up to one-third have sleep-disordered breathing with nocturnal SpO₂ < 90%.

Gastrointestinal (75% of patients)

  • Aspiration risk — influences choice of anaesthetic technique. Dysphagia and reflux oesophagitis are almost universally present.
  • Postoperative issues: GI bleed, obstructive acute abdomen, gastric stasis/ileus, constipation, bacterial overgrowth.
  • Long-term PPIs are common but premedication with an antacid + PPI should not be overlooked.
  • Impaired mastication + GI issues → malnutrition. Screen actively (e.g. MUST) and correct preoperatively.

Renal

  • Mild proteinuria, elevated creatinine and hypertension are common; up to 50% have asymptomatic renal disease until late.
  • Forms: scleroderma renal crisis, ANCA-associated vasculitis, isolated reduced GFR, reduced renal reserve, antiphospholipid-associated nephropathy, intrarenal arterial stiffness, penicillamine-associated disease, proteinuria.
  • Prerenal contributors: heart failure, PAH, hypovolaemia, diuretics, anti-inflammatory drugs.
  • Proteinuria >1 g/day is uncommon and suggests primary glomerular disease.

Practical assessment caveats

Simple measurements may be technically difficult and unreliable: pulse oximetry and non-invasive BP are affected by Raynaud's phenomenon and thickened skin. Plan alternatives in advance.

Screening cadence All patients should undergo annual screening for PAH: PFTs (DLCO) and TTE. Repeat preoperative TTE is indicated in those with SSc-PAH, specific cardiac risk factors, or known cardiac involvement without recent stratification.

Venous thromboembolism

  • SSc → 3-fold increased risk of VTE.
  • Risk factors: female sex, age 18–45, anti-Scl-70/anti-topoisomerase 1 positivity, first year since diagnosis, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, PAH, SSc-ILD, peripheral arterial disease, perioperative glucocorticoids, positive antiphospholipid screen, Raynaud's at presentation.
  • Up to 57% of patients in one study had positive antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL).
  • Suspect APS with livedo reticularis, digital ischaemia, VTE or stroke → rheumatology review and screening (anticardiolipin, β2-GP1, lupus anticoagulant, APTT).
Perioperative VTE prophylaxis should be routine in the absence of medical/surgical contraindications.

Monitoring

  • Thickened, bound skin and flexion contractures → oscillometric NIBP unreliable. Invasive arterial monitoring may be required even for uncomplicated surgery.
  • Skin thickness, ulceration and Raynaud's → poor pulse oximetry signal.
  • Vasospasm/Raynaud's affects digital cuff and "volume clamp" cardiac output/BP measurement.
  • Avoid digital cuff devices — may trigger vasospasm and finger necrosis.
  • Acute changes in pulse oximetry signal quality can herald Raynaud's (from poor temperature control, pain, perioperative stress or drugs).
  • Move the pulse oximetry probe regularly between sites to reduce digital ulcer risk.
  • Invasive arterial monitoring should preferentially use a large artery (e.g. brachial) to avoid RP and secondary ischaemia/necrosis.
  • TOE may be warranted for cardiac monitoring when cardiac involvement is suspected — decide case-by-case after reviewing recent gastroscopy (oesophageal involvement ↑ risk of probe injury).

Vascular access

  • Peripheral venous access may require ultrasound guidance.
  • Central venous access may be needed even for uncomplicated surgery if peripheral access cannot be secured reliably.

Positioning & general care

  • Skin fibrosis (pressure ulcer prone in late stages), decreased subcutaneous fat and contractures → meticulous positioning.
  • Keratoconjunctivitis sicca and eyelid skin changes are common → corneal lubrication and careful eye taping.
  • Prevent vasoconstrictive episodes: theatre temperature >23 °C, warm fluid infusion, convective air blankets.
  • A theoretical risk of intraoperative hyperthermia exists because of vasospasm and impaired sweating.

Airway

  • Challenges: difficult mask ventilation (facial anatomical deformities), limited mouth opening (microstomia, reduced TMJ movement), limited neck movement (soft-tissue fibrosis, not cervical spine involvement — so plain cervical spine imaging has limited planning value), and aspiration risk.
  • Microstomia + high Mallampati grade = ominous.
  • Preoperative mouth physiotherapy in the month before surgery may add up to 1 cm to mouth opening.
  • Untreated dental disease adds to airway difficulty.
  • Airway adjuncts (oral/nasal) significantly facilitate single-provider technique even when mask ventilation is difficult.
  • Strategies described: direct laryngoscopy, trans-tracheal wire guidance, fibreoptic intubation, intubating stylets, or avoiding airway manipulation with regional anaesthesia.
  • Awake fibreoptic intubation via the nasal route — use cautiously: risk of massive nasal haemorrhage and smaller nasal fossae.
  • Rapid sequence induction with cricoid pressure should be routine given the high incidence of GI involvement.

Ventilation

  • In advanced SSc-ILD, avoid intermittent positive pressure ventilation (IPPV) where possible.
  • Expect decreased compliance, increased airway pressures, poor oxygenation.
  • Lung protective strategy: tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, adjusted to driving and plateau pressures.
  • Adjust rate to maintain pH > 7.25; inspect flow trace for intrinsic PEEP.
  • Avoid high PEEP in fibrotic lungs; use a lung-resting strategy — tolerate atelectasis by titrating PEEP to lower values if SpO₂ stays 88–90% or above 8 kPa.
No specific anaesthetic technique has been shown to be ideal. Base the choice on: type of surgery; organ systems involved (PAH, cardiac impairment); aspiration risk; difficult/failed airway risk; and familiarity with the technique.
  • General anaesthesia with a "natural" airway (no tracheal tube or supraglottic device) has been used with no increase in intraoperative aspiration — but IPPV should be avoided when possible.
  • Optimise preoperatively: hydration, adequate haemoglobin, strict perioperative BP control (avoid hypotension), limit contrast agents → reduce renal impairment / SRC risk.

Regional anaesthesia

  • Attractive, particularly in severe pulmonary disease — but consider failure and conversion to GA.
  • Difficulties: positioning (contractures, fixed deformities), difficult skin puncture, and exaggerated haemodynamic instability (autonomic neuropathy + neuraxial vasodilatation).
  • Extensive soft-tissue fibrosis may limit spread of local anaesthetic and the effect of fascial plane blocks.
  • Prolonged sensory block has been described in SSc — mechanisms: decreased vascularity and increased fibrous tissue delaying systemic LA absorption, tissue pH differences, mechanical nerve compression in non-elastic perineural structures.
  • Neuraxial cannulation is not usually technically difficult (the spine is usually spared).
  • Neuraxial hypotension is hard to manage: excess IV fluid → pulmonary oedema/heart failure; vasoconstrictors → worsening RP, ischaemia, necrosis. Incremental titration via catheter techniques (continuous spinal or epidural) gives smoother haemodynamics.
Analgesia Good analgesia may reduce the risk of SRC and catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) postoperatively by mitigating the surgical stress response. A multimodal approach is likely most beneficial — but consider preoperative renal impairment and hypovolaemia.

Part B — Rheumatological Conditions in Critical Care ICU

Key points
  • Rheumatological disease (RD) management now focuses on early, aggressive immunosuppression using disease-modifying and biological agents.
  • These therapies cause immunosuppression and both specific and general side effects relevant to the intensivist.
  • Consider atypical infection — Pneumocystis pneumonia, tuberculosis, fungi — in all rheumatological patients on treatment presenting to ICU.
  • Disease flare and sepsis are often indistinguishable. Patients not responding to treatment may need further rheumatological/immunosuppressive treatment.
  • Prompt rheumatology consultation is paramount.

Why RDs matter in ICU

Predominantly affect young females Multi-system diseases Potential multi-organ failure ICU mortality 15–55% Complicated pharmacotherapy Immunocompromise (disease and/or drugs) Conditions coexist/overlap

Common rheumatological reasons for ICU admission

  1. New manifestations / end-organ sequelae of the RD
  2. Infection secondary to immunosuppressive treatment
  3. Adverse effects of disease-modifying drugs
  4. Acute critical illness unrelated to, but exacerbated by, the underlying RD

Three-quarters of RD ICU admissions are due to rheumatoid arthritis, SLE or scleroderma. Others: antiphospholipid syndrome, vasculitides, dermatomyositis.

The classic trap Differentiating sepsis from a flare of the underlying inflammatory disease. Management differs substantially — and treatment of both infection and the RD may be required in tandem.
Definition & mortality Rare but potentially lethal complication of several RDs; grouped under haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Loss of immune regulation → inappropriate macrophage and T-cell activity → uncontrolled, self-perpetuating hyperinflammation ("cytokine storm"). Quoted mortality 15–20% (one recent study 8%).

Cytokine profile: unregulated release of TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-10, IL-1β with markedly decreased natural killer cell activity.

  • Primary HLH — genetic perforin defects, mainly infants.
  • Secondary/acquired MAS-HLH — malignancy, acquired immune deficiency (e.g. transplant recipients), and RD.
  • Most often associated with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA); also SLE, adult-onset Still's disease, vasculitis.
  • May occur at any stage — onset, active disease, or quiescent disease. Can also develop de novo in non-rheumatology ICU patients suspected of sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction, or with triggers such as lymphoma and EBV.

Diagnosis

  • Difficult to differentiate from underlying RD or overwhelming sepsis — requires high clinical suspicion.
  • Trends in serum values are more important than absolute values.
  • Ferritin >10,000 µg/L is pathognomonic; >5,000 µg/L is very suggestive. Low percentage of glycosylated ferritin is useful. Consecutive ferritin levels can be used as a marker of response to therapy.
  • Marked changes: AST ↑346%, ALT ↑325%, D-dimer ↑122%, LDH ↑121%.
  • CRP expected to increase while ESR may decrease.

Treatment

  1. Early, aggressive supportive therapy
  2. High-dose corticosteroids — dexamethasone or methylprednisolone, escalating to additional immunosuppression with ciclosporin
  3. Elimination of known or suspected triggers
  4. Infection control

Refractory acquired MAS/HLH: IV immunoglobulin (1 g/kg for 2 days), plasma exchange, biological agents (anakinra most commonly selected). In one series: corticosteroids 98%, ciclosporin 61.2%, IVIG 36.3%, biological agents only 15.2%.

Deteriorated with rash, spiking temperatures, seizure, pancytopenia, ferritin >16,500 µg/L, triglycerides 10.3 mmol/L. Bottom line A life-threatening complication of systemic sclerosis affecting ~5–10% of patients with scleroderma. Presents as a fulminant, life-threatening acute episode. Any new rise in creatinine postoperatively must be taken seriously.
  • Associated with anti-RNA polymerase III antibodies.
  • Historically the leading cause of death in SSc — mortality has been attenuated by ACE inhibitors.
  • Features: microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, new/worsening hypertension, rising creatinine, proteinuria, oliguria.
  • Perioperative precipitants: hypotension, hypovolaemia, nephrotoxins, contrast, high-dose steroids, calcineurin inhibitors (renal vasoconstriction).
Management principles
  • ACE inhibitors are first-line — do not discontinue without specialist advice.
  • Maintain renal perfusion: avoid hypotension, hypovolaemia and nephrotoxins; limit contrast.
  • Involve rheumatology and nephrology early; renal replacement support may be required.
  • Steroid use in SSc is controversial because high doses have been associated with SRC — but high-dose steroids should not be withheld if otherwise indicated.
  • Calcineurin inhibitors (ciclosporin, tacrolimus) are associated with renal vasoconstriction and SRC.

The inverted pyramid Inflammatory burden drives long-term complications (e.g. cardiovascular disease — RA is an independent risk factor in QRISK2). Treatment is therefore started early and aggressively, often as dual or triple therapy. More patients are therefore on DMARDs and biologics.

Part C — Systemic Lupus Erythematosus CPG 2023

Diagnosis & classification

Diagnosis of SLE should be based on clinical manifestations supported by laboratory findings, following exclusion of alternative diagnoses. Classification criteria should not be used for diagnosis.

Principles of treatment

All patients with SLE should have clinical assessment of disease activity, using validated tools for SLE.

Treatment

  • Patients should practise sun avoidance, use protective clothing and broad-spectrum sunscreen with at least SPF 50.
  • Corticosteroids should be used for acute flare; the dose should be minimised and discontinued whenever possible.
  • All patients with SLE should be on hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) unless intolerant or contraindicated.
  • Immunosuppressants should be considered as add-on therapy in patients not responding to HCQ alone or in combination with corticosteroids, or when corticosteroids cannot be tapered.
  • Cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate mofetil may be used as induction therapy in certain major organ involvement.
  • Biologics may be used as adjunct therapy in active SLE despite standard therapy with corticosteroids and immunosuppressants.

Monitoring

  • All patients should be monitored on clinical and laboratory parameters.
  • Screen for cardiovascular risk factors and osteoporosis.
  • Infection should be identified early and treated accordingly.

Special considerations

  • All women of reproductive age should receive pre-pregnancy counselling.
  • In pregnancy: continue hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, calcineurin inhibitors and low-dose corticosteroids.
  • Low-dose aspirin should be initiated in all pregnant SLE patients unless intolerant or contraindicated.
  • Vaccination status and indications for further vaccination should be assessed yearly.
Clinical audit indicator Percentage of newly diagnosed SLE patients prescribed HCQ — target ≥90%.
SLE is a chronic autoimmune multisystem disorder with diverse and complex clinical manifestations characterised by inflammation in a variety of organs. Exact aetiology unknown; genetic, hormonal and environmental factors implicated. Relapsing–remitting course with unpredictable prognosis and considerable morbidity.
  • Survival has improved — 5-year survival ranges from 60% (Aboriginal Australians) to 98% (Shanghai); Malaysia ≈80%. 10-year survival 64% (Japan) to 94% (Hong Kong).
  • Leading causes of death: infections and active disease (predominantly lupus nephritis).
  • Management challenges in Asia: limited healthcare access, delayed diagnosis, poor treatment adherence.

Non-pharmacological

  • Sun protection: broad-spectrum sunscreen SPF 50–75 reduces lesion development on photo-provocation. Advise sun avoidance and protective clothing.
  • Nurse-led care: transitional care programmes led by specialist nurses improved self-care, quality of life and reduced readmission up to 90 days.
  • Physiotherapy/exercise and psychological therapy: combination of aerobic exercise or psychological intervention (psychoeducation, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, biofeedback-assisted CBT with relaxation) with usual medical care improved fatigue, psychological function, pain and quality of life.

Corticosteroids

  • Cornerstone of SLE treatment; rapid onset; useful in mild to severe disease.
  • Long-term adverse events (per 1000 person-years): peptic ulcer 147.84; bacterial infection 136.42; fungal infection 42.26; hypertension 37.67; osteoporosis 28.17; fracture 23.77; tuberculosis 7.85.
  • Medium-to-high dose: pooled rates 25/100 patients/year for infections, 12/100 for avascular necrosis of the hip, 9/100 for hyperglycaemia/diabetes.
  • Risk of new organ damage increases significantly with higher corticosteroid dosage.

Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ)

  • Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory. Reduces flares, achieves higher remission in membranous LN, protects against irreversible organ damage, thrombosis and bone loss.
  • Reduced risk of death (HR 0.46) and renal damage (HR 0.30) vs non-use.
  • Long-term use is safe; toxicity infrequent, mild and usually reversible.
  • Retinal toxicity: incidence ~1 in 1000 person-years; no retinal toxicity reported in the first five years of exposure. Dose should not exceed 5 mg/kg actual body weight.
  • Adherence can be assessed with blood drug levels — not routine practice.
Recommendation 6 All patients with SLE should be on HCQ unless intolerant or contraindicated. Ophthalmologic assessment at baseline, then: yearly if retinopathy risk factors present; after five years and yearly thereafter if none. Risk factors: long-term/high-dose HCQ, hepatic and renal disease, tamoxifen, retinal/macular disease, advanced age. HCQ is safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE)

Treatment determined by pathophysiology: inflammatory → corticosteroids and/or immunosuppressants; thrombotic → anticoagulant/antithrombotic (especially with aPL). CYC plus corticosteroids more effective than corticosteroids alone. Antiepileptics and antipsychotics may be adjuncts.

Haematological

Significant lupus thrombocytopenia (platelets <30,000/mm³) and AIHA: first-line = moderate/high-dose corticosteroids + immunosuppressant (AZA, MMF or ciclosporin).

Cardiorespiratory

Limited evidence. In lupus myocarditis, adding CYC to corticosteroids showed no significant difference in ICU stay, hospital stay or median LVEF. Management of organ-specific SLE is complex — a multidisciplinary team approach with rheumatology and relevant specialists is recommended.

Contraception

Oral contraceptives and intrauterine devices have no association with thrombosis, worsening SLEDAI or mortality in SLE. Discuss contraception and pregnancy planning at initial or early visit with women of reproductive age; assess aPL status. Combined oestrogen–progestin contraceptives are avoided if aPL positive or in moderate–high disease activity (oestrogen patch avoided in SLE). In special circumstances (e.g. mycophenolate), an IUD or a combination of two other forms of contraception is required. Avoid depot medroxyprogesterone acetate in patients at risk of osteoporosis.

Lactation & adolescents

  • Breastfeeding is encouraged and not contraindicated provided disease control is maintained with lactation-compatible medication.
  • SLE is diagnosed during childhood in 15–20%. A co-ordinated transition from paediatric to adult care during adolescence is recommended. A structured transition programme improves health-related quality of life, disease knowledge, satisfaction, vocational readiness and disease-specific health status.

Indications for referral to a rheumatologist

  • Confirm diagnosis; assess disease activity and severity
  • Provide general disease management
  • Manage organ involvement or life-threatening disease
  • Manage/prevent treatment toxicities
  • Specific circumstances: APS, pregnancy and perioperative management

Urgent referral (red flags)

Clinical suspicion of SLE with major or multisystem organ involvement (undiagnosed) Disease flare with major organ or multisystem involvement (diagnosed) Pregnancy (at booking) Severe infection

For moderate-to-severe organ involvement, patients require multidisciplinary care involving various subspecialties.

Source note The CPG explicitly lists perioperative management as an indication for rheumatology referral, but does not provide anaesthetic-specific guidance. The points below synthesise the CPG content with the perioperative principles from the rheumatology critical care and SSc literature in this reference.

Preoperative checklist

  • Disease activity — use SLEDAI-2K/BILAG; active major organ involvement (nephritis, neuropsychiatric, cytopenias, vasculitis) → defer elective surgery and refer.
  • Renal — renal profile, urinalysis, UPCR/24hUP. Class III–V nephritis, proteinuria, impaired eGFR alter drug dosing and fluid strategy.
  • Cardiorespiratory — serositis, PAH (associated with anti-RNP and anti-SSA), ILD, coronary artery disease. SLE is an independent CVD risk factor.
  • Haematological — thrombocytopenia, anaemia, leukopenia; consider bleeding risk and infection risk.
  • Antiphospholipid status — check LA, aCL, anti-β2GP1. Plan perioperative anticoagulation carefully; DOACs are not recommended for secondary prevention in SLE-APS.
  • Infection screen — consider TB and atypical organisms in immunosuppressed patients. Both ESR and CRP elevated suggests infection rather than flare.
  • Steroid cover — patients on adrenal-suppressive doses (>5 mg prednisolone/day for >4 weeks) require perioperative supplementation; evidence does not support withholding it.
  • Medication plan — HCQ is continued (safe in pregnancy/lactation); consider MTX continuation (recent guidance); rituximab surgery at the 7th month since last dose; AZA, MMF, CYC, biologics require individualised decisions with rheumatology.
  • Vaccination — assess yearly; vaccinate before immunosuppression or during remission where possible.

Intraoperative & ICU considerations

  • Airway: consider involvement of the larynx, cricoarytenoid joints and cervical spine; microstomia and mucosal ulceration may complicate intubation. Avoid excessive pressure on skin and joints.
  • Aspiration risk: oesophageal dysmotility, reflux and GI involvement — consider rapid sequence induction and premedication with antacid/PPI.
  • Renal protection: maintain perfusion and avoid nephrotoxins, especially with nephritis or on calcineurin inhibitors.
  • Infection: strict asepsis; postoperative fever may be infection, flare, or both — do not assume one or the other.
  • Thrombosis: APS and aPL positivity substantially increase VTE and arterial event risk. Balance thromboprophylaxis against bleeding risk in thrombocytopenia.
  • Drug interactions: steroid-related hyperglycaemia, hypertension, osteoporosis and peptic ulcer disease; NSAID-related renal, hepatic and CV toxicity; the possibility of MTX pneumonitis with unexplained respiratory deterioration.
  • If the patient worsens despite antibiotics — consider a flare of the underlying rheumatic disease, MAS/HLH or CAPS, and involve rheumatology urgently.
