1 · Definition & Diagnosis (Berlin criteria)

The older American–European Consensus Conference (AECC, 1994) definition was replaced in 2011/2012 by the Berlin definition, which improved reliability and predictive value. It also abolished the term “acute lung injury” (ALI) and categorises ARDS as mild, moderate or severe.

Berlin definition: “An acute diffuse, inflammatory lung injury, leading to increased pulmonary vascular permeability, increased lung weight, and loss of aerated lung tissue… [with] hypoxaemia and bilateral radiographic opacities, associated with increased venous admixture, increased physiological dead space, and decreased lung compliance.”

Four key diagnostic criteria

  1. Acute onset — within 7 days of a defined trigger (e.g. pneumonia).
  2. Hypoxaemia — PaO2:FiO2 ratio < 300 mmHg (< 40 kPa) on at least 5 cmH2O PEEP.
  3. Bilateral lung infiltrates on chest X-ray or CT.
  4. Not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload — echocardiography is now recommended (rather than left atrial / pulmonary artery pressure measurement). The definition only requires that respiratory failure is not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload, so cardiac disease and ARDS can coexist.

Validation & caveats

  • The Berlin definition has slightly better prediction validity for mortality than the AECC definition — area under the ROC curve 0.577 (95% CI 0.561–0.593) vs 0.536 (95% CI 0.520–0.553), P < 0.001.
  • Features that clinicians often assumed were prognostically useful — high PEEP (> 10 cmH2O), static lung compliance, radiographic severity, and corrected expired volume > 10 L min−1 — were not predictive of mortality or other outcomes, and were removed from the first draft of the Berlin definition.
  • No clinical gold standard exists for ARDS. Diffuse alveolar damage on post-mortem confirms the diagnosis in non-survivors, but histological biopsy is rarely performed in the critically ill — so over-diagnosis is possible, and false-positive recruitment into trials may confound results.

2 · Epidemiology

LUNG SAFE study (landmark observational data)

  • Prospective observational study of ARDS epidemiology and management: 29,000 patients, > 50 countries.
  • ARDS criteria fulfilled in 10% of all ICU admissions and in 23% of patients requiring mechanical ventilation.
  • Severity distribution: mild 30%, moderate 47%, severe 23%.
  • Median duration of mechanical ventilation 8 days; median ICU stay 10 days.
  • ICU mortality 34%; hospital mortality 40%.
  • Conclusion: ARDS is under-recognised and under-treated, with high mortality. Clinician recognition occurred in only about 60% of cases, and adherence to best practice was poor.

Other figures

  • Estimated incidence ≈ 64 cases per 100,000 people (figures vary widely because of the subjectivity of the old AECC definition).
  • ARDS is associated with multiple organ dysfunction and carries high mortality, morbidity and financial cost.

4 · Pathophysiology (three overlapping phases)

ARDS is the end result of a complex interplay of inflammatory mediators producing diffuse alveolar damage, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, surfactant dysfunction and atelectasis. Angiotensin II is of particular research interest because of its ability to induce alveolar apoptosis and fibrosis.

Phase 1 — Exudative / acute (up to ~1 week)

  • Acute inflammatory response with release of cytokines — TNF-α, IL-1 and IL-6.
  • These stimulate neutrophil migration into the alveoli, where they become activated and release reactive oxygen species.
  • Free-radical production damages endothelial cells and pneumocytes.
  • Damage to alveolar and capillary endothelium causes accumulation of protein-rich inflammatory exudate in alveoli and interstitium — i.e. non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema.
  • Plasma proteins denature alveolar surfactant → alveolar collapse.
  • Collapsed / fluid-filled alveoli cause shunt → hypoxaemia; there is also reduced pulmonary compliance.
  • Radiological infiltrates appear during this phase.

Phase 2 — Proliferative / subacute (≈ 72 hours to day 7; may begin from day 5)

  • Proliferation of type 2 pneumocytes, fibroblasts and myofibroblasts with deposition of new matrix.
  • Fibroproliferation and microvascular thrombus formation cause further reduction in lung compliance and continuing hypoxaemia.
  • Some patients improve rapidly at this stage; others progress.

Phase 3 — Fibrotic / chronic or resolution

  • Either resolution and reconstitution of normal pulmonary parenchyma, or a fibrotic stage.
  • Interstitial and intra-alveolar fibrosis results from excessive, disordered collagen deposition and is associated with a poor outcome; lung remodelling may be irreversible.
  • The factors determining progression to fibrosis versus resolution are not well understood.
“Baby lung” concept: ARDS affects alveoli heterogeneously. Some areas become stiff and hard to expand, while more normal, compliant alveoli may become over-distended and damaged. Patients therefore have a smaller proportion of lung available for ventilation and lower respiratory system compliance (CRS).
5 · Investigations & imaging
  • ARDS is predominantly a clinical diagnosis; adequate clinician experience is needed to recognise the mimics.
  • Chest radiograph: bilateral lung infiltrates are an early finding.
  • Echocardiography: used to quantify cardiac function and exclude a cardiac explanation (Berlin criterion 4).
  • CT: particularly useful when there is an underlying pulmonary cause. It demonstrates the heterogeneity of the disease — small areas of relatively unaffected lung with potential for recruitment, interspersed with segments of severely affected lung (typically anterior sparing with extensive posterior change).
  • Caution: transferring a patient with ARDS for CT is not without risk and may lead to subsequent increases in ventilatory support.
6 · Ventilatory strategies (lung-protective ventilation)
Core principle — lung-protective ventilation: minimise lung injury by ventilating with low tidal volumes at controlled pressure, using appropriate PEEP, a rationalised FiO2, and a permissive approach to hypercapnoea. These basics require minimal specialised equipment and should be achievable in any ICU.

Guidelines: American Thoracic Society / European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (2017) and the UK Faculty of Intensive Care Medicine / Intensive Care Society ARDS guideline (2018). Despite a strong evidence base, LUNG SAFE found ARDS under-diagnosed (recognised in only 60% of cases) and poor adherence to best practice.

6.1 · Low-tidal-volume ventilation — the cornerstone

Pioneered by the ARDSnet ARMA trial (2000):

  • Tidal volume 6 mL/kg based on ideal (predicted) body weight
  • Plateau pressure < 30 cmH2O
  • Permissive hypercapnoea tolerated as long as pH > 7.2

In the landmark trial, traditional tidal volumes of 12 mL/kg with plateau pressure ≤ 50 cmH2O were compared with 6 mL/kg and plateau ≤ 30 cmH2O. Survival (39.8% vs 31%) and ventilator-free days were higher in the lower tidal volume group. Notably, minute ventilation was higher than in previous low-tidal-volume studies, and treatment of acidosis may have contributed to the improved outcomes.

The benefit is thought to arise from reducing excessive distension/stretch of the aerated lung — which can release pro-inflammatory cytokines contributing to lung injury (volutrauma) and other organ injury.

Ideal body weight (kg) from height (cm)

  • Males = 50 + 0.91 × (height − 152.4)
  • Females = 45.5 + 0.91 × (height − 152.4)

Practical mechanics

  • Position the patient optimally to avoid extra-pulmonary causes of high ventilator pressures.
  • Assess for and decompress a distended, high-pressure abdomen — nasogastric drainage or surgical means.
  • Barriers to successful implementation of low-tidal-volume ventilation are common; anticipate and address them early rather than abandoning lung protection.
6.2 · Driving pressure (ΔP) — the “baby lung” index

ΔP reflects the reduced functional size of the lung by accounting for lung compliance. Also called the driving pressure:

ΔP = VT / CRS = inspiratory plateau pressure − PEEP
  • A post hoc analysis of 3,562 ARDS patients from nine RCTs evaluated ΔP as a predictor of outcome.
  • In trials where tidal volume and PEEP were independent variables, decreased ΔP was the variable most strongly associated with survival.
  • This suggests that reducing tidal volume in relation to lung compliance, rather than in relation to ideal body weight alone, may be the key to improving survival.
  • Further trials are needed to determine whether PEEP increments are protective only when the increased PEEP allows the same VT to be delivered at a lower ΔP.
An individualised, stepwise approach to lung recruitment and PEEP selection has been described. This may redistribute tidal volume and transpulmonary pressures more homogeneously, improve overall compliance with a larger functional lung, and allow a further decrease in ΔP without decreasing ventilation. Where evidence for optimal PEEP is lacking, individual assessment of lung mechanics may be appropriate.
6.3 · Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)

Physiological effects

  • Prevents alveolar derecruitment, minimising atelectrauma.
  • Increases functional residual capacity.
  • Displaces fluid from the alveoli into the interstitium.
  • Moves the lung onto the steeper part of the compliance curve.
  • Reduces shunt and ventilation/perfusion mismatch.
Balance against: potential hyperinflation and decreased venous return → decreased cardiac output.

Setting PEEP

PEEP is patient-specific and determining the “optimal” level is challenging. Several strategies have been suggested — none has proven superiority:

  • Use of PEEP/FiO2 tables
  • Titration based on plateau pressure
  • Stepwise increase until maximal compliance
  • Recruitment manoeuvre followed by decremental titration
  • Based on the inflection point of the pressure/volume curve

Guidelines recommend high PEEP (defined variably, but on average 15 cmH2O) in patients with moderate and severe ARDS, in whom a mortality benefit was demonstrated in clinical trials. ARDSnet recommend increasing PEEP in tandem with increasing FiO2. No study has identified a universally optimal PEEP level.

6.4 · Prone-position ventilation

Postulated physiological benefits

  • Improved recruitment of atelectatic dorsal lung tissue; note that total respiratory compliance may actually decrease even as oxygenation improves.
  • More even distribution of tidal volume.
  • Improved V/Q matching (V = pulmonary ventilation; Q = pulmonary perfusion).
  • Decreases alveolar hyperinflation.
  • Reduces cardiac compression of the underlying lung.
  • Improves drainage of secretions.
  • Experimental evidence suggests it may influence the generation and evolution of ventilator-induced lung injury.

Evidence

  • Long known to improve oxygenation, but evidence of a mortality benefit was lacking until recently.
  • Gattinoni et al. (2010) — meta-analysis of four major trials: absolute mortality of severely hypoxaemic ARDS patients may be reduced by around 10% with prone positioning.
  • PROSEVA (2013) — landmark “Prone positioning in severe ARDS” trial: mortality benefit when used for > 12 hours per day, in the severe ARDS subgroup only. 28-day mortality 16% prone vs 32% supine; number needed to treat = 6.
Safety: must be carried out with caution and appropriate training. Proven increased risk of endotracheal tube obstruction and pressure sores. Prone positioning is best attempted in selected severe ARDS patients in a fully equipped, trained unit.
6.5 · Airway pressure release ventilation (APRV)
  • Sometimes known as “inverse ratio ventilation”.
  • The majority of the respiratory cycle (usually around 90%) is spent at a high pressure, with a brief drop to lower pressure to allow expiration.
  • Crucially, the time at low pressure is set to allow the expiratory flow rate to decrease only to 70–75% of its peak value — preventing alveolar derecruitment.
  • Spontaneous breathing without additional support is encouraged.
  • Rationale: the higher mean airway pressure opens the alveoli and holds them open, improving oxygenation and decreasing lung injury — the “open lung” concept. Supported by small trials and animal studies.
Evidence is currently weak: only one single-centre RCT, which showed improved gas exchange and more ventilator-free days in the APRV group. This minimal evidence base meant neither the ATS nor the ICS guidelines offered an opinion on APRV.
6.6 · High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV)
  • More commonly used in paediatrics. The lung is held open at a high mean airway pressure (typically 25–30 cmH2O), with small tidal volumes of 1–3 mL/kg delivered at a frequency of 3–15 Hz.
  • Theory: low VT with higher PEEP limits ventilator-associated lung injury.

Two trials published in 2013 changed practice

  • OSCAR (High Frequency OSCillation in ARDS) — multicentre, non-blinded RCT using lower airway pressures than OSCILLATE; no difference in mortality vs control at 1 month.
  • OSCILLATE (High-Frequency Oscillation in Early ARDS) — halted early after 548 patients; in-hospital mortality 47% with HFOV vs 35% with low VT/high PEEP. Possible confounders: more midazolam, neuromuscular blockade, vasopressors and higher mean airway pressures in the HFOV group.

As a result, HFOV has fallen out of favour; guidelines strongly recommend against its routine use in ARDS. It may be considered in refractory cases where access to ECMO is limited.

6.7 · Recruitment manoeuvres

Aim: use a transient increase in ventilatory pressure to open (“recruit”) collapsed alveoli, reversing the atelectasis of ARDS and increasing the lung area available for gas exchange. Following the manoeuvre, PEEP is generally increased to hold the newly recruited alveoli open.

Described methods

  • Sustained inflation — e.g. CPAP at 30–40 cmH2O for 30–40 seconds.
  • Stepwise increase in peak pressure and/or PEEP.
  • “Extended sigh” — PEEP incrementally increased while tidal volume is incrementally decreased, aiming to keep plateau pressure constant. This is followed by sustained inflation at CPAP 30 cmH2O, and the sequence is then carried out in reverse to return to baseline.
Recruitment manoeuvres undoubtedly improve oxygenation and compliance — at least temporarily — but they can cause haemodynamic instability and barotrauma, and the physiological benefit may not be sustained.
  • ATS 2017 guidelines give a weak conditional recommendation for their use.
  • However, since publication, the ART trial (Alveolar Recruitment for ARDS Trial, 2017) showed increased mortality with a strategy of recruitment manoeuvre plus PEEP titration.
  • The place of the recruitment manoeuvre is therefore currently unclear.
6.8 · Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)
  • Uses large-bore central venous access to remove blood, pass it through a gas exchanger, and return it to the (usually venous) circulation. A modified cardiopulmonary bypass device used at the bedside for days to weeks.
  • Invasive, expensive and complex; a highly effective method of oxygenation, with a particular role in both gas exchange and circulatory support.
  • Complications relate to vascular access, anticoagulation and serious bleeding.
  • As a specialist therapy, its use is limited to respiratory failure centres.

Trial evidence

  • CESAR (2009) — showed a mortality benefit in those assigned to the ECMO treatment group. Criticised as being a trial of good conventional treatment at a respiratory failure centre rather than a trial of ECMO specifically, because 24% of patients assigned to ECMO did not receive it. Also lacked safety analysis of ECMO and standardised ARDSnet care; transfer to a tertiary centre was a confounder.
  • EOLIA (2018) — ECMO for severe ARDS showed an 11% reduction in mortality in the treatment group, which failed to reach statistical significance. The trial was underpowered and complicated by a high rate of crossover from control to ECMO.
  • ECMO also gained a particular role during the 2009 H1N1 pandemic — largely young patients with respiratory failure from non-necrotising viral pneumonia and little co-morbidity.
Present role: ECMO is a rescue therapy in severe ARDS only.
7 · Non-ventilatory & adjunctive strategies
7.1 · Conservative fluid therapy

Given that ARDS is a pathological state of non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, conservative fluid management appears intuitive. Excessive fluid administration is deleterious to gas exchange because of increased pulmonary capillary permeability.

  • FACTT (Fluid And Catheters Treatment Trial) compared conservative and liberal fluid strategies: no difference in mortality, but a significant improvement in ventilator-free days and ICU-free days in favour of the conservative arm.
  • Reassuringly, no increased risk of renal failure / acute kidney injury was identified in the conservative arm.
  • Despite the lack of strong evidence, guidelines give a weak recommendation for conservative fluid management.

Albumin

  • A systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs (Uhlig et al.) in ARDS patients showed albumin treatment improved oxygenation in the first 2 days and after 7 days compared with crystalloid therapy — but this did not translate into a mortality difference. The sample size was relatively small.
  • Research has indicated favourable physiological changes when albumin is combined with furosemide in ARDS patients with low serum albumin: improved oxygenation, weight loss and normalisation of serum protein levels, with no significant adverse events — but no direct impact on ventilator time or survival.
  • Without strong evidence dictating how this is achieved, there is increasingly a move to a negative fluid balance approach, which also aims to address low colloid oncotic pressure.
7.2 · Neuromuscular blockade (NMB)

Rationale

  • Improve patient–ventilator synchrony by eliminating spontaneous respiratory effort and coughing.
  • May facilitate compliance with lung-protective ventilation protocols and reduce barotrauma.
  • Improves oxygenation as measured by PaO2:FiO2 ratio — in part due to better control of ventilation and in part due to a decrease in oxygen consumption.
Paralysis clearly necessitates a deep level of sedation, which may cause additional problems. There are also concerns about a role in causing increased ICU-acquired weakness.

Evidence

  • The Intensive Care Society guideline concludes there is weak evidence from systematic reviews to support the use of a 48-hour cisatracurium infusion in patients with moderate to severe ARDS.
  • All trials to date have been of cisatracurium, which may have a direct anti-inflammatory action in addition to its neuromuscular effect. It is unclear whether these findings generalise to other neuromuscular blockers.
  • An observational study comparing cisatracurium with vecuronium found no difference in mortality but a significant reduction in ventilator days in favour of cisatracurium.
7.3 · Corticosteroids

Given the known inflammatory component of ARDS, there is a potential role for steroids to suppress this phase. Many small studies exist, but many were of low quality and were conducted before the advent of lung-protective ventilation.

  • SCCM/ESICM corticosteroid guideline: methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg/day is recommended with moderate certainty for moderate to severe ARDS.
  • Meduri trial of low-dose methylprednisolone in early ARDS: numbers were small (91 patients) and the placebo group had more patients with catecholamine-dependent shock, rendering the reduction in ventilator days less significant.
  • LaSRS trial (late rescue steroids for ARDS): reported an increase in ventilator- and ICU-free days but no improvement in mortality. In fact, those with persistent ARDS had higher mortality with a number needed to harm of 4.
  • Steroids may potentially increase the risk of hospital-acquired infections, neuromyopathy and delirium.
  • Due to the low quality of existing evidence, the FICM/ICS document makes a recommendation for further research only. Steroids have not reliably shown improvement in hospital mortality and are not recommended for routine use. Their role remains controversial; further trials are ongoing.
7.4 · Other adjunctive / pharmacological therapies

A number of pharmaceutical therapies have been trialled in attempts to improve V/Q matching, reduce ventilator-induced lung injury, and modulate the inflammatory process underlying ARDS.

Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) — detail

  • Used historically, but evidence of efficacy is lacking.
  • Meta-analysis of 12 RCTs, 1,237 patients: despite improved oxygenation at 24 h, no mortality benefit was gained, and a possible link was found to increased renal dysfunction.
  • A new understanding that iNO may have complex systemic effects means that, without a clear mortality benefit in ARDS, it has no role in current treatment.

Statins were considered because of anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating effects; ketoconazole for its anti-inflammatory properties. Neither demonstrated improvement in mortality or other endpoints. Existing evidence does not support their use.

7.5 · Nutrition
  • Nutrition is a key part of treatment in critical illness, but optimal feeding in ARDS has not been delineated.
  • EDEN trial: failed to establish a difference between standard full enteral feed and trophic feed in terms of the number of ventilator-free days, or in survivor outcomes at 6 and 12 months.
8 · Prognosis & long-term outcomes
  • Studies on long-term outcomes of ARDS survivors are limited, but reductions in functional outcomes are increasingly recognised.
  • In one study of 109 ARDS survivors, the 6-minute walking distances at 1, 3 and 5 years were 66%, 67% and 76% of predicted values respectively.
  • None of the patients in this relatively young cohort had returned to normal function within the 5-year follow-up.
  • Significant cognitive and psychiatric sequelae are also recognised in survivors, as is persistent cognitive impairment in the longer term.
9 · Summary & key points
  • ARDS is a syndrome of hypoxaemic respiratory failure associated with non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, occurring in 10% of ICU patients and 23% of those requiring mechanical ventilation.
  • It is characterised by an acute inflammatory reaction followed by a proliferative stage, which may then progress to fibrosis or resolution.
  • Diagnosed by the Berlin definition, and categorised as mild, moderate or severe by PaO2:FiO2 ratio.
  • Principles of management aim to avoid exacerbating lung injury by ventilating at low tidal volumes with controlled pressure, appropriate PEEP, and a permissive approach to hypercapnoea.
  • Adjuncts include a conservative fluid management strategy and, in moderate to severe cases, neuromuscular blockade and prone-position ventilation.
  • ECMO can be used as a rescue therapy for severe cases.
  • No routine role for steroids, statins, inhaled nitric oxide, or high-frequency oscillatory ventilation.
  • Adverse functional and neuropsychological outcomes are increasingly recognised in long-term ARDS survivors.
10 · Ten high-yield case examples
Case 1 · Does this meet Berlin criteria — and how severe?

A 34-year-old man, day 2 of severe acute pancreatitis, is intubated for hypoxaemia. FiO2 0.6, PEEP 10 cmH2O, PaO2 9.3 kPa (70 mmHg). CXR shows bilateral infiltrates. Echocardiography: normal LV function, no significant valve disease, no fluid overload.

Answer: P/F = 70 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 117 mmHg → moderate ARDS (100–200 mmHg). All four Berlin criteria are met: acute onset within 7 days of a defined trigger (pancreatitis), P/F < 300 on PEEP ≥ 5, bilateral infiltrates, and not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload (echo normal).

Action: lung-protective ventilation (VT 6 mL/kg IBW, plateau < 30 cmH2O), optimise PEEP, conservative fluid strategy. Prone positioning is generally reserved for severe disease, but re-assess severity frequently — deterioration to P/F < 150 on FiO2 ≥ 0.6 triggers consideration of prone ventilation.

Case 2 · Severe ARDS — what is the full bundle?

A 68-year-old with faecal peritonitis and septic shock, day 1 of ICU. FiO2 0.9, PEEP 14 cmH2O, PaO2 8.0 kPa (60 mmHg). Plateau pressure 32 cmH2O. CXR: bilateral infiltrates.

Answer: P/F = 60 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 67 mmHg → severe ARDS (< 100 mmHg; mortality ~45%).

  • Lung-protective ventilation: reduce VT towards 6 mL/kg IBW; target plateau < 30 cmH2O; accept permissive hypercapnoea while pH > 7.2.
  • PEEP: guidelines recommend high PEEP (average ~15 cmH2O) in moderate–severe ARDS where a mortality benefit has been shown.
  • Prone positioning > 12 h/day — PROSEVA: 28-day mortality 16% vs 32%, NNT 6. Requires a trained team; watch for ETT obstruction and pressure sores.
  • Neuromuscular blockade: consider 48 h cisatracurium infusion (weak evidence in moderate–severe ARDS).
  • Conservative fluid strategy — more ventilator-free and ICU-free days, no excess AKI.
  • Refractory hypoxaemia: discuss referral to a respiratory failure centre for ECMO as rescue therapy.
Case 3 · Plateau pressure is 38 — what now?

A man of height 175 cm, weight 70 kg, is ventilated with a tidal volume of 700 mL. Plateau pressure is 38 cmH2O. His oxygen requirements are rising.

Answer: Ideal body weight = 50 + 0.91 × (175 − 152.4) = ≈ 70.6 kg. Target VT = 6 mL/kg IBW ≈ 420 mL (current VT is ~10 mL/kg IBW).

  • Reduce tidal volume to 6 mL/kg IBW and target plateau pressure < 30 cmH2O.
  • Accept permissive hypercapnoea provided pH > 7.2.
  • Consider driving pressure (ΔP = plateau − PEEP) — a post hoc analysis of 3,562 ARDS patients found decreased ΔP was the variable most strongly associated with survival.
  • Exclude extra-pulmonary causes of high pressures: patient position, abdominal distension (consider NG drainage or surgical decompression), bronchospasm, secretions, circuit issues.
Case 4 · pH falls to 7.16 after reducing tidal volume

After lowering the tidal volume to 6 mL/kg IBW, the patient's pH is 7.16 and PaCO2 9.5 kPa. The registrar wants to increase the tidal volume back up.

Answer: Permissive hypercapnoea is an accepted component of lung-protective ventilation and is tolerated as long as pH > 7.2. Do not abandon lung protection — cyclical over-stretching causes volutrauma and drives mortality.

  • Increase respiratory rate (watch for auto-PEEP and air trapping).
  • Reduce dead space: suction secretions, treat bronchospasm, check circuit/tubing length.
  • Ensure adequate sedation and patient–ventilator synchrony.
  • Consider renal replacement therapy/buffering in extreme refractory acidaemia.
  • Remember permissive hypercapnoea is relatively contraindicated with raised intracranial pressure, severe pulmonary hypertension or severe LV dysfunction.
Case 5 · Prone positioning — who, how long, and what are the risks?

A colleague asks you to prone a patient with severe ARDS (P/F 110 on FiO2 0.8 and PEEP 12).

Answer:

  • Indication: mortality benefit demonstrated in the severe ARDS subgroup only, when used for > 12 hours each day. PROSEVA enrolled patients with P/F < 150 on FiO2 ≥ 0.6 and PEEP ≥ 5.
  • Benefits: recruitment of atelectatic dorsal lung, more even tidal volume distribution, improved V/Q matching, reduced alveolar hyperinflation, reduced cardiac compression of lung, improved secretion drainage. Note that total respiratory compliance may actually fall even as oxygenation improves.
  • Risks: proven increased risk of endotracheal tube obstruction and pressure sores; also haemodynamic instability.
  • Requirements: caution, appropriate training, and a fully equipped unit with an experienced team.
Case 6 · Request for inhaled nitric oxide

A patient with severe ARDS remains hypoxaemic despite lung-protective ventilation and high PEEP. A colleague suggests inhaled nitric oxide.

Answer: A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs and 1,237 patients showed that inhaled nitric oxide improved oxygenation at 24 hours but produced no mortality benefit, with a possible link to increased renal dysfunction. It has no role in current treatment. Inhaled prostacyclin has insufficient evidence.

Instead: optimise PEEP and ΔP, ensure lung-protective volumes, consider prone positioning, consider neuromuscular blockade, manage fluid balance conservatively, and refer for ECMO assessment if refractory.

Case 7 · Mild ARDS — how aggressive should you be?

A 55-year-old with community-acquired pneumonia, day 1 of ICU, FiO2 0.5, PEEP 5 cmH2O, PaO2 125 mmHg. Bilateral infiltrates. Haemodynamically stable, euvolaemic, good urine output.

Answer: P/F = 125 ÷ 0.5 = 250 mmHg → mild ARDS (200–300 mmHg; cohort mortality ~27%).

  • Lung-protective ventilation with VT 6 mL/kg IBW and plateau < 30 cmH2O.
  • Conservative fluid strategy — FACTT showed no mortality difference but significantly more ventilator-free and ICU-free days, with no increase in acute kidney injury.
  • Not indicated routinely: prone positioning (reserved for severe), neuromuscular blockade (moderate–severe), steroids (controversial), inhaled nitric oxide or HFOV.
  • Treat the underlying cause and monitor closely for deterioration — ARDS severity can escalate quickly.
Case 8 · ARDS or cardiogenic pulmonary oedema?

A 72-year-old with known heart failure presents with bilateral infiltrates, hypoxaemia and raised JVP. How do you distinguish ARDS from cardiogenic pulmonary oedema?

Answer: Apply Berlin criterion 4 — the respiratory failure must not be fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload. The Berlin definition moved away from pulmonary artery occlusion pressure and left atrial pressure measurement; echocardiography is now the recommended tool and accounts for patients with congestive heart failure or left atrial hypertension who also develop ARDS.

  • Use echocardiography to quantify cardiac function and identify structural disease.
  • Both conditions can coexist — if ARDS criteria are met, ARDS management still applies.
  • Note that historical bedside indices such as high PEEP (> 10 cmH2O), static compliance, radiographic severity and corrected expired volume > 10 L/min were not predictive of outcome and were removed from the draft Berlin definition.
Case 9 · Recruitment manoeuvre requested

A colleague proposes a sustained inflation to 40 cmH2O for 40 seconds, followed by a PEEP titration, in a patient with severe ARDS and a high plateau pressure.

Answer: Recruitment manoeuvres do improve oxygenation and compliance at least temporarily, but they can cause haemodynamic instability and barotrauma, and the physiological benefit may not be sustained.

  • ATS 2017: weak conditional recommendation for use.
  • ART trial (2017): showed increased mortality with a strategy of recruitment manoeuvre plus PEEP titration.
  • The place of recruitment manoeuvres is currently unclear — do not use routinely, and be especially cautious with haemodynamic instability or pre-existing barotrauma.
Case 10 · Survivor asks about long-term prognosis

A patient who survived severe ARDS 12 months ago asks whether he will return to normal.

Answer: Adverse functional and neuropsychological outcomes are increasingly recognised in long-term ARDS survivors.

  • In a study of 109 ARDS survivors, 6-minute walking distances at 1, 3 and 5 years were 66%, 67% and 76% of predicted values.
  • None of this relatively young cohort had returned to normal function within the 5-year follow-up.
  • Significant cognitive and psychiatric sequelae are recognised, as is persistent cognitive impairment in the longer term.
  • Refer to critical care follow-up and rehabilitation services; manage expectations honestly but supportively.
