1 Admission, Discharge & Triage
Core principle

Admission decisions are based on the concept of potential benefit. Admit critically ill patients with a reversible condition who have a reasonable prospect of meaningful recovery. Use a combination of criteria — triage exists to ensure optimal and equitable use of limited resources.

Admission policy

  1. The patient's attending clinician is responsible for requesting ICU admission.
  2. The ICU specialist decides on admission using clinical judgement guided by the admission criteria.
  3. The decision to admit or deny admission shall not be made at the level of a medical officer.
  4. Admissions from the Emergency & Trauma Department or another hospital shall have a primary unit.
  5. Admission from other hospitals must be discussed with the ICU specialist prior to transfer.
  6. The primary team continues resuscitation while awaiting ICU admission.
  7. Patients in ICU remain under the responsibility and ownership of the primary unit. Transfer of care is arranged by the primary unit.
  8. Family shall be informed of ICU admission as soon as possible, with further updates when required.

ICU admission criteria — factors to consider

  • Prioritisation according to severity of illness
  • Specific patient needs such as life-supportive therapies
  • Diagnosis
  • Prognosis
  • Potential benefit from interventions
  • Objective parameters at the time of referral
  • Available clinical expertise
  • Bed availability

Admission priority

Priority 1

  • Critically ill, unstable
  • Require life support for organ failure, intensive monitoring and therapies not available elsewhere (invasive ventilation, CRRT, invasive haemodynamic monitoring and interventions)
  • No limitations of treatment
  • High likelihood of benefit

Priority 2A

  • Acutely ill, relatively stable
  • Organ dysfunction requiring intensive monitoring and/or therapies manageable in an intermediate care facility (HDU or PACU)
  • Admit to ICU if early management fails to prevent deterioration, or if no intermediate care facility exists
  • Examples: post-operative patients requiring close monitoring; respiratory insufficiency on intermittent NIV

Priority 2B

  • Critically ill, unstable; require life support for organ failure
  • Significantly lower probability of recovery because of advanced underlying disease
  • May have specific limitations of care, e.g. no CPR
  • Lower likelihood of potential benefit
  • Examples: metastatic cancer in septic shock from hospital-acquired pneumonia with limitations of therapy (e.g. no CPR); decompensated heart failure with deteriorating functional status and multiple hospital admissions

Priority 3 — not appropriate for ICU admission

  • Terminally ill or moribund patients with no possibility of recovery
  • May benefit from palliative care rather than intensive care
  • Examples:
    • Severe irreversible brain pathology impairing cognition and consciousness, or persistent vegetative state
    • Metastatic cancer unresponsive to chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy
    • End-stage cardiac, respiratory or liver disease with no transplant option
    • Severe disability with poor quality of life
    • Advanced disease of a progressive life-limiting condition (e.g. motor neuron disease with rapid decline, severe Parkinson's disease with reduced independence)
    • Poor response to current treatment (e.g. bowel leak despite multiple laparotomies, recurrent soft tissue/musculoskeletal infections despite multiple surgical interventions, chronic conditions failing to respond such as SLE or HIV)
    • End-stage renal disease with no option or refusal for renal replacement therapy
    • Those who have explicitly stated their wish not to receive life-support therapy

Triage

Placing patients at their most appropriate level of care when demand exceeds ICU bed availability. Factors to consider:

  • Likelihood of benefit
  • Prognosis
  • Life expectancy due to disease
  • Anticipated quality of life

Discharge policy

  1. The decision to discharge is made by the ICU specialist.
  2. Prior to discharge:
    • The primary team shall be informed of the management plan including any limitation of treatment.
    • A discharge summary shall be completed.
    • Any limitation of treatment shall be clearly documented, including why and amongst whom these decisions were made.
    • Family shall be informed.
    • The primary team is responsible for receiving and reviewing patients promptly in the ward.
    • Patients requiring a higher level of nursing care may benefit from a step-down unit, if available.

ICU discharge criteria

Assess continuously to identify patients who no longer need ICU care:

  • Stable physiological status, no longer needing ICU monitoring and treatment
  • Stable haemodynamics on low-dose inotropic support
  • Stable respiratory status with oxygen requirement not more than 60%
  • Neurological stability
  • Tracheostomy, not requiring frequent suctioning
  • Chronic mechanical ventilation (e.g. motor neuron disease, cervical spine injury) with the acute critical problem resolved
  • Deteriorating or irreversible physiological status where active interventions are no longer beneficial — withdrawal of therapy should be initiated; patient may be discharged to the ward if an ICU bed is required
2 Vasoactive Agents in Acute Circulatory Failure

General principles

  1. Initiate vasopressors promptly in severe hypotension, especially undifferentiated shock, after excluding obstructive shock.
  2. It is appropriate to initiate vasoactive agents while ensuring optimal fluid resuscitation in severe hypotension.
  3. Titrate the dose to achieve adequate tissue perfusion.
  4. Aim for MAP 60–65 mmHg (or > 70 mmHg in chronic hypertension) if there is evidence of inadequate tissue perfusion.
  5. Main route is via a central venous catheter; peripheral administration may be considered in low doses.
  6. Invasive blood pressure monitoring is preferred — allows immediate recognition of change and precise titration.
  7. Monitor regularly for complications, e.g. arrhythmias and peripheral ischaemia.
  8. Echocardiography is useful in the diagnosis and management of shock.
  9. Consider advanced haemodynamic studies, e.g. cardiac output monitoring, in refractory shock.

Septic shock

  1. Predominantly caused by vasodilatation and relative hypovolaemia. Myocardial depression is often present although cardiac output is elevated.
  2. Noradrenaline is the vasoactive agent of choice — venoconstriction (↑preload), arterial vasoconstriction, positive inotropy, improved cardiac output and improved renal perfusion.
  3. Consider adding a second vasoactive agent (e.g. vasopressin or adrenaline) when noradrenaline > 15–20 mcg/min does not achieve targets.
  4. Consider adding IV hydrocortisone 50 mg q6h following a bolus of 100 mg when noradrenaline dose reaches 15–20 mcg/min.
  5. Consider adding IV dobutamine (2–5 mcg/kg/min) in the presence of myocardial depression, and if noradrenaline dose is < 15–20 mcg/min.
  6. Wean vasoactive agents once targets are optimised. Consider weaning noradrenaline first before vasopressin.

Anaphylactic shock

  1. Acute, multi-system disorder (predominantly heart, lung, vasculature) from sudden release of mediators from mast cells, basophils and macrophages into the circulation.
  2. Stop the suspected offending agent.
  3. Administer IV adrenaline in boluses of 0.05–0.1 mg. If no response, administer infusion at 0.1 mcg/kg/min. Adrenaline is preferred as it reverses peripheral vasodilatation and reduces oedema.
  4. Administer IV crystalloid 20 mL/kg rapidly. Avoid colloids.
  5. May consider H1 anti-histamine, IV chlorpheniramine 10 mg, to counter histamine-mediated vasodilatation and bronchoconstriction. No evidence for H2 anti-histamine.
  6. May consider IV hydrocortisone 200 mg stat to shorten a protracted reaction.

Neurogenic shock

  1. Secondary to spinal cord injury or disease — lack of sympathetic tone causing vasogenic and cardiogenic instability.
  2. Fluid resuscitation is required to restore intravascular volume.
  3. Noradrenaline is recommended as the initial agent for its alpha and beta activity.
  4. Add adrenaline if a second vasopressor is needed.

Cardiogenic shock

  1. Inadequate tissue perfusion due to myocardial depression or structural abnormality.
  2. Causes: left ventricular failure (most common), right ventricular failure, structural heart disease, arrhythmias.
  3. Early echocardiography is recommended for diagnosis and monitoring of therapy.
  4. Early cardiology consult if there is an indication for rescue revascularisation.
  5. Perform test for fluid responsiveness and correct hypovolaemia in those who are fluid responsive.
  6. The vasoactive agent of choice remains unclear — little evidence guides one agent over another.

Fluid responsiveness

  1. Defined as the ability of the left ventricle to increase stroke volume by 10–15% after rapid bolus fluid administration.
  2. Cardiac output or its indicators should be assessed accurately during the test.

  5. Passive leg raising (PLR) is the preferred method — it does not involve additional fluid administration.

3 Severe Hypoxaemic Respiratory Failure
Definition

Severe hypoxaemic respiratory failure is defined by PₐO₂/FᵢO₂ < 100–150 mmHg. The primary aim of ventilatory support is adequate gas exchange while minimising ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI).

Principles

  1. No outcome advantage of volume- vs pressure-controlled ventilation.
  2. Institute protective lung ventilation (PLV) as the initial strategy. Target tidal volume ≤ 6 mL/kg ideal body weight, plateau pressure (Pplat) ≤ 30 cmH₂O, and accept a lower PₐO₂.
  3. Lung recruitment manoeuvre is reserved for patients who show 'PEEP responsiveness'.
  4. Prolonged prone positioning (> 16 hours) should be considered in the early phase.
  5. Muscle paralysis with a neuromuscular blocking agent (NMBA) may be considered in the early phase of ventilation.
  6. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) may be considered as rescue therapy, based on clinician expertise, case by case.
  7. Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) should be avoided in patients with PₐO₂/FᵢO₂ < 200 — associated with a high failure rate.

Ventilatory strategy

  1. Adhere to protective lung ventilation strategy.
  2. Optimise mean alveolar pressure.
  3. Perform lung recruitment manoeuvre if applicable and select optimal PEEP.
  4. Prone positioning.
  5. Use neuromuscular blocking agent.

Initial ventilator settings

  1. Ideal body weight (IBW):
    Male = 50 + 0.91 [height (cm) − 152.4] kg
    Female = 45.5 + 0.91 [height (cm) − 152.4] kg
  2. Mode: pressure-controlled (PCV) or volume-controlled ventilation (VCV).
  3. Target tidal volume (Vt) 6 mL/kg IBW with Pplat ≤ 30 cmH₂O.
  4. Set initial PEEP at 10–15 cmH₂O.
  5. In PCV, aim for a driving pressure not more than 15 cmH₂O.
  6. In VCV:
    • Pplat should be measured every 4 hours in passively ventilated patients (no spontaneous breathing).
    • If Pplat > 30 cmH₂O, decrease Vt by 1 mL/kg until target achieved or a minimum 4 mL/kg Vt is reached.
  7. In patients with high respiratory drive resulting in Vt > 6 mL/kg and patient–ventilator dyssynchrony, consider muscle paralysis to prevent patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI).
  8. Use the lowest FᵢO₂ to achieve adequate oxygenation. Allow permissive hypoxaemia: accept PₐO₂ ≥ 55 mmHg or SpO₂ ≥ 88%.
  9. Accept permissive hypercapnia with pH > 7.1. Respiratory rate may be increased to a maximum of 35/min. Contraindications to permissive hypercapnia: intracranial hypertension, acute coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, right heart failure, worsening pulmonary hypertension.

Mean alveolar pressure

  1. Optimise mean alveolar pressure:
    • Increase inspiratory time (Tᵢ):
      • In VCV: decrease inspiratory flow rate or add inspiratory pause.
      • In PCV: Tᵢ is directly set in seconds or adjusted as I:E ratio.
      • May require deep sedation — potential to cause air trapping with haemodynamic consequences or barotrauma.
    • Optimise PEEP and inspiratory pressure.

Dynamic compliance

C_dyn = Vt delivered ÷ (P_peak − PEEP) — automatically measured in some ventilators.

Non-recruiters

Do not perform lung recruitment manoeuvre. Keep PEEP 10–15 cmH₂O. Consider prone positioning and muscle paralysis.

Recruitment manoeuvre in recruiters

  • Additional sedation, paralysis or both may be required. Monitor for hypotension. Transient desaturation may be expected.
  • Types of recruitment manoeuvre:
    • Sustained high pressure inflation — CPAP 30–50 cmH₂O for 20–40 s with zero pressure support.
    • Extended sigh — stepwise increase in PEEP and reduction in tidal volume over 2 minutes; used in VCV.
    • Staircase recruitment manoeuvre (SRM) — PCV with stepwise increase in PEEP every 2 min, keeping driving pressure constant, up to a peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) of 45–50 cmH₂O.

Staircase recruitment manoeuvre (SRM) protocol

  • PCV with driving pressure 15 cmH₂O, RR 10/min, I:E 1:1, FᵢO₂ 1.0.
  • Start with PEEP 20 cmH₂O.
  • Increase PEEP by 5 cmH₂O every 2 min until PIP 45–50 cmH₂O and PEEP 30–35 cmH₂O.
  • Alternative: stepwise increase in PEEP with return to baseline between each increase.

Determining optimum PEEP after recruitment

  1. Set PEEP at 20 cmH₂O and reduce stepwise (1 cmH₂O every 3 min) to achieve a maximal decrease in PₐO₂ or C_Rs.
  2. A PₐO₂ reduction of > 10% or a reduction in C_Rs indicates de-recruitment and collapse pressure.
  3. Repeat recruitment with sustained high pressure inflation (CPAP 30–50 cmH₂O for 20–40 s with zero pressure support).
  4. Optimal PEEP is at 2 cmH₂O above the collapse pressure.
Post-recruitment assessment

If PₐO₂/FᵢO₂ ≤ 150 mmHg, start deep sedation and prone ventilation. Consider NMBA.

Early prone positioning

  1. Severe hypoxaemic patients should be placed prone within 24 hours for at least 16 consecutive hours.
  2. Measure ABG and respiratory compliance 1 hour before turning supine and within 4 hours following supine position.
  3. Prone position is no longer required when there is sustained improvement of oxygenation, defined as PₐO₂/FᵢO₂ > 150 mmHg with FᵢO₂ < 0.6 and PEEP 10–15 cmH₂O at 4 hours upon turning supine.
  4. Patient may be placed prone again at any time before the 4-hour assessment if oxygenation criteria are not met.
  5. Complications: desaturation, hypotension, pressure ulcers, unplanned extubation, facial and airway oedema, catheter dislodgement.

Neuromuscular blocking agents

  1. Muscle paralysis may be considered in the early phase of severe hypoxaemia in selected patients, e.g. ventilatory dyssynchrony or prone position.
  2. Current evidence supports using cisatracurium besylate for not more than 48 hours.
  3. No consensus on depth of paralysis. Titrating to achieve 'Train of Four' count of 2/4 with a peripheral nerve stimulator may be reasonable.
  4. Concerns: neuropathy and the need for deep sedation.

Non-ventilatory strategy

  1. Conservative fluid management using fluid restriction and diuretics may improve oxygenation.
  2. The use of corticosteroids cannot be recommended with the current level of evidence.
  3. Consider echocardiography to assess right heart function:
    • Acute cor pulmonale in severe hypoxaemic failure may require right heart inotropic support and afterload reduction.
    • RV protective ventilation strategy: limit Pplat < 27 cmH₂O; limit driving P < 15 cmH₂O; target PₐCO₂ < 60 mmHg; decrease PEEP if RV dysfunction; prone positioning.
  4. Refractory hypoxaemia may be secondary to patent or reopened foramen ovale with cardiac shunt.

4 Weaning from Mechanical Ventilation
Definition

Weaning begins as soon as the underlying cause for mechanical ventilation has sufficiently improved, the level of ventilatory support is reduced, and transition to spontaneous breathing is initiated.

Principles

  1. Weaning begins as soon as the underlying cause has sufficiently improved.
  2. Benefits of early weaning should be weighed against risks associated with failed extubation.
  3. Weaning requires a sedation protocol aiming for light sedation and a rehabilitation protocol directed towards early mobilisation.
  4. Assess patients' readiness to wean on a daily basis.
  5. Perform SBT in patients who pass readiness to wean; assess for extubation in those who pass the SBT.
  6. Perform a cuff leak test in patients at high risk of post-extubation stridor.
  7. Initiate NIV weaning in selected patients at high risk of weaning failure.

Spontaneous breathing trial (SBT)

  1. SBT identifies patients likely to fail liberation from mechanical ventilation.
  2. Patient spontaneously breathes through the endotracheal tube for a set period, with or without minimal ventilatory support.
  3. Ideally the patient should be awake on minimal or no sedative infusion.
  4. Methods:
    • Without ventilatory support: T-piece trial — disconnect ETT from ventilator, provide humidified oxygen via T-piece.
    • With minimal ventilator support (additional support may overcome ETT resistance):
      • Low-level pressure support (5–8 cmH₂O) and PEEP (1–5 cmH₂O) — preferred
      • CPAP (5 cmH₂O) with zero pressure support
      • Automatic tube compensation (available in some ventilators)
  5. A duration of 30 min for an initial trial is usually sufficient. For patients who failed previous SBTs or on prolonged ventilation, longer trials up to 2 hours may be required.

Cuff leak test

Perform as a surrogate marker of laryngeal oedema in patients at high risk of post-extubation stridor:

  • Traumatic intubation
  • Duration of intubation ≥ 7 days
  • Excessively large ETT (≥ 7.5 mm in women, ≥ 8.5 mm in men)
  • Excessive ETT mobility (psychomotor agitation or ETT not anchored properly)
  • Reintubation after unplanned extubation

Technique:

  1. Use volume-controlled ventilation and record inspiratory tidal volume.
  2. Deflate cuff and average the cuff leak volume over 6 breaths.
  3. Calculate cuff leak volume (CLV) = difference between inspired tidal volume and average expired tidal volume. CLV < 110 mL or < 15–20% of delivered tidal volume indicates a failed cuff leak test.
Failed cuff leak test

Systemic steroids 4 hours prior to extubation reduce risk of post-extubation stridor and reintubation. Recommended: single dose IV methylprednisolone 40 mg. Alternative: IV dexamethasone 8 mg. A repeat cuff leak test is not required after steroid administration.

Difficult-to-wean

  1. Provide ventilator management that encourages spontaneous breathing while avoiding respiratory fatigue.
  2. Use pressure support (PS) as the weaning method — continuous gradual reduction of PS by 2 cmH₂O at a time, once or twice daily. Once PS is reduced to minimal level, repeat SBT daily.
  3. Strategies before resuming SBT:
    • Place patient in upright position for the SBT
    • Suction airway secretions
    • Optimise nutrition — avoid over- or underfeeding
    • Ensure adequate nocturnal rest with daytime respiratory muscle training
    • Aim for negative balance in patients with positive cumulative fluid balance
    • Consider longer SBT duration of up to 2 hours
  4. Identify and treat causes of weaning failure with detailed physical examination and investigations.
  5. Consider tracheostomy in patients unable to wean within 1–3 weeks.

NIV weaning

  1. NIV for weaning can only be successful in patients with good airway protection, strong cough and manageable secretions.
  2. Potential candidates at high risk for weaning failure:
    • COPD
    • Congestive heart failure
    • Elderly > 65 years
    • Hypercapnia during SBT
    • Failed > 1 SBT
  3. Start NIV immediately after extubation and maintain for at least 24 hours.
  4. Do not delay reintubation in patients who fail NIV.

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen

  • Consider HFNC oxygen in patients with post-extubation hypoxaemic respiratory failure, if available.

Prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV)

  1. Common consensus definition: requirement of ventilation > 21 days for at least 6 hours a day.
  2. These patients will require a tracheostomy.
  3. Identify factors that are potentially reversible.
  4. Perform increases in duration of SBT with frequent assessment for signs of failure. Suggested approach:
    • Assess for readiness to wean when PS has been gradually reduced to 10–12 cmH₂O.
    • Perform SBT using trachemask for a specified duration, e.g. 2 hours.
    • If SBT successful, perform daily SBT of progressively longer duration, e.g. 4 hours, 6 hours, etc.
    • Lengthen the SBT duration if the patient feels comfortable and wishes to continue at the end of the SBT.
    • Do not repeat the SBT for at least another 24 hours if SBT fails.
    • Eventual goal: reach 24 hours and be completely liberated from the ventilator.
  5. Patients requiring PMV should not be considered permanently ventilator-dependent until at least 3 months of weaning attempts have failed, unless respiratory failure is due to an irreversible process.

5 Pain, Sedation & Delirium

Principles

  1. Focus first on analgesia, then sedation.
  2. Practise analgesic-first sedation — use an analgesic (usually an opioid) prior to a sedative to reach the sedation goal.
  3. Aim for light sedation unless contraindicated.
  4. Titrate analgesic and sedative drugs to a defined target, using the lowest effective dose.
  5. Identify risk factors and implement effective preventive measures for delirium.
  6. Assess pain, sedation and delirium objectively using validated monitoring tools.
  7. Employ pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies to manage pain, agitation and delirium.

Pain

  1. Use validated scales: Behavioral Pain Score (BPS) or Critical Care Pain Observational Tool (CPOT) in the unconscious; Visual Analogue Score (VAS) in conscious patients.
  2. Assess pain at least 4 hourly.
  3. Institute pain management when pain score is: BPS ≥ 5 · CPOT ≥ 3 · VAS ≥ 3
  4. Opioid-based analgesia remains the mainstay.
  5. Consider adjuncts to reduce opioid dose and/or severity of pain: paracetamol (IV/PO/PR); IV ketamine in post-surgical patients.
  6. Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) can be provided for awake and cooperative patients.
  7. Use an analgesic prior to a procedure that may cause pain, with the lowest effective dose, timed so that peak effect coincides with the procedure.
  8. Use gabapentin or carbamazepine with opioids for neuropathic pain, e.g. Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  9. Consider regional analgesia in selected surgical or trauma patients, e.g. thoracic epidural in post-operative abdominal aortic aneurysm surgery or traumatic rib fractures.
  10. Non-pharmacological interventions may complement conventional approaches: music therapy, relaxation techniques, massage therapy.

  1. Diagnosed when there is alteration or fluctuation in mental status, inattention and disorganised thinking. Three forms: hypoactive (44%), hyperactive (2%), mixed (54%).

  3. Use validated screening tool — Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU). Perform objective assessment when RASS > −3 or revised Riker > −2.
  4. Non-pharmacological approaches to prevent delirium:
    • Correct physiological derangements
    • Avoid physical restraints
    • Perform early mobilisation
    • Protect sleep cycles — cluster care activities, decrease stimuli at night
    • Improve environmental conditions — orientation, noise reduction, adjustment of light
    • Use visual and hearing aids, if applicable
  5. Pharmacologic agents are not used to prevent or treat delirium, but to control symptoms in hyperactive delirium.

6 Nutritional Therapy

Key points

  1. Assess nutritional risk.
  2. Commence enteral nutrition within 24–48 hours upon ICU admission.
  3. Aim only for 70–80% of targeted calories within 72 hours.
  4. Aim to provide protein of at least 1.2 g/kg/day.
  5. Prescribe parenteral nutrition after 5–7 days if enteral nutrition is not feasible.
  6. Avoid overfeeding.
  7. Identify patients at risk of refeeding syndrome.

Nutritional risk

  1. Assess nutritional status clinically to identify malnutrition or risk of malnutrition.
  2. Considered at high risk for malnutrition:
    • ICU stay > 2 days and mechanically ventilated
    • Underfed for > 5 days
    • Pre-existing severe chronic disease

Enteral nutrition (EN)

  1. Initiate EN within 24–48 hours of ICU admission if the GI tract is functioning and the patient is adequately resuscitated.
  2. Delay EN in:
    • Shock with haemodynamic instability
    • Severe hypoxaemia and acidosis
    • Active upper GI bleeding
    • Acute bowel ischaemia
    • Abdominal compartment syndrome
    • Gastric residual volume > 500 mL/6 hours
  3. Aim to achieve only 70–80% of targeted calories in the first 72 hours of admission (early phase of acute illness).
  4. Provide EN via nasogastric or orogastric tube size 10–12 Fr after confirming correct placement by:
    • Chest radiograph
    • Aspiration of gastric contents
    • Measuring pH of aspirate using pH indicator strip (if available)
  5. Ensure head of bed elevated at least 30 degrees during feeding.
  6. Administration methods:
    • Continuous: hourly rate using a feeding pump for 24 hours
    • Intermittent: feeding pump over a few hours with rest period, e.g. feeding over 4 hours with 2 hours rest
    • Bolus: by gravity over 15 min every 3–4 hours
  7. Enteral formulation:
    • Use standard polymeric isocaloric or near-isocaloric of 1–1.5 kcal/mL
    • Consider diabetic-specific formula with low glycaemic index in diabetics
    • Consider a calorie-dense formula in patients with fluid restriction

Feeding intolerance

Defined as high gastric residual volume (GRV), abdominal distension, vomiting, diarrhoea, or reduced passage of stools.

A. GRV > 300 mL

  • Use a prokinetic agent:
    • IV erythromycin 125 mg q6h or 250 mg q12h, or
    • IV metoclopramide 10 mg q8h
  • Combination of both agents improves gastric tolerance and may be considered. Both are associated with QT prolongation.
  • Review the need for prokinetics after 48 hours.

B. Diarrhoea

  • Defined as > 2–3 liquid stools per day or > 250 g liquid stool per day.
  • Do not routinely interrupt feeding for diarrhoea.
  • Contributing factors: type/amount of fibre in formula · osmolality of formula · delivery mode · EN contamination · medications (antibiotics, PPI, prokinetics, laxatives) · Clostridium difficile infection · intra-abdominal collections following abdominal surgery
  • Management: review medications and dietary formula · send stools for C. difficile toxin assays · monitor serum electrolytes · rule out abdominal pathology
  • If diarrhoea persists, consider adding soluble fibre supplement into standard feeds or use small peptide semi-elemental formula.

Monitoring and special situations

  1. Monitor blood glucose: keep between 8–10 mmol/L. Start insulin if blood glucose > 10 mmol/L.
  2. Trophic feeding (10–20 mL/h or up to 500 kcal/day) should be considered in:
    • Resolving shock (minimal or decreasing vasopressors or inotropes)
    • Prone position
    • Intra-abdominal hypertension without abdominal compartment syndrome
  3. Fasting:
    • In intubated patients, do not withhold feeding for procedures in ICU or operating theatre or extubation.
    • Feeds should be aspirated prior to transport, procedures or extubation.
    • For non-intubated patients, follow the fasting protocol as per general anaesthesia.

Safe practice for enteral nutrition

  • Use sterile water for formula reconstitution and tube flushing.
  • Flush feeding tubes with at least 20–30 mL of water at the end of feeding.
  • Flush volume should be included in daily fluid intake.
  • Hang time duration:
    • Closed system (ready to hang): 24 hours or per manufacturer's recommendation
    • Sterile decanted formula: 8 hours
    • Powdered reconstituted formula: 4 hours
  • Change administration set every 24 hours.
  • Opened decanted formula must be refrigerated and discarded within 12 hours if not used.

Parenteral nutrition (PN)

  1. Indications:
    • As soon as possible in patients who are severely malnourished and EN is not feasible
    • After 5–7 days in patients not deemed malnourished on admission and EN is not feasible
    • Supplemental PN if > 60% of energy and protein requirement via EN is not met after 7–10 days
  2. Dosing:
    • Start with non-protein calories ≤ 20 kcal/kg/day with protein ≥ 1.2 g/kg/day. Increase gradually and aim to achieve target within 5–7 days.
    • Macronutrients per day: carbohydrate 2–5 g/kg · lipids 0.75–1.5 g/kg · proteins 1.2–2.0 g/kg
    • Carbohydrate-to-lipid ratio should be 60:40 or 70:30
    • Prescribe electrolytes based on daily serum levels
    • Add trace elements and vitamins
  3. Administration:
    • High osmolality via a dedicated lumen of a central line
    • Low osmolality (< 850 mOsmol/L) can be administered via peripheral line
    • Change administration set every 24 hours
  4. Monitoring:
    • 2-hourly blood glucose. Target 8–10 mmol/L
    • Daily serum potassium, phosphate, calcium and magnesium
    • Biweekly liver function test
    • Weekly serum triglycerides. Target < 4.5 mmol/L
  5. Discontinuation: discontinue PN when patient receives > 60% of targeted calories enterally.

7 Early Mobilisation

Key points

  1. Initiate early mobility within 24–48 hours of admission in the absence of contraindications.
  2. Assess appropriateness by weighing risks of adverse events against benefits.
  3. Step-up progression of mobilisation based on conscious level, functional capability and endurance.
  4. Implement early mobilisation in combination with pain, sedation and delirium management.

General

  1. Assess patients within 24–48 hours of ICU admission for early mobilisation.
  2. Very early (< 24 hours) and intensive out-of-bed mobilisation has been shown to be harmful in acute stroke patients.
  3. Contraindications to early mobility:
    • Cardiovascular instability: SBP < 90 mmHg, HR > 120/min, unstable cardiac rhythm, use of 2 or more vasoactive agents
    • Neurological instability: acute traumatic brain injury, acute intracranial bleed, unstable spinal cord injury, or any new neurological deterioration
    • Respiratory instability: FᵢO₂ > 0.6, PEEP > 10 cmH₂O, RR > 35/min
  4. Early mobilisation activities require a multidisciplinary team comprising clinicians, physiotherapists and nurses.
  5. Consult the ICU specialist when there is uncertainty about safety.
  6. Inform patients and families of the importance of early mobility.

Activities

  1. Level of activity and mobilisation should be guided by conscious state, strength, endurance and safety criteria.
  2. Passive and active activities:
    • Passive: movements performed by the physiotherapist, e.g. flexion and extension of limb joints
    • Active: patient assists using their own muscle strength
      • In-bed exercises (while sitting or lying in bed): rolling, bridging, upper limb weight training
      • Out-of-bed exercises: sitting at the edge of the bed, sitting out-of-bed, standing, marching on the spot, walking
  3. In all patients, unless contraindicated:
    • Alternate supine position with right and left lateral positions 2 hourly
    • Perform passive range of motion exercises

  3. Add more distilled water to the paste until liquid is formed.
  4. Add the sodium bicarbonate powder. Stir until sodium bicarbonate and pantoprazole completely dissolve.
  5. Make up the final volume with distilled water.

Key points

  1. Assess bleeding risk before initiating pharmacological prophylaxis.

10 Prevention & Control of Multi-Drug Resistant Organisms
Introduction

Early identification of patients colonised or infected with multi-drug resistant organisms (MDRO) is important to minimise the risk of transmission. MDROs covered: MRSA, VRE, and multi-drug resistant gram-negative organisms (MDR Acinetobacter baumannii, MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa, ESBL, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae [CRE]).

Principles

  1. Identify patients at risk.
  2. Adhere to hand hygiene.
  3. Use personal protective equipment.
  4. Place in a single room or cohort for suspected or confirmed cases.
  5. Intensify cleaning and disinfection of environment and equipment.
  6. Comply with antimicrobial stewardship.
  7. Provide education and training updates to healthcare professionals.

Risk factors

MRSAVREMDR gram-negative bacilli including resistant Enterobacteriaceae
Previous MRSA infection or colonisation · frequent hospital admissions · recent broad-spectrum antibiotic (carbapenem, quinolones, 3rd/4th generation cephalosporins) · recent admission in a hospital with high prevalence of MRSA · from long-term care facility · areas where community-acquired MRSA is prevalent · chronic disease and impaired functional status · from high-risk units (ICU/nephrology/haematology/solid-organ transplant unit) Contact with patient colonised or infected · on dialysis · prolonged or broad-spectrum antibiotic use, specifically vancomycin · with invasive devices · on urinary catheters · chronic disease and impaired functional status · from high-risk units (ICU/burns/neurosurgery/solid-organ transplant unit) Contact with patient colonised or infected · prolonged hospital stay · with invasive devices · with chronic wounds · from high-risk units (ICU/HDU/burns/spinal unit)

Hand hygiene

  • Hand hygiene is the most important measure in preventing transmission of microorganisms.
  • Use either 70% alcohol-based hand rub or antiseptic soap and water.
  • Use soap and water if hands are visibly dirty or soiled with blood or other body fluids.
  • Remove jewellery, watch or rings with ridges or stones prior to hand hygiene.
  • Observe the 5 moments of hand hygiene:
    1. Before touching a patient
    2. Before any procedure
    3. After procedure or body fluid exposure risk
    4. After touching a patient
    5. After touching a patient's surroundings
  • Hand hygiene should always be performed:
    • Before donning and after removal of gloves
    • Before and after using computer keyboard in a clinical area
    • After leaving patient-care area including isolation rooms during an outbreak
  • Reinforce the importance of hand hygiene in an outbreak setting.
  • Audit compliance and provide immediate feedback to healthcare personnel.

Personal protective equipment (PPE)

PPE includes aprons, gowns, gloves, surgical mask and eye shields used alone or in combination to protect healthcare personnel (HCP) from contact with transmissible organisms. Selection is based on the extent of patient contact. Perform hand hygiene before donning PPE.

PPESpecificationIndication
Plastic apron Non-sterile, single use and disposable During care activities involving minimal patient contact where there is low risk of contaminating the HCP's arms, e.g. examining patient, airway suctioning

Isolation

  • Isolate patients colonised or infected with MDRO in single rooms.
  • Cohort patients if single rooms are not available. Cohorting = placing patients infected or colonised with the same organism in the same area.
  • Prioritise single room isolation to patients with:
    • CRE, VRE or MRSA
    • Incontinence of faeces or urine
    • Open or draining wounds
    • Enterostomies
    • Copious respiratory secretions
  • Staff nursing patients with MDRO should not have contact with other patients.
  • Appropriate signage should be placed outside the door to alert HCP of contact precautions.

Discontinuation of isolation precautions

  • Shedding of MDRO may be intermittent and their presence may not always be detected by active surveillance culture.
  • Continue contact precautions for the duration of admission.

Equipment and patient care items

  • Remove non-essential equipment from the room.
  • Minimise consumable items placed in the room, e.g. syringes, needles and gauze.
  • Use disposable items whenever possible.
  • Dedicate reusable equipment to a single patient, e.g. thermometer, blood pressure cuff, infusion pumps, stethoscope.
  • Clean and disinfect equipment shared between patients, e.g. hoist and armchairs.
  • Use a dedicated trolley for sponging and cleaning.

Environmental cleaning

  • Increase cleaning frequency.
  • Clean and disinfect frequently touched surfaces (bedrails, drip stands, nursing tables, trolleys, doorknobs, tap handles, switches) at least once every nursing shift. This involves either:
    • 2-in-1 clean: commercially prepared combination of detergent and 1000 ppm chlorine solution or impregnated wipes
    • 2-step clean: clean with detergent or detergent-impregnated wipes, then disinfect with 1000 ppm chlorine solution or impregnated wipes
  • Perform terminal cleaning after patient discharge:
    • Terminal cleaning process should be monitored and supervised
    • Remove curtains prior to cleaning
    • Cleaning should include walls, bed, frequently touched surfaces and all equipment
    • Discard unused consumables that cannot be disinfected

Antimicrobial stewardship

Coordinated interventions to ensure appropriate use of antimicrobials. When antimicrobials are prescribed ensure:

  • Adequate dose and the shortest duration for efficacy.
  • Use the narrowest spectrum whenever possible.
  • De-escalate when cultures are available.

Education

  • Education of all staff, including cleaning staff, should be intensified.
  • Reinforce standard and contact precautions.

Screening and surveillance

  • Contact screening is done to reduce the risk of transmission of MDRO:
    • Screen patients in close contact for more than 24 hours with a CRE or VRE confirmed case. Send rectal swab.
    • Screening for other MDRO depends on local hospital infection control policy.
    • Isolate or cohort contacts pending screening result.
  • Active surveillance cultures should be considered in outbreak settings:
    • Screen for CRE, VRE or MRSA in high-risk patients admitted to the unit. Send rectal swabs for CRE or VRE and nasal swabs for MRSA.
    • Screening for other MDRO depends on local hospital infection control policy.

Other considerations

  • Support staff (radiographer, physiotherapist, pharmacist, dialysis nurse, dietitian): inform of patient's status. Support staff to attend to the patient last in the unit except in emergencies.
  • Visitors: all visitors are directed to perform hand hygiene before and after contact with patient or environment. Visitors are not required to wear PPE unless involved in patient care.
  • Patient's movement: keep to a minimum. If transportation is required (radiology, operating theatre, another facility), the receiving unit should be informed of the patient's status.
  • Upon discharge: receiving wards should be informed early, for isolation or cohorting purposes.
11 Withholding & Withdrawing Life-Sustaining Treatment

Key principles

  1. Abide by the principles of medical ethics when making end-of-life decisions.
  2. Assess patient's decision-making capacity on end-of-life decisions. Patients with limited or absent capacity: families become surrogate decision-makers.
  3. Respect patient's autonomy except in cases of non-beneficial medical treatment.
  4. Implement a palliative care plan once withholding or withdrawing LST is decided upon.
  5. Document clearly all decisions on withholding or withdrawing LST in the clinical records.
  6. Treat dying patients with respect, dignity and compassion.

Ethical principles

  1. Principles that underpin end-of-life (EOL) medical decisions:
    • Respect for autonomy: acting in accordance with what the patient wants
    • Beneficence: acting to benefit the patient
    • Non-maleficence: causing no harm to the patient
    • Distributive justice: paying attention to fairness and equity
  2. These principles are used individually or collectively to frame the discussion on EOL; one does not supersede the other.
  3. The principles may conflict, for example:
    • Between respect for autonomy and beneficence: a terminal cancer patient who insists all LST be provided although it will not benefit him.
    • Between beneficence and justice: providing invasive mechanical ventilation in a patient with decompensated heart failure who has had multiple hospital admissions in the last 6 months and is dyspnoeic at rest, versus allocation of an ICU bed for a polytrauma patient.

Decision-making capacity

  1. Assess patient's decision-making capacity before discussing EOL. Includes ability to comprehend, appreciate, rationalise and express choice of treatment.
  2. Most ICU patients do not have decision-making capacity; families become the surrogate decision-maker.
  3. Standards used by surrogates:
    • Substituted judgement: decision of the patient if he/she had capacity
    • Best interest: decision based on potential benefits and burdens of treatment vs. patient's values and beliefs
  4. EOL decisions are shared medical decisions made by clinicians and concurred by family members.

Autonomy and obligation to treat

  1. Once decision-making capacity is established, patient's autonomy must be respected even though survival may be implicated.
  2. In cases of non-beneficial medical treatment, clinicians are not obliged to initiate or continue LST.
  3. The MMA Code of Ethics 2001 states: "where death is deemed to be imminent and where curative or life-prolonging treatment appears to be futile, ensure that death occurs with dignity and comfort. Such futile therapy could be withheld, withdrawn or one may allow irreversible pathology to continue without active resuscitation. One should always take into consideration any advance directives and the wishes of the family in this regard. In any circumstance, if therapy is considered to be life saving, it should never be withheld."

Care of the dying patient

  • All dying patients should be afforded the same standard of care as other patients.
  • They should be treated with dignity, respect and compassion.
  • Their privacy and confidentiality should be respected at all times.

Practical issues

1. Medical team consensus

The intensive care team and the primary team should agree on EOL decisions.

2. Communication with patient and relatives

  • It is best that the same clinician (specialist/consultant) involved in the active care of the patient deals with the family. He/she should be someone who frequently communicates with the family and has established a rapport. A witness (nurse or doctor) should be present during these discussions.
  • Respect that each patient and family will differ in how much input they wish to have in the decision-making process.
  • In the event of disagreement, allow time for repeated discussions and negotiations. Failing this, consider:
    • Time-limited trial (TLT): a goal-directed trial of any intervention limited by predetermined outcomes evaluated at planned intervals
    • Second medical opinion from another clinician from a similar specialty
    • Facilitation by a third party, e.g. spiritual advisor
  • Patients and families must be given sufficient time to reach decisions on EOL.

3. Management plan for withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment

A clear management plan is essential. It should be conveyed to the family, with emphasis on maintenance of comfort. Components:

  • Maintain current support until the patient and family have had adequate time together.
  • Ensure other healthcare professionals (primary team, physiotherapist, dietitian) are aware of the withdrawal plan. Cease all investigations, e.g. blood taking and X-rays.
  • Ensure pain and other symptoms (e.g. dyspnoea) are well controlled. Morphine is the most commonly used opioid for analgesia and comfort. There is no maximum dose. Large doses of opioids may be required for comfort and may unintentionally hasten death. This "double effect" of opioids is acceptable.
  • Manage airway secretions using glycopyrrolate, positioning in lateral position, and frequent suctioning.
  • Discontinue all treatment that does not contribute towards comfort, e.g. antibiotics, blood transfusions. Feeding and intravenous fluids may be discontinued unless specifically requested by family.
  • Maintain patient's personal hygiene and dignity at all times, e.g. diaper soiling is dealt with immediately.
  • Withdrawal of vasopressors may result in immediate death. Families should be aware and nearby.
  • Withdrawal of mechanical ventilation may be carried out either as:
    • Terminal weaning: ventilator settings are reduced while leaving the endotracheal tube in situ
    • Terminal extubation
  • Disable all monitor and ventilator alarms. Demedicalise the patient and allow family members to be close by.

4. Other considerations

  • Family should be given unrestricted access to the patient.
  • Non-invasive ventilation may be used as a palliative care technique to minimise dyspnoea in conscious patients.
  • Neuromuscular blockade makes assessment of comfort impossible and should not be used in intubated patients.
  • Consider terminal sedation with benzodiazepines only when high doses of opioid are inadequate for comfort.

5. Documentation

Document all decisions regarding withdrawal and withholding of treatment, including the basis of the decision and amongst whom it was reached.

6. Notification of death

Death should be communicated in direct language, gently.

7. Bereavement

Provide bereavement support to the family and healthcare providers if necessary.

12 Invasive Ventilation in Non-Critical Care Areas
Introduction

Invasive mechanical ventilation in wards should be discouraged. However, some patients are ventilated in the general ward due to limited resources. Clinical considerations and principles of medical ethics should guide decisions on ventilating patients in the ward.

Principles

  1. Interdisciplinary discussion is essential when a decision is made to ventilate patients in the ward.
  2. The decision to ventilate patients in non-critical care areas should be reached on the basis of clinical and ethical considerations.
  3. Clinical considerations are based on the patient's probable clinical outcome, premorbid status and burden of treatment.
  4. Level of monitoring and care of these patients should be provided accordingly.

Category 1

  • Make every effort to admit them to ICU.
  • Admit them to other critical care areas within the hospital while awaiting ICU bed availability.
  • Arrange for inter-hospital transfer to another ICU if a critical care bed is unavailable within the hospital. The arrangement and transport should preferably be done by the ICU team.
  • A Category 1 patient should be given priority for ICU admission over an elective surgical patient.

Category 2

  • Specialists from the ICU team and primary unit should discuss the benefits vs. burdens and risks of ventilation prior to intubation, including the possibility of limitation of care at 24–48 hours if no improvement is observed.
  • Involve families in decision-making and inform them of the treatment plan.
  • If the joint decision is to intubate and ventilate:
    • Admit to ICU if bed is available
    • If bed not available, assess progress at 24–48 hours and consider ICU admission if there is clinical improvement
    • If patient is ventilated in the ward, manage as per Category 3 patient if there is deterioration
  • Continue care of the patient in the ward if the joint decision is not to ventilate.
  • Document the decision clearly in the case notes.
  • Seek a second medical opinion to resolve differences if conflicts arise among the teams.

  • Consider withholding or withdrawal of therapy while continuing comfort care.

Category 4

  • If the patient deteriorates, re-assess the indication for ICU admission and manage accordingly.

Care in the ward

  • The primary team is responsible for the care of the patient in the ward.
  • The ICU team should review the patient daily.

Minimum requirements — staffing

Nurses should be trained and privileged on basic care and safety issues in caring for patients on mechanical ventilation, e.g.:

  • Tracheal suctioning
  • Bag-valve-mask ventilation
  • Nebulisation
  • Recognition of life-threatening events, e.g. desaturation, ETT blockage, disconnection or dislodgement of ETT
  • Infection control measures

Minimum requirements — monitoring

  • Blood pressure, pulse rate and respiratory rate hourly
  • SpO₂ — continuous if available
  • ECG — continuous if available
  • Level of consciousness
  • Arterial blood gases as indicated
  • Other standard nursing monitoring

Minimum requirements — equipment (readily available)

  • Suction catheters
  • Vacuum suction apparatus
  • Laryngoscopes
  • Endotracheal tubes and tapes
  • Oral airways
  • Bag-valve-masks
  • Resuscitation trolley

Procedures of care

  • Elevate head of bed 30 degrees unless contraindicated
  • Ensure endotracheal or tracheostomy and nasogastric tubes are firmly secured
  • Perform tracheal and oropharyngeal suctioning q4h or more often if necessary
