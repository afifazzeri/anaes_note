Maternal Cardiac Arrest

Quick reference for anaesthetists & intensivists · CPR · left uterine displacement · resuscitative hysterotomy

—
1 · At a glance — epidemiology & outcomes
  • Maternal cardiac arrest is rare: 1 in 12,000–36,000 women per year in the developed world.
  • Maternal mortality (2015): UK 9/100,000; USA 14/100,000. Low-income countries (WHO 2017): 462/100,000 live births.
  • UK survival after basic life support (2011–2014): 58%.
  • ~25% of UK maternal cardiac arrests were anaesthesia-related (tracheal intubation problems, cardiovascular collapse after epidural top-up, total spinal) — obesity complicated most of these.
  • USA predominant causes: haemorrhage, heart failure, amniotic fluid embolism, sepsis.
  • Obstetric issues associated with arrest: stillbirth, caesarean delivery, severe pre-eclampsia, placenta praevia.
  • Racial disparity: mortality approximately 3× higher in Black than White women (UK and USA).
  • Survival is better when arrest is anaesthesia-related (hypoxia, cardiac causes, anaphylaxis, drug toxicity) and worse with hypovolaemia, VTE, sepsis, amniotic fluid embolism, intracerebral bleed, aortic dissection, asthma, pulmonary artery rupture.
Golden rule: prompt, coordinated multidisciplinary response — advanced cardiac life support + left uterine displacement + resuscitative hysterotomy + treatment of the underlying cause.
2 · The first 5 minutes — 9 steps (quick reference card)
1

Confirm arrest & call for help

Declare “Obstetric cardiac arrest”. Team for mother and team for neonate if ≥20 weeks: obstetrician with scalpel, obstetric & cardiac anaesthetists, neonatologist, adult cardiac arrest team, ECMO team if available, TOE/TTE.

2

Position & uterine displacement

Lie flat, apply manual left uterine displacement; or left lateral tilt 15–30° (head to toe) on a firm surface.

3

Commence CPR

Standard CPR ratios and hand position apply. Request cardiac arrest trolley. Evaluate potential causes (4H’s & 4T’s — see section 7).

4

Team leader & roles

Identify team leader, allocate roles including scribe. Note the time.

5

Rhythm & defibrillation

Apply pads, check rhythm. Defibrillation is safe in pregnancy at standard energies. VF/pVT → shock; adrenaline & amiodarone after 3rd shock. PEA/asystole → resume CPR, adrenaline immediately. Check rhythm & pulse every 2 min; adrenaline every 3–5 min.

6

Airway & ventilation

100% O₂ via bag-valve-mask. Supraglottic airway with drain port, or tracheal tube if trained (intubation may be difficult). Waveform capnography — if expired CO₂ is absent, presume oesophageal intubation until excluded.

7

Circulation & access

IV access above the diaphragm; if it fails or is impossible, use upper limb intraosseous (IO). Drugs per section 8. Consider extracorporeal CPR (ECPR) if available.

8

Emergency hysterotomy

Perimortem caesarean section if ≥20 weeks. Perform immediately with fatal maternal injuries or prolonged pre-hospital arrest; by 5 minutes if no return of spontaneous circulation.

9

Post-ROSC / haemorrhage

Activate Massive Haemorrhage Protocol; consider uterotonics, fibrinogen, tranexamic acid, uterine tamponade/sutures, aortic compression, hysterectomy.

3 · Chest compressions
  • Hard and fast, on a firm flat surface: depth ≥5 cm at a rate of 100 min⁻¹.
  • Use a backboard if resuscitating on a typical hospital mattress.
  • Optimal hand position is the lower sternum. There is no scientific evidence to support a higher hand position, despite superior/anterolateral myocardial displacement by the gravid uterus.
  • Where available, transoesophageal echocardiography can confirm adequate compression location.
  • Swap/change providers every 2 minutes.
4 · Left uterine displacement (LUD)
  • From 20 weeks’ gestation the gravid uterus obstructs the vena cava in the supine position → reduced venous return and impaired resuscitation.
  • Assume ≥20 weeks if the fundus reaches or exceeds the umbilicus.
  • Manual LUD is preferred over left lateral tilt — it is more difficult to create the appropriate compression force vector with tilt.
  • From the patient’s left side: use two hands to pull the uterus upward and to the left.
  • From the patient’s right side: push the uterus up and to the left.
  • OAA alternative: lie flat with manual LUD, or left lateral tilt 15–30° (head to toe) on a firm surface.
5 · Airway & ventilation
  • ↓ Functional residual capacity plus ↑ oxygen consumption by the fetus → rapid hypoxaemia.
  • Vascular engorgement causes oedema of the oro- and nasopharynx, larynx and trachea → difficult intubation; use smaller tracheal tubes.
  • Videolaryngoscopy is recommended for the first intubation attempt, if possible.
  • Confirm tracheal intubation with a capnometer.
  • Use nasopharyngeal airways with caution (increased bleeding risk).
  • Ventilate with 100% oxygen, tidal volumes 500–700 mL at 10 bpm.
  • Bag-mask ventilation until endotracheal intubation; place a laryngeal mask airway in the event of failed intubation.
  • End-tidal CO₂ >1.3 kPa (10 mmHg) suggests quality chest compressions. Absent EtCO₂ → presume oesophageal intubation until absolutely excluded.
6 · Intravenous access, drugs & defibrillation

Access

  • Large-bore IV access above the diaphragm — uterine compression and bleeding may prevent adequate resuscitation from below.
  • Ultrasound-guided venous access or intraosseous access in the proximal humerus if access is difficult.
  • Rapid intravascular volume repletion; fluid warmer; massive transfusion blood products as needed; send laboratory tests as indicated.

Drugs

  • Physiological anaemia of pregnancy: circulating volume increases out of proportion to haemoglobin; ↑ volume and ↓ protein binding may mildly alter volume of distribution, but changes are negligible in the low-flow state of arrest.
  • Give resuscitation drugs according to standard guidelines at usual doses — none are considered contraindicated.
  • Stop oxytocin (vasodilatory and negative inotropic effects).
  • Stop neuraxial local anaesthetic infusions.
  • Stop magnesium if it is contributing to significant hypotension.

Defibrillation

  • Perform for a shockable rhythm — safe for the fetus during maternal cardiac arrest.
  • No change to energy requirements; thoracic impedance is unchanged in pregnancy.
  • The theoretical risk of burns from fetal external/internal monitors should not delay defibrillation while waiting to disconnect or remove them.
  • Resume compressions immediately after defibrillation.

10 · Resuscitative hysterotomy (perimortem caesarean delivery)
  • Timing matters: arrest-to-hysterotomy <5 min vs >5 min → maternal survival 61% vs 35%; neonatal survival 96% vs 70%.
  • Site matters: maternal survival 72% vs 36% when performed at the site of arrest rather than after transfer to the operating theatre.
  • Perform by 5 minutes if no return of spontaneous circulation at 4 minutes, in patients ≥20 weeks’ gestation.
  • Perform immediately if there are fatal maternal injuries or a prolonged pre-hospital arrest.
  • Physiology: removing the fetus reduces oxygen consumption and removes the anatomical barrier to vena caval flow and optimal pulmonary mechanics.
  • Equipment for caesarean delivery — especially a scalpel — must be immediately available.
  • After delivery: further surgery may be required to repair or close the hysterotomy; ensure haemostasis, antibiotics and intensive care protocols.
Do not transfer the patient to the operating room for hysterotomy — transfer causes unnecessary delays and interruption in care.
11 · Transoesophageal echocardiography & ECMO-CPR

Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE)

  • Where equipment and expertise are available, TOE may identify the cause of arrest and assess the efficacy of resuscitation.
  • Helps identify gross pathology: spontaneous coronary artery dissection with wall motion abnormalities, aortic dissection, pericardial and pleural effusions, saddle pulmonary embolism, ventricular thrombus.
  • May aid placement of cannulae for ECMO.
  • Can visualise compression vector and return of myocardial function.
  • Transthoracic or subcostal images are limited during resuscitation.

ECMO / ECPR

  • Used to stabilise critically ill obstetric patients and as rescue therapy in maternal cardiac arrest attributable to cardiopulmonary causes.
  • Few absolute contraindications in obstetric patients, but caution with massive haemorrhage or DIC — extracorporeal circulation may activate coagulation factors and worsen thrombosis or coagulopathy.
  • Obstetric patients at high risk of cardiopulmonary decompensation should be managed at hospitals with ECMO capabilities.
12 · Return of spontaneous circulation & post-resuscitation care
  • Usual post-resuscitation care once ROSC is achieved.
  • Patients may require further surgery to repair or close the resuscitative hysterotomy.
  • Follow appropriate haemostasis, antibiotics and intensive care protocols, including hypothermia where appropriate — but note that maternal hypothermia may worsen coagulopathy.
  • Patients with return of awareness require appropriate analgesia and sedation.
  • Haemorrhage: activate Massive Haemorrhage Protocol; consider uterotonic drugs, fibrinogen and tranexamic acid; uterine tamponade/sutures, aortic compression, hysterectomy.
13 · Team preparedness & human factors
  • Maternal cardiac arrest is a high-stakes / low-frequency event: response plans and plans to mobilise responders should be regularly reviewed by obstetricians, anaesthesiologists, neonatologists and labour & delivery nurses, with didactic and simulation sessions.
  • In-situ drills reduce the time to initiation of CPR and resuscitative hysterotomy in simulated settings.
  • Strong leadership and teamwork are essential.
  • Use closed-loop communication: acknowledge tasks assigned by the leader, announce when tasks are completed, and feel empowered to make suggestions.
  • Once enough support arrives, the code leader should move to a position of oversight — ensure completion of tasks, identify the differential diagnosis and direct appropriate treatment.
Educational quick reference only. Not a substitute for local protocols, current resuscitation guidelines or clinical judgement. Always follow your institution’s maternal cardiac arrest / major haemorrhage policies.
