★ Key points at a glance
  • Acute kidney injury (AKI) is common in critical care, and a significant fraction of patients require renal replacement therapy (RRT). (2)
  • There are multiple indications for RRT, but there is limited guidance on the optimal initiation time in critically ill patients. (2)
  • Good vascular access and adequate anticoagulation are required to minimise circuit clotting and downtime. (2)
  • Regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) is now considered the first-line strategy for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) anticoagulation in patients with no contraindications. (2)
  • Despite improvements in RRT technology, mortality associated with AKI remains high, and there is no benefit from high-intensity RRT as part of routine care. (1)
Bottom line for the anaesthetist: start RRT for the classical indications, target 20–25 ml kg−1 h−1 effluent dose, minimise filter downtime, and remember the circuit changes your drug handling. (1)(2)

01 AKI — definition & epidemiology

Definition

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined as an abrupt reduction in kidney function, encompassing structural damage and loss of function. It is characterised by a rapid (hours to days) reduction in the ability of the kidneys to eliminate small to middle molecular weight solutes (e.g. nitrogen, urea, creatinine). These solutes can be measured to assess the extent of functional loss, but they often do not reflect dynamic changes in glomerular filtration rate. (2)

KDIGO criteria — AKI is defined by any of

Table 1. KDIGO diagnostic criteria for AKI (2)
#Criterion
1An increase in serum creatinine of ≥ 26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL)
2An increase in serum creatinine ≥ 1.5 × baseline (occurring within the prior 7 days)
3A urine volume of < 0.5 mL/kg/hr for 6 hours

Epidemiology

1% of US hospital admissions 5–7% of hospitalised patients Up to 60% of ICU patients Up to ⅔ need RRT
  • AKI is estimated to account for 1% of hospital admissions in the United States, and to develop in 5–7% of hospitalised patients. (2)
  • Up to 60% of intensive care patients are affected by AKI, of whom up to two-thirds require RRT. (2)
  • AKI is associated with decreased overall survival; even mild, reversible AKI can lead to increased mortality and long-term dialysis. (2)
  • Within the adult critical care population in the UK, continuous modes of RRT are generally preferred, although intermittent and hybrid therapies remain in use. (1)
  • No current specific pharmacological therapy is effective in treating AKI; care is limited to supportive treatment, including RRT. (2)
  • AKI often has a mixed aetiology — coexisting sepsis, ischaemia and nephrotoxicity can complicate recognition and treatment. (2)

Why incidence is probably rising

  • Increasing age and comorbidity of the hospitalised population.
  • Increasing prevalence of AKI risk factors such as chronic kidney disease and diabetes.
  • More widespread use of i.v. contrast for cardiovascular and other radiological procedures. (1)

Why mortality has not fallen faster

  • Lack of diagnostic tools to detect early AKI.
  • Delayed initiation of RRT.
  • Inadequate delivery of RRT.
  • Inability to fully replace kidney function with current RRT modalities. (1)

04 Convection vs diffusion — how clearance happens

The aims of RRT

Correction of electrolyte abnormalities Solute and water removal Normalisation of acid–base disturbances

These aims are achieved via diffusion (haemodialysis) or convection (haemofiltration). (1)(2)

Haemofiltration (convection)

  • A convective process whereby a hydrostatic pressure gradient filters plasma, water and solute across a membrane — analogous to the process within the renal corpuscle. (1)
  • The underlying mechanism is ‘solute drag’: appropriately sized molecules are pulled along with the mass movement of solvent, traditionally termed ultrafiltration (UF). (1)
  • Convective transport is independent of solute concentration but determined by the direction and magnitude of the transmembrane pressure (TMP). (1)
  • Measures that result in a higher flow rate increase UF production and in turn increase solute clearance. Measures that increase the negative pressure across the membrane (including the pump on the effluent line) can have a marked effect. (1)
  • The fluid produced, known as effluent, is discarded. Owing to the high volumes produced, the patient’s circulating volume is replaced with a balanced crystalloid buffer solution. (1)
  • Convection enhances removal of low and middle molecular weight molecules. (2)

Haemodialysis (diffusion)

  • Solute clearance is achieved by diffusion across the membrane. A concentration gradient between blood and dialysate drives solutes across the semipermeable membrane. (1)(2)
  • The space outside the blood-containing fibres within the filter is filled with dialysate, pumped in a counter-current fashion to the flow of blood. (1)
  • Dialysate is reconstituted to include a buffer (acetic acid or bicarbonate) and essential electrolytes dissolved in ultrapure water rendered devoid of toxins and impurities. (1)
  • Diffusion is most suitable for clearing low molecular weight solutes, e.g. urea and creatinine. (2)
  • Whether tailoring the dose of bicarbonate to match individual patient needs can affect outcome requires further research. (1)

05 The CRRT circuit, filter & membrane

Circuit components

  • An extracorporeal circuit is used during RRT. Blood is passed through the circuit from one lumen of a wide-bore double-lumen central venous catheter (Vascath) and flows towards a semipermeable membrane consisting of multiple hollow fibres with pores throughout. (1)(2)
  • These pores only allow substances with certain molecular sizes to pass through — waste products pass through while cells and blood proteins remain intravascularly. Once the blood has passed through the membrane, it is returned to the patient via the second lumen of the same Vascath. (1)(2)
  • Roller pumps control the speed of blood flow towards the membrane. (1)
  • Middle molecules are preferentially cleared by convective methods; smaller molecules are more reliably cleared by diffusion — this potentially allows individualisation of treatment modality. (1)
  • Anticoagulation is generally required to minimise the chance of thrombosis within the extracorporeal circuit. (1)
  • Modern circuits use polysulphone membranes. (1)

06 CRRT modalities in detail — CVVH, CVVHD, CVVHDF, SCUF

Continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (CVVH)

  • In CVVH, convection is used to remove solutes through large-volume fluid ultrafiltration. Solutes are swept across the membrane with the fluid, without any influence from concentration gradients. (2)
  • Small solute molecules (e.g. urea) and middle-sized molecules (e.g. inflammatory cytokines) pass through the porous membrane. Large volumes of fluid are removed, so replacement fluid is required to maintain intravascular volume. (2)
  • This method can be used for volume removal and correction of electrolyte/acid–base abnormalities. (2)
Table 5. Pre-dilution vs post-dilution replacement fluid (2)
StrategyAdvantagesDisadvantages
Pre-dilution (before hemofilter) Increased filter life; enhanced elution of urea from red blood cells Decreased rate of solute clearance
Post-dilution (after hemofilter) Smaller volume of replacement fluid required; higher solute clearance rate Filter life may be decreased, particularly if anticoagulation is inadequate
In CVVH, an ultrafiltration rate of 2 L/hour leads to a urea clearance of approximately 25 mL/kg/hr in an average 80 kg patient. (2)

Continuous veno-venous hemodialysis (CVVHD)

  • CVVHD utilises counter-current dialysate flow to remove small solutes through diffusion based on their concentration gradients. (2)
  • Higher blood or dialysate flow rates can be used to increase solute clearance. (2)
  • Dialysates contain physiologic concentrations of magnesium, sodium, chloride and glucose. Potassium is prescribed separately, as serum potassium can vary significantly in critically ill patients. (2)
  • Dialysate is buffered with either bicarbonate or a bicarbonate precursor (e.g. lactate or citrate). (2)
  • In CVVHD there is no significant fluid removal, as there is minimal ultrafiltration. (2)

Continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF)

  • A combination of hemodialysis and hemofiltration. It therefore clears a large range of solutes and volume by optimally combining diffusion, convection and ultrafiltration. (2)

Slow continuous ultrafiltration (SCUF)

  • An alternative mode of CRRT used to control fluid balance — essentially CVVH with a low filtration rate. (2)
  • Used mainly for fluid balance but has limited solute removal. The hydrostatic pressure gradient between the blood compartment of the filter and the filtrate compartment determines the rate of fluid removal. (2)
  • Less complex than other modes with a decreased nursing workload. However, it cannot correct acid–base or electrolyte abnormalities. (2)
  • Should be considered in conditions with isolated volume overload, e.g. heart or liver failure, malnutrition. (1)(2)

07 Intermittent haemodialysis & peritoneal dialysis

Intermittent haemodialysis (IHD)

  • Fluid, solutes and toxins are removed through a dual venous access, on multiple days per week, with each session lasting several hours. (2)
  • Typical patients for IHD include end-stage renal disease patients on chronic haemodialysis, and patients with AKI requiring temporary renal support who can tolerate rapid shifts in fluid/solute levels. (2)
  • Dosing of intermittent techniques is difficult because of urea kinetics and fluid shifts in the critically ill: most studies assessing IHD measure dose in relation to frequency and duration of sessions. (1)(2)

Peritoneal dialysis (PD)

  • The patient’s peritoneal membrane is used as a natural filter to remove excess fluid and waste products from the blood. (2)
  • Typically used for CKD patients who prefer a home-based treatment, and patients who wish to avoid haemodialysis and have preserved residual kidney function. (2)

Sources for this section: (1) BJA Education 2017; (2) WFSA Tutorial 548, 2025.

08 Choosing a technique — CRRT vs IHD

The evidence

  • No randomised controlled trials have demonstrated that CRRT is superior to IHD in terms of survival. (1)(2)
  • A Cochrane meta-analysis of 15 RCTs involving 1550 critically ill patients with AKI concluded that CRRT and IHD did not differ significantly with respect to hospital mortality, ICU mortality, or requirement for long-term RRT. (2)
  • However, CRRT does have advantages, including a reduced risk of haemodynamic instability. In that meta-analysis, patients receiving CRRT had significantly higher mean arterial pressures compared with those receiving IHD. (2)

Claimed advantages of continuous techniques

  • Enhanced haemodynamic stability.
  • Superior management of fluid balance.
  • Enhanced clearance of inflammatory mediators, which may provide benefit in septic patients.
  • Better preservation of cerebral perfusion among patients with acute brain injury or fulminant hepatic failure. (1)
KDIGO consensus favours CRRT for haemodynamically unstable patients, and CRRT is typically used in critically ill patients, especially those on vasoactive infusions. (1)(2)
  • There is mounting evidence from observational studies that the use of CRRT at initiation reduces long-term dialysis dependence compared with IHD. (1)
  • With regard to CRRT, there is insufficient data to recommend one form over another. Some clinicians prefer CVVH or CVVHDF in sepsis, because convection is more effective at reducing inflammatory cytokines (mostly middle molecular weight range). (2)
  • Overall, modality selection is primarily based on institutional preference and experience — and on the capabilities within the unit providing RRT. (1)(2)

09 Timing of initiation

AKI is common in critically ill patients; however, the optimal timing for initiating RRT remains uncertain and clinical judgement must be used to determine if and when RRT is required. (1)(2)

  • RRT is generally safe but there are various associated risks, e.g. vascular access complications, hypotension, and complications of anticoagulation. (2)
  • A recent multinational randomized trial concluded that critically ill patients with severe AKI who received early initiation of RRT (within 12 hours of meeting full eligibility criteria) did not have a lower risk of death at 90 days compared to those who received delayed RRT (RRT commenced if AKI persisted for > 72 hours). (2)
  • The decision to commence RRT is clearer when AKI is associated with fluid disturbances and major metabolic disturbances such as hyperkalaemia or acidosis. However, the decision is more difficult when these complications are not present, and often the benefits of RRT in this cohort of patients is unclear. (2)
  • This uncertainty is partly due to the fact that a significant proportion of AKI patients have spontaneous recovery of renal function. (2)

Classical criteria for initiating therapy

  • Hyperkalaemia (potassium > 6.5 mmol l−1 or rapidly rising).
  • Refractory fluid overload.
  • Metabolic acidosis.
  • Certain drug and alcohol intoxications.
  • Signs of uraemia, such as pericarditis or encephalopathy. (1)
These criteria are based on expert opinion rather than evidence arising from randomised controlled trials. (1)

Risks to weigh up

Cannula insertion Biocompatibility Fluid shifts Altered drug metabolism Nursing workload Cost

These downsides must be balanced against the benefits of RRT — and consideration must also be given to the fact that spontaneous recovery of the kidneys may occur. (1)

What the evidence says

  • Some observational studies suggest earlier initiation correlates with improved survival; however, randomised controlled trial evidence is limited to the cardiac ICU population. (1)
  • Two recently published trials reported conflicting results, which may relate to the populations studied:
    • A single-centre study involving predominantly surgical patients (47% cardiac) reported a benefit of early initiation.
    • A multicentre study involving mainly medical patients reported no benefit of early treatment. (1)
  • Multicentre research is ongoing, which should help address this in a general ICU setting. (1)
  • A significant issue regarding studies on timing relates to the previous lack of consensus regarding definitions of AKI. (1)

Beyond the classical criteria

A decision to institute RRT should consider the severity of other organ failure and the trajectory of the patient’s illness. It is increasingly accepted that RRT can be used when managing fluid overload, hyperthermia, and toxicity related to acute hepatic failure or drugs. (1)

10 Discontinuation & weaning from RRT

Most critically ill patients who survive an AKI requiring RRT will recover sufficient renal function to allow discontinuation of RRT. RRT is continued until there is evidence of recovery of native renal function. (1)(2)

Markers of recovery

  • Improvement in urine output — the primary indication of recovery. A urine output of > 400 ml per day is a reasonable cut-off, equating to correct identification of the majority of patients with renal recovery from RRT-dependent AKI. (1)
  • Progressive reduction in serum creatinine during steady-state RRT. (1)(2)
  • Creatinine clearance — a more objective assessment of recovery. (1)
In one large randomised controlled trial, renal support was discontinued when measured creatinine clearance exceeded 20 ml min−1, and was left to the discretion of providers in the range of 12–20 ml min−1. (1)

KDIGO criteria for discontinuation

The KDIGO guideline criteria for discontinuation of acute RRT suggest weaning RRT when there is sufficient renal recovery to meet patient needs, or when RRT is no longer consistent with the goals of care. (2)

11 Dosing of RRT

A key element of RRT delivery is dose. Until recently, little evidence existed for dosing, which led to wide variations in clinical practice. (1)

How dose is defined

  • Continuous techniques: the dose is the sum of all effluent fluids, expressed as ml kg−1 h−1. It is important to note that the addition of dialysate and the targeting of negative fluid balance both add to the summative dose. (1)(2)
  • Intermittent techniques: dosing is difficult because of urea kinetics and fluid shifts in the critically ill — most studies measure dose in relation to frequency and duration of sessions. (1)(2)

The evidence journey

  • The notion of a relationship between treatment dose and outcome was first postulated in a single-centre study, in which patients randomised to post-dilution haemofiltration at a dose of 35 ml kg−1 h−1 or above had significantly improved survival compared with those randomised to 20 ml kg−1 h−1. (1)
  • Similar results were seen using IHD in a daily vs alternate day study. These papers led to the widespread adoption of high-dose prescribing. (1)
  • This practice has recently been challenged: two large multicentre trials both failed to detect any reduction in mortality associated with more intensive RRT, and instead provided quality evidence that effluent flow rates above 25 ml kg−1 h−1 do not improve patient outcomes in ICU. (1)
International consensus guidelines support delivery of 20–25 ml kg−1 h−1. In these trials, delivery of at least 85% of prescribed treatment time was achieved, highlighting the importance of minimising filter downtime. (1)(2)

Weight — an unresolved question

Given that CRRT dosing is intrinsically related to weight, it is surprising that little evidence exists as to the optimal measure of weight that should be used (ideal, pre-morbid, or actual). (1)

Prescribing CRRT

A prescription is required for CRRT. Factors such as body weight, age, co-administered drugs, and mode of elimination must be taken into account. A CRRT prescription should include: (2)

  1. Form of therapy
  2. Blood-flow rate
  3. Type and rate of dialysis fluid (for CVVHD and CVVHDF)
  4. Type and rate of replacement fluid (for CVVH and CVVHDF)
  5. Type and dose of anticoagulation
  6. Net fluid goal
Careful consideration is needed when prescribing high-dose RRT as there are associated risks, e.g. depletion of essential minerals, removal of critical medications, antibiotics and amino acids, and disequilibrium syndrome. It may be advisable to dose RRT dynamically by continuously adjusting the RRT dose to achieve and maintain defined goals for control of pH, volume status, or specific solutes. (2)

12 Drug dosing in CRRT

Patients receiving RRT are often on multiple medications, many of which are vital to their care and require appropriate dosing. (1)

Why pharmacokinetics are complex

  • The disease state can lead to an increased volume of distribution, an extended drug half-life, or an alteration in protein binding capacity. These variables make generalised dosing recommendations difficult. (1)
  • Drugs with low protein binding are removed by CRRT more readily, whereas those with a higher volume of distribution have lower clearance by CRRT. (1)
  • The mechanical processes of CRRT may also affect drug clearance: changes to the dialysate or blood flow rate altering the transmembrane pressure (TMP) lead to changes in drug clearance. Membrane pore size is directly proportional to the degree of drug removal by CRRT. (1)
  • Modern CRRT treatments result in a creatinine clearance of around 25–50 ml min−1. (1)

Practical drug principles

Table 7. Drug dosing considerations in CRRT (1)
Drug / classKey point
Beta-lactam antibiotics Require to be kept above the minimal inhibitory concentration for as long as possible; relatively benign toxic effects. Can be dosed either more frequently or at close to normal dose to achieve maximal benefit.
Fluconazole (antifungals) Undergoes significant tubular reabsorption in health and therefore undergoes greater clearance during CRRT — requires dose adjustment for this reason.
Vancomycin Narrow therapeutic index — requires regular level monitoring and benefits from delivery by continuous infusion to avoid rapid under- or overdosing.

Recommendations with regard to drug dosing are often based on expert consensus in conjunction with the known pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of individual drugs. Dosing of drugs therefore relates to the effects of the AKI, the effect of the RRT modality, and the behaviour of the drug itself. (1)

13 Vascular access

A double-lumen vascular catheter placed in a central vein is required for veno-venous RRT. The site of vascular access is important in ensuring successful RRT and must provide adequate blood flows to mitigate the risk of premature filter clotting. (1)(2)

Practical points

  • Except for the obese patient, the femoral site is no longer considered at higher risk for bloodstream-related infections and may be quicker to insert. (1)
  • The right internal jugular site should be used in preference to the left because of improved delivery of RRT. (1)
  • Position of the catheter tip improves flow; therefore, perhaps not site but length remains a more important feature of vascular access. (1)
  • Optimal flow rates are obtained with the catheter tip in the right atrium for internal jugular lines and within the inferior vena cava for femoral access. (1)
  • Further considerations relating to flow include the central venous pressure of the patient and kinking of the catheter. (1)
  • When not in use, locking lines with citrate reduces catheter-associated infection. (1)

14 Anticoagulation of the circuit

Clotting is the most common cause of circuit downtime. One US retrospective study found that only 68% of patients received their prescribed CRRT dose as a result of circuit downtime. Effective anticoagulation is therefore needed to prevent clotting of the extracorporeal circuit and to optimise the efficiency of the filter. This can be achieved with either systemic or regional anticoagulation. (2)

Systemic anticoagulation

The main agents used are unfractionated heparin (UFH), direct thrombin inhibitors, and low molecular weight heparins (LMWH). (2)

Table 10. Systemic anticoagulants for CRRT (1)(2)
AgentAdvantagesDisadvantages
Unfractionated heparin (UFH) Cost-effective; easy to monitor (via activated partial thromboplastin time); easily reversed with protamine; administered via a separate intravenous line; ubiquitous and cheap Bleeding risk; pro-inflammatory effects; heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT); heparin resistance (reduced antithrombin III in the critically ill)
Direct thrombin inhibitors (e.g. argatroban) Reduced risk of bleeding; commonly used in patients with HIT Associated with shorter filter patency time
Low molecular weight heparins (LMWH) Fewer adverse events compared with UFH Undergo renal elimination, which limits their use in RRT; monitoring (anti-Xa activity) is expensive and not widely available
If patients develop HIT and associated thrombosis, systemic anticoagulation with either the heparinoids (such as danaparoid) or direct thrombin inhibitors (such as argatroban) is required. If patients merely have HIT antibodies and no evidence of thrombosis, RRT circuit anticoagulation can be maintained with prostacyclin or citrate, provided systemic anticoagulation is unnecessary. (1)

Regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA)

  • Introduced in the 1990s and an alternative strategy to systemic anticoagulation. (2)
  • Citrate is added at the beginning of the circuit and works by chelating ionised calcium in the blood, leading to inhibition of the coagulation cascade. (1)(2)
  • The citrate–calcium complexes are partially filtered across the RRT membrane, thus preventing clotting in the circuit. (2)
  • The remaining citrate enters the patient’s circulation and is metabolised in the kidney, liver, or skeletal muscle (with no anticoagulant effect on the patient). (2)
  • Calcium supplementation must be given post-filter to avoid hypocalcaemia, which can be life-threatening. (1)(2)
A 2016 meta-analysis showed a significantly prolonged filter lifespan when citrate was used, and also confirmed that RCA is superior in decreasing the risk of bleeding. However, there was no mortality difference observed in the RCA group. (2)

RCA is now considered the first-line strategy for CRRT anticoagulation in patients without contraindications. (2)

15 Fluid balance & volume control
  • Mean fluid balance is a significant and independent predictor of mortality in ICU, and is significantly more positive in AKI patients who die compared with survivors. (1)
  • The extent of fluid accumulation is negatively associated with survival, as is the duration of fluid overload in patients receiving RRT. (1)
  • Fluid overload during AKI is also associated with a decreased likelihood for renal recovery. (1)
  • Use of beds with inbuilt scales can allow for trending of patient weight during RRT treatments. (1)
  • SCUF, as a form of RRT, can be used for patients whose primary problem is fluid overload. (1)

Source for this section: (1) BJA Education 2017.

16 Controversies & emerging evidence

High-volume haemofiltration in sepsis

  • It has been hypothesised that patients with sepsis may benefit from more intensive CRRT dosing owing to the removal of circulating inflammatory mediators. (1)
  • Current evidence for high-volume haemofiltration (defined by effluents greater than 50 ml kg−1 h−1) suggests it has no influence on mortality or dialysis dependency and no consistent effect with regard to serum cytokine concentrations, but can lead to a reduction in vasopressor requirements. (1)
  • This technique is associated with increased episodes of electrolyte disturbance and the risk of decreased plasma concentrations of antimicrobials in a high-risk population. (1)

Biomarkers of AKI

  • There is interest in the development of sensitive early biomarkers of AKI, given the low sensitivity and delay in increase of serum creatinine. (1)
  • There is no current level 1 evidence supporting the use of any single biomarker for the diagnosis of AKI, but using a panel of different markers may increase utility. (1)
  • There may be a further role for their use in detecting patients who are recovering from AKI. (1)

Ongoing questions

  • The timing of commencement of RRT in critically ill patients. (1)(2)
  • Optimal drug dosing during CRRT. (1)
  • Optimal anticoagulation strategies. (1)
  • The role of bicarbonate buffering tailored to individual patient needs. (1)

Source for this section: (1) BJA Education 2017.

17 Complications of RRT

Complications of RRT can be related to vascular access, the use of the extracorporeal circuit, or the use of anticoagulation. (2)

18 Long-term renal impact & follow-up
  • Among survivors of AKI, a proportion become dialysis dependent. A further subset of patients recovering from AKI never return to their baseline renal function: ongoing evidence of renal dysfunction has been described in as many as 40% of survivors. (1)(2)
  • The more severe the AKI, the higher the risk of renal dysfunction long term. (1)
  • A number of patients who survive AKI will become dependent on dialysis, whereas others will never return to their baseline renal function — long-term renal dysfunction may occur in up to 40% of patients. (2)
  • Nephrology input should be sought early on to help with the management of complicated renal disease. (2)
  • ICUs should have formalised regional pathways for specialist nephrology input. Whilst the patient remains in ICU, nephrologists can provide necessary expertise in the evaluation and management of those with complicated renal disease. (1)
  • For patients with non-resolving AKI, long-term follow-up should be arranged after discharge from both ICU and hospital. (1)
