Burn Injury Anaesthesia & Critical Care Quick Reference

Collapsible, searchable clinical summary for anaesthetists and intensivists

  • Burn injuries are more common in low- and middle-income countries. Timely resuscitation and early grafting have improved survival following significant burn injury.
  • Anaesthetic management must prioritise the short-term impact on upper airway function as a result of inhalational injury.
  • Pulmonary oedema can occur following inhalational burn injuries, or after severe burns as a result of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).
  • Appropriate early fluid resuscitation in severe burns improves survival and can be guided by special fluid resuscitation guidelines based on estimates of the injured skin area.
  • Intraoperative management should focus on specific airway management, restoration of intravascular volume, careful monitoring and judicious drug administration.
  • Pain control is complex — often requiring high-dose opioids including methadone, plus adjuncts such as ketamine, lignocaine, clonidine and other non-opioid analgesics.
  • Burn injuries contribute significantly to the global burden of disease and remain a leading cause of worldwide morbidity.
  • More than 8,000,000 new cases of burns occur annually worldwide.
  • More than 95% of burn deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Most aetiologies result from direct skin contact with a chemical, heat, electrical, friction or radiation source, causing damage to superficial tissues.
  • The specific mechanism and extent of burn injury strongly influence prognosis.
  • Consequences range from simple first aid, through to advanced resuscitation, complex pain management, progressive wound care and major surgery.
  • Occurs in 20% of burn injuries; arises from inhalation of smoke components that damage oropharyngeal and pulmonary airway structures.
  • Patients with inhalation injury are at risk of higher mortality due to sequelae including:
    • Asphyxiation from carbon monoxide poisoning
    • Acute pulmonary oedema
    • Bronchospasm
  • SIRS can also contribute, causing severe lung injury as well as organ failure.

Acute — the "ebb phase" (first 48 hours)

  • Immediately following a burn insult, the local area undergoes cell death; this can be extremely painful due to activation of exposed injured nerve endings.
  • Tissue injury releases inflammatory mediators — histamine, leukotrienes, kinins and prostaglandins — which may initiate a systemic response if a large enough burn area is involved.
  • Burns involving more than 25% TBSA are at increased risk of systemic inflammatory response.
  • Systemic response can lead to shock associated with:
    • Low peripheral vascular resistance
    • Decreased cardiac output
    • Increased vascular permeability
    • Hypercoagulability
    • Hypoalbuminaemia
  • All of these can compromise potentially viable areas of tissue. Early careful wound management and rapid fluid resuscitation can reduce the TBSA susceptible to ischaemia and tissue damage.

Plateau phase

  • Occurs for a number of days after the initial metabolic changes.
  • Characterised by hyperdynamic circulation with increased oxygen and glucose consumption.

Long term

  • More than half of patients with significant burn injuries report chronic pain years after the injury.
  • Pathophysiology is multifactorial:
    • Prolonged intense nociceptive pain from damaged tissues
    • Neuropathic pain from specific damage to, and inflammation involving, injured nerve fibres
    • Both injured tissues and donor tissues can be affected
  • Repeated painful dressing changes can potentially prolong pain — but are required to minimise infection and promote healing.
  • Additional factors: scarring interfering with musculoskeletal function, and psychological sequelae from physical disfiguration and potential loss of livelihood.
  • First degree — epidermis alone (superficial). Not counted in calculations of TBSA involvement.
  • Second degree — dermis and epidermis (partial thickness).
  • Third degree — epidermis, dermis and hypodermis (full thickness). Requires surgical debridement, full excision and grafting.
  • Early excision and grafting, within 3 days of injury, has reduced mortality from 45% to 9% in patients with greater than 30% TBSA burns not involving inhalation injury.

Determining severity

  • Patient age
  • TBSA involved
  • Depth of burn
  • Mechanism of burn
  • Specific body areas affected
  • There is no consensus classification for what constitutes a "major burn".

  • Patients with a major burn injury often require immediate airway management, which can be challenging.
  • Airway status can evolve rapidly after burn injury and may necessitate intervention at an early stage to secure the airway before it becomes more difficult to manage.
  • Airway management can be complicated by:
    • Limitation in mouth opening
    • Airway and facial oedema
    • Difficulties interpreting airway anatomy
  • Techniques to consider: direct or video laryngoscopy, awake fibre-optic intubation, laryngeal mask insertion (if required for transient airway control), and — less commonly — emergency cricothyroid puncture and tracheostomy.
  • Secure airway devices with suturing — tape or ties may not adhere to inflamed tissues or may interfere with burned areas requiring ongoing surgical management.
ISBI guidance: the only indication for intubation or tracheostomy should be current or evolving impairment of airway patency.

Clinical signs prompting action

  • Airway swelling
  • Soot contamination of airway secretions
  • Increasing work of breathing
  • Hoarseness
  • Stridor
  • Dysphagia
  • Increased salivation

This emphasises the importance of continued clinical assessment and early intervention should airway signs show a clear pattern of deterioration.

  • Pulmonary complications, such as pulmonary oedema, remain a major cause of mortality after severe or inhalational burn injury.
  • Symptoms: wheezing, hoarseness, bronchorrhoea and excessive sputum production, together with tachypnoea and low oxygen saturations.

Two mechanisms of pulmonary oedema after burn injury

  • Direct irritation by heat, smoke or chemicals.
  • Systemic inflammation (SIRS) — a stereotypical lung response to severe injury.

Management

  • Oxygen therapy
  • Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)
  • Intubation and controlled ventilation for patients with signs of inhalation injury or compromised pulmonary function
  • Bleeding from wounds, evaporative losses and systemic responses involving vasoactive and inflammatory mediators can lead to impaired organ perfusion after burn injury.
  • Burn shock — marked hypoperfusion and hypovolaemia occurring within the first 24 hours after a major burn — necessitates aggressive fluid resuscitation.
  • Inadequate fluid resuscitation results in worsening burn injury and higher mortality.
  • Burn shock remains the most common cause of death from severe burn injury in the first week.

Practical use of formulas

  • Formulas provide estimates of both rate and volume of fluid resuscitation.
  • Historically the modified Brooke formula and the Parkland formula were used; the Rule of 10 is a recent addition.
  • These formulas are best used in the context of full assessment of:
    • Vital sign patterns
    • Urine output
    • Cardiac filling pressures
    • Trending creatinine

Crystalloid vs colloid

  • Crystalloid is an accepted form of therapy for volume loss after burn injury.
  • It may be more appropriate to transfuse colloid when intravascular volume levels are critical, as part of a restrictive fluid strategy.

Blood transfusion & coagulopathy

  • More restrictive strategies are recommended for blood transfusions: transfusing for haemoglobin <7 g/dL compared with <10 g/dL revealed no difference in mortality or length of stay.
  • Major burn injuries can trigger disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), potentially exacerbated by:
    • Haemodilution as a result of rapid resuscitation
    • Endothelial damage
    • Systemic inflammatory response
    • Hypothermia
  • Current guidelines recommend factor replacement for management of DIC in these patients.
  • Antifibrinolytic treatments such as tranexamic acid are reserved for patients experiencing DIC as well as severe bleeding.
  • Purpose of surgery: remove dead and unviable tissue to avoid infection and ongoing fluid loss, and replace it with healthy skin or a reasonable substitute that can temporarily or permanently cover the wound.

Graft classification

  • Autograft — harvested from normal skin from the same patient:
    • Split-thickness — covers the epidermis and part of the dermis
    • Full-thickness — covers epidermis, dermis and hypodermis
  • Allograft — harvested from humans
  • Xenograft — harvested from non-humans or synthetic material

Temporary coverage

  • Where the patient's own skin is not available, allografts or xenografts can be considered as a temporary cover for 1 to 2 weeks — an area of ongoing research.
  • Donor material derived from cadaveric or living relative donor skin can be used as allografts.
  • Xenografts have been derived from porcine, bovine, synthetic, human, amniotic membrane and cultured cell sources.

Blood loss — a key intraoperative trap

  • Operations involving excision of burns and donor skin harvesting can result in deceptively high losses of blood because of the potentially large surface areas involved.
  • Surgical use of topical vasopressors can further confuse volume loss estimates by temporarily elevating blood pressure in settings of low intravascular volume.
  • A pattern of continued high heart rate, together with falls in both blood pressure and cardiac filling pressure, may indicate hypovolaemia that is unmasked after the effects of any vasopressor have worn off.
  • This should be addressed with volume therapy and blood transfusion as appropriate.

Depending on the site of burn injury, intraoperative monitoring can be difficult.

  • Pulse oximetry may not function on fingers or toes — alternative sites such as the nose, ear or tongue often need to be used instead. Rarely, arterial blood gases may have to be used to estimate oxygenation level.
  • Temperature probes are essential. Monitoring site can include oral, axillary, tympanic, rectal, bladder or oesophagus, depending on the injured areas.
  • Non-invasive blood pressure is possible if the cuff can be applied to non-burned areas; otherwise, invasive blood pressure monitoring is required.
  • ECG may be difficult to obtain as the adhesive may not stick to burnt tissues. Options include needle electrodes or skin staples.
  • Patients with burns are often transferred intubated and ventilated from intensive care or high-dependency facilities — care must be taken to transfer patients to theatre without incident and to maintain existing ventilation therapies (PEEP, pressure control, lower fractional oxygen therapies).
  • Patients who are not intubated may present all the challenges discussed in the resuscitation section and may require strategies to manage difficult intubation scenarios.
Suxamethonium: may be safely used in burn patients in the first 48 hours. After that, its use may cause an exaggerated hyperkalaemic response in acutely burned patients.
  • Analgesia for severe burn injuries can be challenging to manage. Opioid requirements can be significant, and opioids remain the mainstay of analgesia in severe burn injury.
  • In many cases, high-dose infusions of opioids and sedatives are commenced to aid endotracheal tube tolerance. This may create opioid tolerance, leading to higher opioid requirements.
  • Specialist pain management is desirable, with strategies such as:
    • Opioid rotation
    • Introduction of opioid-sparing analgesic strategies
    • Careful opioid de-escalation

Opioid-sparing agents

  • Ketamine infusion
  • Lignocaine infusion
  • As a patient progresses from major graft surgery to ward-based dressing changes, ketamine and non-opioid analgesia are often added to existing opioid therapy to facilitate conscious sedation.

Non-pharmacologic therapies

  • Virtual reality devices and music therapy have shown some promise in recent years to aid dressing changes, particularly in the paediatric burn population.
  • Significant fluid shifts and volume losses occur in this patient population — vascular access is crucial but may present special challenges.
  • Invasive monitoring and large-bore venous lines are essential, as burn patients have large, exposed surface areas leading to unexpectedly high losses — especially during first presentation and during surgical procedures.
  • Such access facilitates:
    • Rapid fluid resuscitation
    • Access and monitoring
    • Inotropic support
  • Lines should be sited in areas that are not in the surgical field.
  • Suturing and ties rather than standard vascular access adhesive dressings may be needed.
  • Use of ultrasound to visualise arteries and veins may be required to guide placement.

Nutrition

  • Adequate nutritional support is essential in severe burn management — particularly in individuals with burns exceeding 40% TBSA, who experience a doubling of their basal metabolic rate.
  • The enteral route is preferred, to facilitate post-pyloric feeding and avoid the effects of associated gastric stasis.
  • If regular oral feeding is not possible, nasogastric or total parenteral nutrition options must be explored — insufficient nutrition following a burn injury has been correlated with increased mortality.

Thermoregulation

  • Monitoring of temperature in patients with severe burns can be challenging but is vital for detecting thermodysregulation.
  • Severe hypothermia — due to heat and fluid loss.
  • Profound hyperthermia — can lead to multiorgan failure.
  • Hyperthermia in major burn patients can be a sign of:
    • Altered hypothalamic thermal setpoint
    • An infectious complication
    • Transfusion reaction
All episodes of hyperthermia must be investigated appropriately — infections contribute to approximately half of all deaths due to burn injury.
  • Patients with significant burn injuries have altered pharmacodynamic responses as well as alterations in pharmacokinetic parameters such as volume of distribution, levels of protein binding, and hepatic and renal clearance.
  • These pathophysiological changes mean that careful titration and monitoring of commonly used anaesthetic drugs may be required.

Suxamethonium & hyperkalaemia

  • Burn injury–related changes cause proliferation of extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors that release more potassium into the extracellular space.
  • This predisposes to life-threatening hyperkalaemia when suxamethonium is used.
  • Risk appears greatest in patients more than 48 hours after injury and for up to 1 year post-injury.

Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs)

  • Pharmacodynamics may also be altered in burn patients — thought to be multifactorial.
  • Rocuronium 1.2–1.5 mg/kg is recommended for rapid sequence induction.
  • Effective paralysis time is approximately 90 seconds, compared with 60 seconds in the non-burn population.
  • Thought due, at least in part, to the presence of extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors, which bind non-depolarising NMBAs and compete with junctional receptors.

Plasma protein binding

  • Plasma protein concentration and binding capabilities may be drastically altered after severe burn injury.
  • Hypoalbuminaemia — due to protein-rich fluid loss and decreased hepatic synthesis.
  • Alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AAG) concentrations can increase as part of the inflammatory response.
  • Consequences:
    • Drugs bound to albumin may be present in a higher free concentration.
    • Drugs primarily bound to AAG — such as local anaesthetics and alfentanil — will have a lower free concentration than in the non-burn population.
  • Pharmacodynamic changes may also arise from continued exposure to opioid therapy for relief of sustained pain, resulting in opioid tolerance.
  • Improved resuscitation management and surgical techniques have led to improved mortality rates following severe burn injury.
  • Despite this, survivable burn injuries can create long-term issues that significantly affect quality of life.
  • Approximately 1 of every 3 patients develops at least one contracture following a burn injury, which can limit mobility and function.
  • Contractures can require further surgeries many months after the original injury and cause additional scarring as well as contribute to chronic pain.
  • Careful consideration must be given to airway management, muscle relaxant choice and positioning during surgery in these patients.

Case 1 Parkland in practice

Scenario35-year-old, 60 kg, 40% TBSA flame burn, arrives 2 hours after injury, no inhalation injury.

Key issue4 mL × kg × %TBSA = 4 × 60 × 40 = 9600 mL / 24 h. Half (4800 mL) should be given in the first 8 hours — counted from the time of injury, so 2 hours have already elapsed.

ActionHartmann's via large-bore access. Titrate to urine output (target ~0.5 mL/kg/h in adults), vital signs, cardiac filling pressures and trending creatinine. Never rely on the formula alone.

Case 2 Inhalation injury and the evolving airway

Scenario28-year-old from a house fire: hoarse voice, singed nares, soot in the oropharynx, new stridor.

Key issueCurrent or evolving impairment of airway patency is the ISBI indication for intubation. Airway status evolves rapidly and oedema progresses.

ActionIntubate early with a planned technique (video laryngoscopy or awake fibre-optic). Secure the ETT with sutures — tape will not adhere to inflamed tissue. Do not wait for frank obstruction.

Case 3 Suxamethonium timing

ScenarioDay 5 after a 50% TBSA burn, booked for a dressing change under general anaesthesia.

Key issueExtrajunctional acetylcholine receptor proliferation causes an exaggerated hyperkalaemic response to suxamethonium after 48 hours, with risk persisting up to 1 year.

ActionAvoid suxamethonium. Use rocuronium 1.2–1.5 mg/kg for RSI — effective paralysis takes ~90 seconds versus ~60 seconds in non-burn patients. Suxamethonium is acceptable only within the first 48 hours.

Case 4 The deceptively bloody excision

Scenario45% TBSA burn booked for excision and split-skin grafting.

Key issueLarge raw surface areas cause large concealed blood loss. Topical vasopressors used surgically can temporarily mask hypovolaemia by propping up blood pressure.

ActionLarge-bore access, invasive blood pressure monitoring, cross-matched blood available, core temperature monitoring. Watch for persistent tachycardia with falling BP and filling pressures as the vasopressor wears off — treat with volume and transfusion. Note pulse oximetry may need nasal/ear/tongue sites, and ECG may need needle electrodes or staples.

Case 5 Transfusion threshold and DIC

ScenarioMajor burn with Hb 8 g/dL, oozing from all wound and line sites.

Key issueA restrictive strategy (transfuse for Hb <7 g/dL) showed no difference in mortality or length of stay versus <10 g/dL. DIC can be triggered by major burns and exacerbated by haemodilution, endothelial damage, SIRS and hypothermia.

ActionTransfuse for Hb <7 g/dL. Manage DIC with factor replacement. Reserve tranexamic acid for patients with DIC plus severe bleeding.

Case 6 Paediatric scald

Scenario3-year-old, 12 kg, 20% TBSA scald.

Key issueAll paediatric burns (<14 y or <30 kg) may benefit from burn centre referral — for pain, dressing change needs, rehabilitation, caregiver needs, or non-accidental trauma.

ActionWeight-based fluid resuscitation, meticulous thermoregulation (high surface-area-to-mass ratio), careful weight-based drug dosing, and early paediatric burn centre involvement.

Case 7 Opioid-tolerant chronic burn pain

Scenario6 months post-burn, on high-dose opioids, presenting for contracture release.

Key issueOpioid tolerance from sustained exposure; both injured and donor/grafted areas can be painful; >50% of significant burns report chronic pain years later.

ActionOpioid rotation, add ketamine and lignocaine infusions, consider methadone and clonidine, use non-opioid adjuncts, and offer virtual reality/music therapy for dressing changes. Involve specialist pain management with a planned opioid de-escalation strategy.

Case 8 High-voltage electrical injury

ScenarioHigh-voltage (≥1000 V) electrical injury with entry and exit wounds.

Key issueAll high-voltage electrical injuries and lightning injuries require burn centre referral. Deep tissue damage may far exceed visible skin injury.

ActionCardiac monitoring for arrhythmia, assess for rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome, generous fluid resuscitation, and screen for associated blunt trauma and fractures.

Case 9 Hyperthermia in a major burn

Scenario60% TBSA burn, day 4 in ICU, temperature 39.5°C.

Key issueHyperthermia may represent an altered hypothalamic thermal setpoint, an infectious complication, or a transfusion reaction. Infections contribute to ~half of all burn deaths.

ActionFull septic screen, review lines and wounds, send cultures, and consider transfusion reaction. Treat both hypothermia and hyperthermia actively — profound hyperthermia can lead to multiorgan failure.

Case 10 Altered protein binding

ScenarioBurn patient on an alfentanil and lignocaine infusion appears unexpectedly light at a standard dose.

Key issueAAG rises as part of the inflammatory response — AAG-bound drugs (local anaesthetics, alfentanil) have a lower free concentration. Albumin falls — albumin-bound drugs have a higher free fraction.

ActionTitrate to clinical effect, anticipate altered requirements, and monitor for toxicity of highly protein-bound drugs. Reassess dosing as the inflammatory response evolves.
