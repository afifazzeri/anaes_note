★ Top Take-Home Bricks›
★ The single most important message High blood pressure in isolation is NOT a reason to cancel elective surgery. Decide on the patient's overall cardiac risk, not on one number. There is no "one size fits all" threshold above which elective surgery must be deferred.
  • AoA/BHS: defer elective surgery if BP > 180/110 mmHg.
  • POQI: questions whether any specific BP threshold should determine proceeding.
  • 2025 AHA/ACC: if SBP ≥ 180 or DBP ≥ 110, deferring may be considered (2b) — especially in high-risk patients. Below 180/110 there is little evidence to defer.
  • "White-coat" day-of-surgery hypertension — check baseline/home/ambulatory BP before acting.

The 3 perioperative phases

1 · Preoperative Assess overall CV risk + target-organ damage. Individualise antihypertensives (hold ACEI/ARB 24 h; continue β-blocker if established; never start β-blocker de novo on day of surgery).
2 · Intraoperative Avoid hypotension. Cumulative MAP < 55 mmHg is associated with myocardial and kidney injury. Individualised management within 10% of baseline with noradrenaline reduced postoperative organ dysfunction (INPRESS).
3 · Postoperative Postop hypertension occurs in up to 20%. SBP > 180 is a high-risk flag. Do not give PRN IV/oral antihypertensives to asymptomatic hospitalised patients — restart/intensify oral therapy.

2 Preoperative Assessment & Investigations›

Hypertension is usually asymptomatic — it is often found on routine screening or during surgical work-up. Symptoms suggesting hypertensive emergency: papilloedema, new confusion, chest pain, heart failure signs, AKI.

🚨 Same-day specialist review Symptomatic patients with BP > 180/120 mmHg.

History — what to ask

Disease Duration & level of raised BP · symptoms of secondary causes · symptoms of target-organ damage (renal impairment, HF) · CV disease symptoms (CHD, cerebrovascular) · concomitant disease (diabetes, gout) · snoring / daytime somnolence (OSA).
Drugs & lifestyle Prescribed & OTC meds (NSAIDs, nasal decongestants, OCP/HRT, steroids, ciclosporin) · herbal/complementary medicines · salt, caffeine, liquorice, alcohol · smoking, physical inactivity, substance use, psychosocial stressors, weight gain.

Examination

  • Height, weight, BMI, waist circumference
  • BP measured properly — both arms on first visit, take the higher reading; sitting and standing in the elderly
  • Fundoscopy (hypertensive retinopathy) · carotid & abdominal bruit · peripheral pulses & radio-femoral delay
  • Cardiac: cardiomegaly, HF signs, aortic regurgitation · abdominal: renal masses, AAA · neuro: stroke signs
  • Endocrine stigmata (Cushingoid, acromegaly, thyroid) · ankle–brachial index if available

3 Secondary Hypertension — Red Flags›

Accounts for 5–15% of hypertension (up to 25% in some series). Non-urgent surgery should be delayed to allow investigation. If identified, targeted therapy may dramatically improve BP control and reduce CV risk.

🚩 Red flags — think secondary cause Young < 30 y Abrupt onset Sudden deterioration in previously stable BP Resistant hypertension Spontaneous or diuretic-induced hypokalaemia Adrenal incidentaloma OSA features Renal bruit TOD disproportionate to BP duration

4 Preoperative Antihypertensive Management — THE KEY BRICK›

POQI consensus — preoperative drug algorithm (simplified)

ACEI / ARB Hold 24 h preoperatively · restart within 48 h after operation.
Withholding is associated with lower risk of death and postoperative vascular events (VISION cohort, n = 14,687). If accidentally taken on the morning of surgery, do not cancel — anticipate hypotension and plan management.
▼
Thiazide diuretic / Calcium channel blocker No evidence to stop — continue.
Loop diuretics: consider per patient (volume status, HF).
▼
β-Blocker Continue if prescribed for heart failure or previous MI.
Do NOT start preoperatively for risk reduction.
POQI note No evidence to lower a high BP immediately preoperatively.
POQI note Preoperative BP values may be used to define perioperative BP targets.
POQI note Preoperative hypotension may be associated with increased risk.

Drug-by-drug perioperative plan

Drug classPerioperative actionEvidence / caveats
ACE inhibitors
(ACEI)		 Withhold 24 h before surgery (POQI). Restart as soon as appropriate — ideally within 48 h. VISION study (14,687 non-cardiac surgery patients): withholding → lower risk of death & postoperative vascular events. Risk: intraoperative hypotension → organ hypoperfusion and AKI. Conflicting: 2014 ACC/AHA recommend continuing; ESC/ESA warn of hypotension but suggest continuing in LV dysfunction. POISE-3 RCT found no significant difference between withholding vs continuing strategies, but withholding reduced intraoperative hypotension. Individualise.
Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) Withhold 24 h before surgery (POQI). Restart within 48 h. Same evidence base as ACEI. Hypotension may be more profound when combined with diuretics. In cardiac surgery, withholding ACEI/ARB has been reported to be associated with increased mortality — different context.
β-Blockers Continue if already established (especially HF, post-MI, arrhythmia). Do NOT initiate on the day of surgery in β-blocker-naïve patients. Fixed-dose β-blockade initiated de novo in the immediate perioperative period is associated with increased risk of perioperative death (POISE). Abrupt withdrawal of long-term β-blockers → rebound hypertension and is potentially harmful. β-Blockers are no longer first-line for uncomplicated hypertension.
Calcium channel blockers Continue — no evidence to stop (POQI). Limited perioperative evidence; decisions on an individual patient basis. Long-acting dihydropyridines are first-line agents for chronic treatment.
Thiazide / thiazide-like diuretics Continue (POQI: no evidence to stop). Watch electrolytes (hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia) — check K⁺ and Na⁺ 2–4 weeks after initiation/dose change. Thiazide-like agents (chlorthalidone, indapamide) preferred in resistant hypertension.
Loop diuretics Consider per patient — assess volume status and HF. Preferred in moderate–severe CKD (eGFR < 30) and symptomatic HF.
Clonidine / central α-2 agonists Do not stop abruptly — taper. Rebound hypertension with catecholamine surge. Avoid oral clonidine for hypertension where possible. If patient cannot take oral meds, use IV alternatives.
MRA (spironolactone / eplerenone) Continue unless hyperkalaemia or AKI risk. Recheck K⁺ and creatinine 2–4 weeks after changes. Fourth-line agent for resistant hypertension. Avoid with K⁺ supplements, other K⁺-sparing diuretics, or eGFR < 45. Avoid in pregnancy.
Statins / antiplatelets Continue. Part of optimal medical therapy for atherosclerotic disease.

⚠️ Combination pills Single-pill combinations (e.g. ACEI + CCB + diuretic) are convenient and improve adherence but complicate perioperative management when one class is to be stopped and another continued. Check exactly what the patient is taking.
2025 AHA/ACC perioperative recommendations
  • Continue β-blockers throughout the perioperative period in patients on chronic therapy (COR 1).
  • It is reasonable to continue most antihypertensives throughout the perioperative period (COR 2a).
  • Discontinuation of ACEI/ARB may be considered to prevent intraoperative hypotension (COR 2b).
  • If SBP ≥ 180 or DBP ≥ 110, deferring elective surgery may be considered, especially in high-risk patients (COR 2b).
  • Abrupt preoperative discontinuation of β-blocker or clonidine is potentially harmful (COR 3: Harm).
  • Do NOT start β-blockers on the day of surgery in β-blocker-naïve patients (COR 3: Harm).
5 Intraoperative Blood Pressure Management›

Why hypertensive patients are different

  • General anaesthetic agents cause hypotension via vasodilation and reduced cardiac output.
  • Patients with hypertension show greater cardiovascular lability: BP and HR surges at induction, and greater risk of hypotension intraoperatively.
  • Cerebral and organ autoregulation curves may be shifted rightward in chronic hypertension (not a consistent finding across studies) — a "normal" MAP may be relatively hypoperfusive.

Evidence for BP targets

Observational (Walsh 2013, n ≈ 33,000) Increasing cumulative duration of MAP < 55 mmHg was associated with myocardial injury and acute kidney injury.
RCT — INPRESS (Futier 2017) Individualised BP management (within 10% of baseline, using dilute noradrenaline infusion) vs standard care (ephedrine for SBP < 80 mmHg or < 40% of baseline) in major surgery.
→ Individualised group had significantly lower rates of postoperative organ dysfunction.

Practical intraoperative targets

ParameterSuggested targetNotes
Systolic arterial pressure> 100 mmHgMay reduce risk (no universal consensus)
Mean arterial pressure> 60 mmHgAvoid cumulative MAP < 55 mmHg
Relative to baselineWithin 10% of baseline; within 20% of baseline if baseline is high (> 130/80)Preoperative BP may be used to define intraoperative targets
⚠ BP is not perfusion An "adequate" BP does not guarantee adequate tissue oxygenation. Young patients can compensate for significant hypovolaemia and maintain normal BP at the expense of regional hypoperfusion. Perfusion pressure is affected by myogenic, neurogenic and metabolic factors. Interpret BP in the clinical context — not as a standalone number.
✔ Practical recipe
  1. Establish a reliable baseline BP (pre-op clinic / home readings, not the day-of-surgery value).
  2. Use continuous or frequent arterial monitoring in high-risk or major surgery.
  3. Prefer titratable vasopressor infusion (e.g. dilute noradrenaline) over intermittent boluses to smooth BP.
  4. Avoid prolonged periods below MAP 55–60 mmHg, especially in patients with CKD, CAD or cerebrovascular disease.
  5. Anticipate greater hypotension if ACEI/ARB or diuretics were taken on the day of surgery.
6 Postoperative Hypertension›
  • Occurs in up to 20% of patients after elective non-cardiac surgery.
  • Associated with adverse outcomes: stroke, myocardial injury, arrhythmias, bleeding.
  • Limited evidence for a specific harmful threshold — but SBP > 180 mmHg is included as a high-risk trigger in several validated early warning systems.
🚫 2025 AHA/ACC — COR 3: HARM For adults with severe hypertension (> 180/120 mmHg) hospitalised for non-cardiac conditions without evidence of acute target-organ damage, intermittent use of additional IV or oral antihypertensives is NOT recommended to acutely reduce BP.
Observational data: PRN antihypertensives in hospitalised patients → increased in-hospital mortality, AKI and prolonged stay. Spontaneous BP falls occur in 40–50% of such patients.
✔ What to do instead Reinstitute, intensify or modify oral antihypertensive therapy (preferably outpatient), with close follow-up within 4 weeks. Use careful, frequent monitoring. Avoid as-needed medications in asymptomatic patients.

Postoperative hypertension checklist

  • Is there pain, agitation, full bladder, hypoxaemia, hypercapnia or hypothermia? Treat the cause first.
  • Was chronic antihypertensive therapy omitted? Restart.
  • Is there acute target-organ damage (chest pain, breathlessness, neuro deficit, oliguria)? → Hypertensive emergency pathway.
  • Was the patient on β-blocker or clonidine that has been stopped? → Rebound hypertension risk.
  • Check for drug interactions: NSAIDs, sympathomimetics, steroids.

Hypertensive urgency — management

Target: reduce BP by ~25% over 24 hours — do NOT normalise rapidly Rapid reduction in urgency may precipitate ischaemic events. No role for IV drugs.
  1. Rest in a quiet room for at least 2 hours; repeat BP every 30 min.
  2. If BP responds (10–20 mmHg SBP reduction) → discharge with a hypertensive urgency discharge plan.
  3. If BP remains > 180/110 mmHg after rest → start oral combination antihypertensive therapy, targeting ~25% reduction within 24 h, but not lower than 160/100 mmHg.
  4. Recheck BP every 30 min for 1 hour; if no reduction → admit / refer.
  5. Common precipitants: non-adherence, inadequate outpatient control, acute pain, herbal supplements, emotional stress.

Hypertensive emergency — management

Default strategy (no compelling indication) Reduce BP by no more than 25% within the first hour → then to 160/100 mmHg over the next 2–6 hours → then cautiously towards normal over 24–48 hours.

🎯 Agent selection principles Base the choice on: drug pharmacology · pathophysiological mechanism · degree of target-organ damage · desired rate of BP decline · comorbidities. Continuous infusion of short-acting titratable agents is often preferable in ICU to avoid large BP swings. Avoid peaks and large SBP variability — especially in ICH.
8 Special Groups — Elderly, Pregnancy, Stroke›

🧓 Older adults

  • Prevalence > 60% in people > 60 years; up to 71.7% in Malaysia > 65 years.
  • SBP rises linearly with age — SBP is a better predictor of CV events than DBP in this group.
  • Treatment of isolated systolic hypertension reduces all-cause mortality, CV mortality, MI, HF and stroke — benefit extends to the very elderly (> 80).
  • Postural hypotension: measure lying and standing BP. A systolic drop > 20 mmHg after 1 min standing is significant. Use standing BP to guide treatment.
  • Screen for frailty, mobility, function, cognition, nutrition, falls before intensifying therapy.
  • Start low-dose monotherapy, titrate slowly, review every 2–4 weeks. Initiation with combination therapy is not encouraged in this group.
  • Consider de-prescribing in the frail, functionally/cognitively impaired, multimorbid, or those with adverse drug reactions — evidence shows no increase in mortality with de-prescribing.

🤰 Pregnancy

Preferred agents Labetalol and extended-release nifedipine are the preferred agents (2025 AHA/ACC COR 1). Methyldopa remains first-line in the Malaysian CPG, with labetalol as alternative first-line.
Contraindicated Atenolol, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, direct renin inhibitors, nitroprusside, MRA. Fetotoxic in 2nd/3rd trimester (renal development → oligohydramnios, AKI). Avoid RAS blockers in all women of childbearing potential unless adequate contraception.
🚨 Severe-range hypertension in pregnancy SBP ≥ 160 or DBP ≥ 110 confirmed on repeat within 15 minutes → give antihypertensive medication to lower BP to < 160/< 110 mmHg within 30–60 minutes (COR 1).

🫘 Chronic kidney disease

  • Target SBP < 130 mmHg in adults with hypertension and CKD (eGFR < 60 with albuminuria ≥ 30 mg/g).
  • ACEI or ARB (not both) recommended to decrease CVD and delay kidney disease progression.
  • Expect a dip in eGFR of up to 30% after starting a RAAS inhibitor — this is expected and associated with long-term preservation of kidney function. Do not discontinue unless the decline is persistently > 30%.
  • Recheck electrolytes and creatinine 2–4 weeks after initiation or dose titration.
  • Avoid dual RAAS blockade (ACEI + ARB) — increased AKI and hyperkalaemia.
⚡ Quick Action Cards›
Pre-op clinic — BP 195/115, elective hip BP > 180/110 → AoA/BHS advise deferral and initiation of antihypertensive treatment. 2025 AHA/ACC: deferring may be considered (2b), especially in high-risk patients. Confirm with home/ambulatory readings — exclude white-coat effect. Assess overall cardiac risk and target-organ damage before deciding.
Patient took ramipril this morning Do NOT cancel. Anticipate greater intraoperative hypotension. Plan for vasopressor support and consider invasive monitoring. Restart postoperatively as soon as appropriate (within 48 h).
Intraoperative MAP drifting to 52 mmHg Cumulative MAP < 55 mmHg is associated with myocardial and AKI injury. Aim MAP > 60 and within 10% of baseline. Use a titratable noradrenaline infusion to achieve individualised targets — this reduced postoperative organ dysfunction in INPRESS.
Postop day 1, BP 190/100, asymptomatic Do not give PRN IV/oral antihypertensives to acutely lower BP (COR 3: Harm). Reinstitute/intensify oral therapy, monitor closely, arrange follow-up within 4 weeks. Look for pain, bladder distension, hypoxaemia, missed medication.
