1 Triage — AHA/ACC Acute PE Clinical Categories

Categories are assigned by the most severe clinical, laboratory, or imaging indicator. Patients may move between categories over time. Add “R” (respiratory modifier) when: RR ≥30, SpO₂ <90%, need for supplemental O₂, >6 L/min nasal cannula, or non-rebreather mask.

Score thresholds used to define severity

  • Low clinical severity score: PESI ≤85, or sPESI = 0, or Bova ≤4
  • Elevated clinical severity score: PESI >85, or sPESI ≥1, or Bova >4
  • D2 (normotensive shock) markers — any of: lactate >2 mmol/L · acute kidney injury · urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h · altered mental status · cardiac index <2.2 L/min/m² · MAP <60 mmHg · increased shock score/stage (SCAI, CPES)
  • E2: refractory cardiogenic shock (SCAI D–E) or cardiac arrest without ROSC after 30 min of resuscitation
Disposition implications: Category A can be discharged from the ED without hospitalisation. Category B is generally suitable for early discharge. Categories C, D and E should be hospitalised — with escalation of monitoring and level of care as the category rises.

3 Risk Stratification — Scores, Biomarkers, Haemodynamics, RV Imaging

Clinical risk scores

  • COR 1 · B-R In Categories A and B, use Hestia, PESI and/or sPESI to identify low risk of short-term adverse outcomes.
  • COR 2a · B-NR In haemodynamically stable Categories C and D, a validated PE-specific risk score is reasonable to identify higher short-term risk.
  • COR 2b · B-NR In stable Categories C and D, NEWS/NEWS2 may be a reasonable alternative to a PE-specific score to identify patients needing monitoring for deterioration.

Biomarkers

  • COR 1 · B-NR In Category C (elevated severity score, no hypotension/shock), measure at least one cardiac biomarker (troponin or BNP) for short-term risk stratification.
  • COR 1 · B-NR In Categories C–E, measure lactate (venous or arterial) to assist short-term risk stratification. Use the local assay threshold.

Haemodynamic assessment

  • COR 2a · B-NR In Category D2, evaluate for normotensive shock to identify increased risk of deterioration and in-hospital death.
  • COR 2a · B-NR In Category C3, a MAP <80 mmHg may be useful to identify patients who may require escalation of therapy. (A MAP >80 mmHg identified patients at very low risk of in-hospital death in registry data.)

Right ventricular imaging for risk stratification

  • COR 1 · A In Categories C–D (elevated severity score without shock), RV imaging is recommended for short-term risk stratification.
  • COR 2a · B-NR In Categories C–D, echocardiography is preferred over CT for short-term risk stratification.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · B-NR In Categories A–C, quantification of angiographic thrombus burden for short-term risk stratification is not recommended.

5 Sedation & Ventilation — The Anaesthetist's Core Risk
Central concept: In acute PE with RV dysfunction, compensatory sympathetic tone and heart rate maintain systemic perfusion. Sedation, induction of anaesthesia, and positive-pressure ventilation blunt adrenergic tone, decrease RV preload, and increase RV afterload — a recipe for catastrophic haemodynamic collapse and cardiac arrest.

Recommendations

  • COR 1 · C-LD In Categories C–E requiring sedation for intubation, haemodynamic supportive therapies (vasopressors, inotropes, and/or VA-ECMO) should be available to support the patient if they become unstable.
  • COR 3 · Harm · C-LD In Categories C–E, deep sedation and mechanical ventilation should not be performed unless clinically indicated, to avoid haemodynamic collapse.
  • COR 2a · C-LD For acute PE with moderate–severe hypoxia, heated high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) is beneficial over standard nasal cannula to improve oxygenation.

Evidence behind the caution

  • Case series of surgical embolectomy: CPR required in 19% of patients after anaesthesia induction despite all being haemodynamically stable at induction.
  • A second surgical series reported CPR related to anaesthesia induction in 28% (9/32).
  • In catheter-directed therapy patients (Category C2–3 characteristics), sedation strategy was strongly associated with the need for CPR and in-hospital mortality.
  • Rapid deployment of emergency cardiopulmonary bypass for post-induction arrest produced hospital mortality similar to patients who did not require CPR — rescue must be immediately available.

Practical rules of thumb

  • Avoid intubation and deep sedation unless strongly indicated (refractory hypoxia despite non-invasive oxygenation, or airway protection).
  • If sedation/intubation is unavoidable: optimise preload, start or escalate vasopressor/inotrope before induction, use the smallest effective dose, and have VA-ECMO or emergency bypass pathways pre-activated.
  • Use HFNC for hypoxaemia in preference to conventional nasal cannula.
  • No published studies have examined outcomes of NIV or invasive mechanical ventilation specifically in acute PE; the recommendation is based on physiological reasoning and observational data.
6 Haemodynamic Pharmacotherapy

Recommendations

  • COR 1 · C-LD For cardiogenic shock due to acute PE (D2–E2), use vasopressors and/or inotropes to improve cardiac output and systemic perfusion.
  • COR 2b · C-LD In Categories D1–2 with concerns for reduced preload, volume management with normal saline or other volume expanders may be considered to improve cardiac output and blood pressure.
  • COR 2b · B-R In Categories C2–E, inhaled pulmonary vasodilators may be considered to reduce RV afterload.

Inhaled pulmonary vasodilators RV afterload reduction Inhaled nitric oxide did not improve the primary composite outcome in a multicentre RCT of normotensive PE with RV dysfunction, but post-hoc analysis showed improved RV size and function at 24 h. Non-selective vasodilators (oral sildenafil, IV epoprostenol) showed minimal or no benefit.
7 Mechanical Circulatory Support (VA-ECMO)

Recommendations

  • COR 1 · B-NR In patients with known or suspected acute PE on VA-ECMO, continue parenteral systemic anticoagulation in the absence of bleeding to prevent further thrombotic or embolic complications.
  • COR 2a · B-NR In acute, refractory cardiogenic shock due to known or suspected acute PE (Category E2), institute VA-ECMO — provided appropriate resources are available — to stabilise haemodynamics and improve oxygenation.
  • COR 2b · C-LD In Category E2 on VA-ECMO, the usefulness of additional advanced therapies (MT, CDL) is not well established.

Physiology and practical points

  • VA-ECMO rapidly decreases RV preload while delivering oxygenated blood to peripheral tissues and end organs, halting the effects of hypoxia and cardiogenic shock.
  • Risks and benefits of continued anticoagulation on mechanical support should be constantly re-evaluated. Bleeding complications are more frequent than thrombotic complications on VA-ECMO; limited reports of ECMO without anticoagulation have not shown increased thrombosis.
  • The caveat is especially relevant in PE, where many patients have already received systemic anticoagulation or thrombolysis before ECMO initiation.
  • Surgical embolectomy is not recommended over other advanced therapies in Category E2 patients not on mechanical circulatory support (COR 3: No Benefit · B-NR).
  • In surgical series, Category E2 patients account for most postoperative deaths, largely from anoxic brain injury after preoperative cardiac arrest.

Systemic thrombolysis — key points

  • COR 2a · C-LD Categories E1–2 with acceptable bleeding risk: systemic thrombolysis plus anticoagulation is reasonable over anticoagulation alone to reduce mortality and recurrent PE.
  • COR 2b · C-LD Categories D1–2 with acceptable bleeding risk: may be considered to prevent further clinical deterioration.
  • COR 2b · C-LD Category C3 with acceptable bleeding risk: benefit is uncertain.
  • COR 3 · Harm · B-R Categories A1–C2: should not be used over anticoagulation alone due to increased major bleeding and ICH.
  • COR 2b · C-LD Lower-dose systemic thrombolytics (e.g. 25–50 mg rt-PA) may be considered to reduce bleeding risk. Standard dose is rt-PA 100 mg over 2 hours.
  • In the PEITHO trial (C3–D2 characteristics): extracranial bleeding 6.3% vs 1.2%; stroke 2.4% vs 0.2% (haemorrhagic in 10 of 12).
  • Meta-analysis of C3–D2 patients: systemic thrombolysis reduced all-cause mortality (OR 0.53) at the expense of excess ICH (OR 4.6); NNT 59, NNH 78.

Catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDL)

  • COR 2a · C-LD Category E1: CDL plus anticoagulation is reasonable to prevent further clinical deterioration and early mortality.
  • COR 2b · B-NR Categories D1–2: may be considered to prevent further clinical deterioration.
  • COR 2b · C-LD Categories C2–3: benefit over anticoagulation alone is unclear.
  • COR 2b · C-LD Categories D1–E1: efficacy of CDL vs systemic thrombolysis is unclear, but CDL may be considered over systemic thrombolysis to reduce major bleeding.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · B-NR A reduced thrombolytic dose of <5 mg alteplase per PA is not recommended over 5–10 mg per PA.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · C-EO Categories A–C1: CDL is not recommended over anticoagulation alone.

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT)

  • COR 2a · B-NR Category E1: reasonable to choose MT plus anticoagulation over anticoagulation alone.
  • COR 2b · B-NR Categories D1–2: may be considered over anticoagulation alone.
  • COR 2b · C-LD Categories C2–3: benefit is unclear.
  • COR 2b · B-NR Categories D1–E1: MT may be considered over systemic thrombolysis to reduce major bleeding risk.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · C-EO Categories A–C1: MT is not recommended.
  • Advantages over other advanced therapies: no concomitant lytic, no indwelling catheter or mandatory post-procedure ICU stay, and older thrombus can sometimes be removed.

Surgical embolectomy

  • COR 2a · B-NR Category E1: reasonable over anticoagulation alone to prevent clinical decompensation and acute mortality.
  • COR 2b · C-LD Categories D1–2: may be considered over anticoagulation alone.
  • COR 2b · B-NR Categories D1–E1 who are surgical candidates: benefits over systemic thrombolysis are unclear, but surgery may be considered over systemic thrombolysis to reduce the risk of ICH.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · C-EO Categories A–C3: not recommended over anticoagulation alone.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · B-NR Category E2 not on mechanical circulatory support: not recommended over other advanced therapies for preventing short-term mortality.
  • Performed on cardiopulmonary bypass, typically via sternotomy, with infrequent aortic cross-clamping. Survival >97% reported among patients not requiring preoperative CPR.

Clot-in-transit

  • COR 2a · C-LD In Categories C3–E2 with free-floating right atrial and/or RV clot-in-transit, advanced therapies over anticoagulation alone are reasonable to reduce the risk of clinical deterioration.
  • Found in 2–4% of patients with acute PE. In the PERT Consortium Registry, clot-in-transit was independently associated with mortality (OR 2.26) in patients consistent with Category E.

CDL vs MT head-to-head

  • COR 2b · B-R In Categories C2–D2 without contraindication to thrombolysis, when advanced therapy is being considered, the usefulness of either CDL or MT over the other is uncertain for reduction in mortality or major bleeding.
  • PEERLESS trial (n = 550, C2–D2): no significant difference in 30-day mortality or major bleeding. The composite of clinical deterioration plus physician-driven bailout to additional advanced therapy was more frequent with CDL; protocol-defined clinical deterioration alone was not significantly different.
9 Anticoagulation — Acute & Peri-Procedural

General

  • COR 1 · B-R In acute PE without an absolute contraindication, start anticoagulation to reduce recurrent VTE and death.
  • COR 1 · B-R In Categories C1–E1 requiring initial parenteral therapy, LMWH is recommended over UFH to reduce recurrent VTE and major bleeding.
  • COR 1 · B-R If eligible for oral anticoagulation, DOACs are recommended over VKAs unless contraindicated.
  • COR 2a · C-EO In suspected acute PE Category C2 or higher with low bleeding risk, it may be beneficial to give therapeutic anticoagulation when imaging is delayed or not immediately accessible.

Endovascular procedures & thrombolysis

  • COR 1 · C-LD During CDL, concurrent therapeutic (LMWH or UFH) or subtherapeutic (UFH) anticoagulation is recommended over no anticoagulation to prevent recurrent PE.
  • COR 1 · B-NR After an endovascular procedure or thrombolytic therapy, initial parenteral anticoagulation with LMWH is preferred over UFH for reliable therapeutic anticoagulation and reduced recurrent VTE.
  • COR 2a · C-LD If endovascular procedures are likely, LMWH is reasonable over UFH.

Monitoring

  • COR 1 · C-LD When LMWH is monitored, measure a peak anti-Xa level 3–5 hours after the dose at steady state (≥3 doses) in preference to trough or random levels.
  • COR 2a · C-LD In severe CKD (CrCl <30 mL/min) treated with LMWH, monitor anti-Xa to guide dose adjustment and reduce bleeding risk.
  • COR 2b · C-LD In pregnancy on LMWH, the usefulness of monitoring peak anti-Xa at least once per trimester is not well established.
  • In ICU/critically ill patients, the benefit of anti-Xa monitoring is not established to avoid supra- or subtherapeutic levels.

10 IVC Filters
  • COR 1 · B-R In acute PE where anticoagulation cannot be tolerated but an IVC filter is deemed necessary, retrievable filters are recommended over permanent filters to reduce short-term recurrent PE while minimising long-term adverse outcomes.
  • COR 1 · C-LD With retrievable filters, attempt retrieval as soon as the risk of PE has sufficiently decreased and anticoagulation is tolerated, to minimise long-term filter-related complications.
  • COR 2a · B-R In acute PE where anticoagulation cannot be tolerated, IVC filters can be useful to reduce short-term recurrent PE.
  • COR 2a · C-LD A structured follow-up programme is reasonable to increase retrieval rates and detect complications.
  • COR 2b · C-LD In Categories D–E undergoing advanced interventions (systemic lysis, CDL, MT, surgical embolectomy), the benefit of IVC filter placement is uncertain for reducing short-term recurrent PE and mortality.
  • COR 2b · C-LD In recurrent PE despite optimal therapeutic anticoagulation (Categories B–E), IVC filter placement may be considered.
  • COR 3 · Harm · A In therapeutically anticoagulated acute PE, routine IVC filter placement should not be performed.

FDA safety communication recommends retrieval within 29–54 days once PE risk has subsided. Difficult retrievals become more common after 50 days; failures more frequent after 90 days. Successful retrievals averaged 85 days dwell time vs 145 days for unsuccessful ones.

11 Level of Care, Interhospital Transfer & PERT

Placement in hospital

  • COR 1 · C-EO In acute PE receiving thrombolytic therapy (systemic or CDL), placement in a unit that can provide close monitoring — ICU or intermediate level of care — is recommended to monitor for adverse events.
  • COR 2a · B-R In haemodynamically stable patients admitted for mechanical thrombectomy, admission to a level of care with continuous telemetry and nursing familiar with post-procedural complications of the device used is reasonable. In PEERLESS, >60% of MT patients received post-procedural care in non-ICU settings with very low adverse event rates.

Interhospital transfer

  • COR 2b · C-LD For acute PE with high-risk features (RV dysfunction, elevated cardiac biomarkers) but haemodynamically stable (Categories C3–D), transfer to a centre that can provide advanced therapies may be considered.
  • COR 3 · Harm · C-EO Unstable patients (Category E) should not be transferred to another centre before stabilising their condition. Widely available life-saving interventions (e.g. IV thrombolysis) should not be delayed for transfer.

Pulmonary Embolism Response Team (PERT)

  • COR 1 · B-NR In acute PE at increased risk of adverse outcomes (Categories C–E), a multidisciplinary PERT assessment is recommended to improve in-hospital clinical care delivery.
  • Categories A or B with multiple comorbidities may also benefit (e.g. Category B with intracranial haemorrhage).

12 Recurrent / Breakthrough PE on Anticoagulation
  • COR 1 · C-EO In patients with a history of PE presenting with new symptoms/signs suggestive of recurrent or breakthrough PE, radiographic imaging with CTPA or V/Q scan is recommended to objectively confirm or exclude the diagnosis.
  • COR 1 · C-LD In documented recurrent PE despite anticoagulation, evaluate for clinical and pharmacological factors that may contribute (adherence, dosing, interactions, cancer, antiphospholipid antibodies, HIT, antithrombin deficiency, subtherapeutic levels, GI surgery/absorption).
  • COR 2a · C-EO In recurrent PE despite adherence to therapeutic-intensity anticoagulation, changing to an alternative drug class rather than continuing the same class is reasonable.
  • COR 2a · C-EO In documented recurrent PE while on a reduced-dose DOAC, it is reasonable to anticoagulate with a full-dose DOAC within the same class.
  • COR 2a · B-NR In cancer with recurrent PE despite therapeutic LMWH, escalate LMWH dose by 20–25% to prevent future recurrence.

Recurrence occurs in ~2% of anticoagulated patients with VTE (2.4% DOACs vs 2.6% VKA in contemporary trials). A negative D-dimer may support exclusion of recurrent PE in anticoagulated patients, but an elevated D-dimer should not be used alone to diagnose recurrence.

13 Post-Acute PE — Follow-Up, Activity & Chronic Sequelae

Follow-up

  • COR 1 · C-LD Clinical follow-up within the first week of discharge to provide education, address barriers to anticoagulation, ensure adherence, and detect bleeding.
  • COR 1 · C-EO A clinical visit at or before 3 months to discuss anticoagulation duration, need for further testing, and persistent PE-related symptoms.
  • COR 1 · C-LD Ask about PE-related symptoms and functional limitations at every visit for at least 1 year to screen for CTEPD or other causes of dyspnoea/functional limitation.
  • COR 1 · B-NR If anticoagulation continues into the extended phase (beyond 3–6 months), periodically reassess the risk–benefit of continuing.
  • COR 2a · B-NR Use general or disease-specific questionnaires to screen for anxiety and depression at follow-up.
  • COR 2a · B-NR In select complex patients, follow-up in a specialised PE clinic is reasonable.
  • COR 2b · B-NR If still symptomatic at 3–6 months, a performance test (6-minute walk test, incremental/endurance shuttle walk test) may be reasonable to quantify limitation.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · A Routine CT or PET-CT imaging to diagnose undetected cancer is not recommended; instead obtain history, examination, and age-appropriate cancer screening.

Activity & travel

  • COR 2a · A Encourage early ambulation rather than bed rest to reduce complications.
  • COR 2a · B-R Use compression stockings during long-haul (≥5 h) travel to lower DVT risk.
  • COR 2b · C-EO For travel/immobility-related PE not currently on anticoagulation, a one-time prophylactic dose of oral anticoagulant or parenteral LMWH on the day of long-haul travel may be reasonable.
  • COR 2b · C-LD For Categories C2–E recovering from acute PE, it may be reasonable to restrict long-haul travel for 4 weeks after treatment initiation or until symptoms resolve.

Persistently symptomatic patients — CTEPD evaluation

  • COR 1 · B-NR In ongoing dyspnoea and/or functional impairment after ≥3 months of therapeutic anticoagulation, perform a diagnostic evaluation for CTEPD.
  • COR 2a · B-NR Obtain both TTE and a lung perfusion scan (planar V/Q, V/Q SPECT, or SPECT/CT) rather than echocardiogram alone.
  • COR 2a · B-NR Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is reasonable to exclude CTEPD.
  • COR 3 · No Benefit · B-NR In resolved symptoms with low suspicion for CTEPD, follow-up CTPA or lung perfusion scan is not beneficial.
  • COR 1 · B-NR Continue anticoagulation until the CTEPD evaluation is complete unless contraindicated by high bleeding risk.
  • COR 1 · B-R If CTEPD is excluded but dyspnoea/functional impairment persists, a pulmonary rehabilitation programme is reasonable.
  • COR 1 · C-LD CTEPD with PH (CTEPH) → refer to a centre with expertise in managing CTEPD.
  • COR 2a · C-LD Select patients with CTEPD without PH may also benefit from referral to an expert centre.

CTEPH complicates approximately 3% of acute PEs; CTEPD without PH is at least as prevalent. TTE alone is insufficient — about one-third of confirmed CTEPH patients had a normal TTE. The negative predictive value of planar V/Q to exclude CTEPD approaches 100%.

Extended phase — dose reduction Half-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban recommended to reduce bleeding risk 1 · A
Extended phase — anticoagulation contraindicated or refused Low-dose aspirin over no therapy is reasonable 2a · B-R

Initial treatment phase = first 3–6 months after acute PE. Extended treatment phase = more than 6 months, without an anticipated stop date.
