1 Definitions & Thresholds What counts as bradycardia, AV block and "symptomatic"
Bottom line: There is no single heart-rate or pause threshold that mandates pacing. In sinus node dysfunction (SND) the decision is driven by a temporal correlation between symptoms and bradycardia. In acquired Mobitz II, high-grade or third-degree AV block (not reversible/physiologic), pacing is indicated regardless of symptoms.
  • Bradycardia (NIH): heart rate <60 bpm in adults (other than well-trained athletes). Population studies often use <50 bpm.
  • This guideline: a sinus rate <50 bpm and/or a sinus pause >3 s may be components of the definition of SND — but either alone does not diagnose SND.
  • Healthy elderly patients can have 2-second pauses; long-distance runners can have 3-second pauses on 24-h monitoring — often physiologic.
  • Symptomatic bradycardia = documented bradyarrhythmia directly responsible for syncope/presyncope, transient dizziness or lightheadedness, heart-failure symptoms, or confusional states from cerebral hypoperfusion due to slow heart rate.

Atropine & beta agonists for bradycardia attributable to SND
  • IIa C-LD In SND with symptoms or haemodynamic compromise, atropine is reasonable to increase sinus rate.
  • IIb C-LD In SND with symptoms or haemodynamic compromise and low likelihood of coronary ischaemia, isoproterenol, dopamine, dobutamine or epinephrine may be considered.
  • III: Harm C-LD In heart-transplant patients without evidence of autonomic reinnervation, atropine should not be used to treat sinus bradycardia.

Atropine has a bimodal sinoatrial response: doses <0.5 mg may paradoxically slow the rate; 0.5–2 mg accelerates it. Half-life ≈ 2 h. Dopamine at 5–20 mcg/kg/min is chronotropic and inotropic; higher doses risk vasoconstriction and proarrhythmia.

Acute medical therapy for bradycardia attributable to AV block
  • IIa C-LD Second- or third-degree AV block believed to be at the AV nodal level with symptoms or haemodynamic compromise — atropine is reasonable.
  • IIb B-NR Second- or third-degree AV block with symptoms or haemodynamic compromise and low likelihood of coronary ischaemia — beta-adrenergic agonists (isoproterenol, dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine) may be considered.
  • IIb C-LD Second- or third-degree AV block with symptoms or haemodynamic compromise in acute inferior MI — IV aminophylline may be considered.
Atropine caveat: unlikely to improve block at the His bundle or His-Purkinje level. Isolated reports describe worsened AV conduction and haemodynamic compromise. Use judiciously when the QRS is wide (suggesting significant His-Purkinje disease).
Digoxin-mediated bradycardia — key corrections applied
  • IIa C-LD Digoxin Fab antibody fragment is reasonable for bradycardia with symptoms or haemodynamic compromise in digoxin toxicity.
  • III: No Benefit C-LD Dialysis is not recommended for digoxin removal (only slightly dialysable). Haemodialysis may be considered for life-threatening hyperkalaemia.
2019 correction applied: patients with hyperkalaemia or arrhythmias in the setting of digoxin serum levels >2 mcg/L are at increased risk of death. (The original text incorrectly read "life-threatening".)

Complications of temporary transvenous pacing: 14–40%. Venous thrombosis (18–85% with femoral access), pulmonary emboli (50–60% with femoral approach), life-threatening arrhythmias, loss of capture (10–37%), perforation and death. Risk of infection of a subsequent permanent pacemaker is increased if a temporary wire has been in situ.
  • Insertion technique matters: balloon-flotation catheters, internal jugular access and fluoroscopic/echo guidance are associated with lower complication rates than semirigid fixed-curve catheters and blind placement.
  • Duration matters: complications rise when wires remain >48 h. Use the minimum duration necessary, and place by the most experienced available operator.
  • If AV block is felt to be irreversible and permanent pacing capability is available, it may be best to avoid temporary pacing altogether and proceed directly to permanent implantation.
  • Transcutaneous pacing: painful (sedation/analgesia usually required); capture must be confirmed by pulse or arterial waveform, not by ECG alone. Best used as a short-term bridge.
  • Externalised permanent lead ("temporary permanent pacemaker"): lower dislodgement and complication rates; allows mobilisation out of ICU; cost-saving after 1–2 days; no infections reported with reusable sterilised generators.
5 Perioperative & Post-Cardiac-Surgery Management Section 8.1 — the anaesthetist's core content
Non-cardiac surgery — patients at risk of intraoperative bradycardia
  • IIa B-NR In patients thought to be at high risk of intraoperative or periprocedural bradycardia (patient characteristics or procedure type), placement of transcutaneous pacing pads is reasonable.
  • III: Harm B-NR In patients with LBBB requiring pulmonary artery catheterisation for intraoperative monitoring, routine prophylactic temporary transvenous pacing should not be performed.

Patient risk factors

  • Age >60–65 years
  • ASA Class III or IV
  • Baseline heart rate <60 bpm or blood pressure <110/60 mmHg
  • Concomitant beta-blockers or renin–angiotensin system blocking drugs

Procedure risk factors

  • Maxillofacial surgery; carotid endarterectomy or stenting
  • Neurosurgery involving the spine or dura mater (trigeminal cardiac reflex)
  • Peritoneal insufflation during laparoscopic/abdominal surgery
Intraoperative bradycardia is most commonly attributable to SND and only rarely to worsening AV conduction.
LBBB + pulmonary artery catheter: complete heart block can occur, but the incidence is low. Prophylactic transvenous pacing is not recommended (temporary pacing itself carries an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias). Instead, be prepared to manage complete heart block with rapid initiation of transvenous pacing, or immediate transcutaneous pacing if sustained rate support is required.
Isolated coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)
  • I B-NR New postoperative SND or AV block with persistent symptoms or haemodynamic instability that does not resolve → permanent pacing before discharge.
  • IIa B-NR Routine placement of temporary epicardial pacing wires is reasonable.
  • IIb C-EO In patients likely to require future CRT or ventricular pacing, intraoperative placement of a permanent epicardial LV lead may be considered.

Incidence of conduction defects after isolated CABG ranges 2–58%. Timing of permanent implant has not been formally studied but 5–7 days postoperatively is reasonable. Patients without diabetes, preoperative arrhythmia, or pacing requirement while coming off bypass had only a 2.6% need for postsurgical temporary pacing (vs 8.6% overall).

Surgery for atrial fibrillation
  • I B-NR Routine placement of temporary epicardial pacing wires is recommended.
  • I B-NR New postoperative SND or AV block with symptoms or haemodynamic instability that does not resolve → permanent pacing before discharge.
  • IIb C-EO Intraoperative permanent epicardial LV lead may be considered if future CRT/ventricular pacing is likely.

Postoperative pacing need is usually for SND; AV block is uncommon. Meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found no difference in permanent pacemaker need with additional AF surgery.

Valve surgery — aortic, mitral, tricuspid

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR)
  • I B-NR New AV block after TAVR with symptoms or haemodynamic instability that does not resolve → permanent pacing before discharge.

Numbers to know:

  • New LBBB in 19–55%; new high-degree AV block in ~10%.
  • Up to half of new bundle branch block and complete heart block resolve before discharge.
  • High-degree AV block persists beyond 48 h in 2–20%.
  • Only about half of patients who receive a new PPM after TAVR are pacemaker-dependent at follow-up.
  • New RBBB is associated with increased PPM need and increased late all-cause and cardiac mortality — independent of whether a PPM was implanted.
  • Persistent new LBBB increases PPM need; studies are mixed on late mortality. Early PPM for new LBBB is not protective against increased mortality.
  • In 29% of patients with new LBBB, the first episode of high-degree AV block occurs after discharge — careful surveillance for bradycardia is appropriate. No specific monitoring protocol has proven superior.

Pre-TAVR predictors of PPM: pre-existing RBBB, increased prosthesis-to-LVOT ratio, increased LV end-diastolic diameter. An HV interval ≥65 ms after TAVR is modestly predictive of high-grade AV block with new LBBB (sens 80%, spec 79%).

Heart transplant, surgical myectomy & alcohol septal ablation
  • Heart transplant: with bicaval (rather than biatrial) anastomoses, the pacemaker rate has fallen from 10–14% to 2–4%. SND remains the most common cause of bradycardia. Atropine is ineffective (denervation) and may cause paradoxical block/asystole — use theophylline/aminophylline instead.
  • Surgical myectomy / alcohol septal ablation for HCM:
    • I B-NR Persistent Mobitz II, high-grade, or persistent complete AV block after alcohol septal ablation or surgical myectomy → permanent pacing before discharge.
    • IIa B-NR If pacing is required and the patient is at high risk of sudden cardiac death with meaningful survival >1 year, a device with defibrillator capability is reasonable.
    • IIb C-LD Prolonged ambulatory ECG monitoring may be considered after alcohol septal ablation in those at risk of late AV block.
    • IIb C-LD EPS evaluation of ventricular conduction at the time of alcohol septal ablation may be considered.

Risk of abnormal AV conduction: ~10–33% with alcohol septal ablation, 3–4% with surgical myectomy (up to 10% if baseline conduction abnormalities are present). RBBB develops in ~60% after alcohol septal ablation; LBBB in up to 90% after surgical myectomy. Transient AV block after alcohol septal ablation occurs in 15–50% and usually resolves within 24 h.

Bradycardia with postoperative atrial fibrillation
  • Postoperative AF peaks at 2–4 days; overall incidence 10–65%; more common after valve surgery than isolated CABG.
  • A slow and regular ventricular response during AF usually indicates complete AV block — pacing may be required if it does not resolve.
  • In general, treat bradyarrhythmias with postoperative AF the same way as in the non-operative setting — a period of watchful waiting is preferred over early PPM implantation.
  • Occasionally, a PPM is required to permit adequate rate-control drug therapy in refractory AF with rapid ventricular response where significant SND limits drug use.
6 Bradycardia in Acute Myocardial Infarction Section 8.3 — waiting periods and what not to pace
ClassRecommendation
I B-NRIn acute MI, temporary pacing is indicated for medically refractory symptomatic or haemodynamically significant bradycardia related to SND or AV block.
I B-NRPatients presenting with SND or AV block in the setting of acute MI should undergo a waiting period before determining the need for permanent pacing.
I B-NRAcute MI with second-degree Mobitz II, high-grade AV block, alternating bundle branch block, or third-degree AV block (persistent or infranodal) → permanent pacing after a waiting period.
IIa B-NRAcute MI with symptomatic or haemodynamically significant sinus bradycardia or AV block at the level of the AV node → atropine is reasonable.
III: Harm B-NRAcute MI with transient AV block that resolves → permanent pacing should not be performed.
III: Harm B-NRAcute MI with a new bundle branch block or isolated fascicular block in the absence of second- or third-degree AV block → permanent pacing should not be performed.
Key principle: temporary pacing does not by itself constitute an indication for permanent pacing. Avoid early permanent pacing (<72 h) to allow for recovery of AV conduction and to avoid unnecessary implants. Long-term prognosis after MI with AV block is driven mainly by the extent of myocardial injury and the character of intraventricular conduction disturbance, not by the AV block itself.
  • Inferior MI with AV block may be due to transient vagal tone increase or reduced blood supply to the AV node — often reversible.
  • Anterior MI with AV conduction impairment carries a worse prognosis and higher mortality than inferior MI with a similar presentation.
  • Atropine is safe at the AV nodal level but can worsen infranodal conduction disease.
  • Given difficulty confirming reliable myocardial capture, transcutaneous pacing should only be used if other temporary methods are delayed or unavailable.
  • Consider a CIED with defibrillator capability if there is a pacing requirement plus low LVEF, per other societal statements.
7 Special Populations Spinal cord injury · sleep apnoea · neuromuscular · epilepsy · ACHD
Sleep apnoea & nocturnal bradycardia
  • I B-NR Documented or suspected bradycardia/conduction disorder during sleep → screening for sleep apnoea symptoms is recommended, with confirmatory testing directed by clinical suspicion.
  • I B-NR Sleep-related bradycardia/conduction disorder with documented obstructive sleep apnoea → treatment directed at the sleep apnoea (e.g. CPAP, weight loss) is recommended.
  • IIa B-NR In patients who have previously received or are being considered for a PPM for bradycardia/conduction disorder, screening for sleep apnoea is reasonable.

Nocturnal bradyarrhythmias are common and usually physiologic/vagally mediated. CPAP reduces episodes of profound sinus bradycardia, prolonged sinus pauses and AV block by 72–89% and eliminates the need for pacing in most patients. Nocturnal bradycardia is not in itself an indication for permanent pacing.

Spinal cord injury
  • IIa C-LD SND with symptoms or haemodynamic compromise in the setting of acute spinal cord injury — aminophylline or theophylline is reasonable to increase heart rate and improve symptoms.

Injury above T6 causes autonomic dysreflexia with unopposed parasympathetic (vagal) drive. Severe sinus bradycardia is common in the acute phase; bradyarrhythmias relate to the level and severity of injury. Cardiac arrest (usually sinus arrest/asystole) occurs in ~16% of severe cervical injuries within the first 2–4 weeks. Common triggers: tracheal suctioning, turning the patient, bladder catheterisation. Bradycardia usually resolves after a few weeks or removal of the noxious stimulus. Treatment can usually be withdrawn after 4–6 weeks.

Heart transplant
  • Atropine is contraindicated — denervated heart; paradoxical complete AV block or sinus arrest occurs in ~20%.
  • IIa C-LD Aminophylline or theophylline is reasonable to increase heart rate if clinically indicated.
  • Sinus bradycardia is common post-transplant; rates <70–80 bpm may be inadequate for postoperative demand.
  • Sinus bradycardia attributable to autonomic denervation, surgical trauma, ischaemia, rejection and prior amiodarone use.
Neuromuscular disorders
  • I B-NR Neuromuscular disease with conduction disorders (e.g. myotonic dystrophy type 1, Kearns-Sayre syndrome) with evidence of second-degree AV block, third-degree AV block, or an HV interval ≥70 ms — regardless of symptoms — permanent pacing (with defibrillator capability if needed and meaningful survival >1 year) is recommended.
  • IIa B-NR Lamin A/C gene mutations (including limb-girdle and Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophies) with PR >240 ms and LBBB — permanent pacing with defibrillator capability is reasonable.
  • IIb C-LD Neuromuscular disease (e.g. myotonic dystrophy type 1) with PR >240 ms, QRS >120 ms, or fascicular block — permanent pacing may be considered.

Up to 20% of myotonic dystrophy type 1 patients have AV block on ECG or intermittent second-/third-degree AV block on 24-h monitoring. >50% with a normal ECG may have infra-Hisian block at EPS. 46.7% of patients with HV ≥70 ms develop high-grade AV block. A large retrospective study showed a 75% lower risk of sudden death in those with a pacemaker.

Epilepsy / ictal asystole
  • IIa C-LD Epilepsy with severe symptomatic bradycardia (ictal bradycardia) where antiepileptic medications are ineffective — permanent pacing is reasonable for reducing symptom severity.

Occurs in ~0.15–0.3% of seizure patients; most commonly temporal lobe origin. Pauses are often >10 s; one study found syncope only occurred with asystole >6 s. Optimise antiepileptic treatment or surgery first. Pacing does not affect any accompanying vasodepressor effect.

Adult congenital heart disease (ACHD)
  • I B-NR Symptomatic SND or chronotropic incompetence → atrial-based permanent pacing.
  • I B-NR Symptomatic bradycardia related to AV block → permanent pacing.
  • I B-NR Congenital complete AV block with any symptomatic bradycardia, wide QRS escape rhythm, mean daytime HR <50 bpm, complex ventricular ectopy, or ventricular dysfunction → permanent pacing.
  • I B-NR Postoperative Mobitz II, high-grade or third-degree AV block not expected to resolve → permanent pacing.
  • IIa B-NR Asymptomatic congenital complete AV block → permanent pacing is reasonable.
  • IIa B-NR Repaired ACHD requiring pacing for bradycardic indications → device with atrial antitachycardia pacing capabilities is reasonable.
  • IIa C-EO Pre-existing sinus node and/or AV conduction disease undergoing cardiac surgery → intraoperative epicardial permanent pacing leads are reasonable.
  • III: Harm B-NR Selected adults with ACHD and venous-to-systemic intracardiac shunts → endocardial pacing leads are potentially harmful.

After surgery, avoid early implantation (<72 h). Patients remain at high risk of permanent heart block if conduction has not resumed within 72 h postoperatively.

Choice of pacing mode — practical points
  • I B-R Symptomatic SND → atrial-based pacing is recommended over single-chamber ventricular pacing.
  • I B-R Symptomatic SND with intact AV conduction and no conduction abnormality → dual-chamber or single-chamber atrial pacing.
  • IIa B-R Dual-chamber pacemaker with intact AV conduction → programming to minimise ventricular pacing is reasonable.
  • IIa C-EO SND where frequent ventricular pacing is not expected, or significant comorbidities likely to determine survival — single-chamber ventricular pacing is reasonable.
  • I A SND and AV block requiring pacing → dual-chamber pacing over single-chamber ventricular pacing.
  • I A Selected AV block patients where frequent ventricular pacing is not expected, or with significant comorbidities → single-chamber ventricular pacing is effective.
  • I B-R Sinus rhythm with a single-chamber ventricular pacemaker who develops pacemaker syndrome → upgrade to dual chamber.
  • IIa B-R AV block with pacing indication, LVEF 36–50%, expected ventricular pacing >40% → physiologic ventricular activation (CRT or His bundle pacing) preferred over RV pacing.
  • IIa B-R AV block with pacing indication, LVEF 36–50%, expected ventricular pacing <40% → RV pacing preferred over CRT/His bundle pacing.
  • IIb B-R AV block at the AV nodal level with pacing indication → His bundle pacing may be considered.
Before implanting — assess ventricular arrhythmia risk
  • I B-NR In patients who require permanent pacing therapy, before implantation, perform an assessment of the risk of future ventricular arrhythmias and the need for an ICD.

Patients with symptomatic bradycardia from AV block who also have heart failure symptoms with LVEF ≤35%, myotonic dystrophy, lamin A/C cardiomyopathy, cardiac sarcoidosis, or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy are at increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, and may benefit from a device with antitachycardia pacing or defibrillation capability. ICDs carry higher complication rates than PPMs — individualise via shared decision-making.

Shared decision-making & end-of-life
  • I C-LD Clinicians and patients should engage in shared decision-making — decisions based on the best available evidence and the patient's goals of care, preferences and values.
  • I C-LD Patients considering implantation, or with a pacemaker requiring lead revision or generator change, should be informed of procedural benefits and risks, including short- and long-term complications and possible alternatives.
  • III: No Benefit C-LD Where significant comorbidities make meaningful clinical benefit unlikely, or where goals of care strongly preclude pacemaker therapy, implantation or replacement should not be performed.
  • IIa C-LD At generator replacement or management of pacemaker complications where the original indication has resolved or is in question, discontinuation is reasonable after evaluation of symptoms during a monitored period with pacing off.
  • Patients with decision-making capacity (or their legally defined surrogate) have the right to refuse or request withdrawal of pacemaker therapy, even if pacemaker dependent. This should be considered palliative end-of-life care, not physician-assisted suicide.
