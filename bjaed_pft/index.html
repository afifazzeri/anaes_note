1 · What PFTs measure Overview

Pulmonary function tests assess three domains of the respiratory system. Know which domain a test interrogates before you interpret it.

DomainWhat it testsTypical tests
Respiratory mechanicsMechanical behaviour of lungs + chest wall as air moves in and outSpirometry, static lung volumes, flow–volume loops, resistance
Parenchymal lung functionGas transfer across the alveolar–capillary membraneTransfer factor (TLCO / DLCO), KCO, VA
Cardiopulmonary interactionIntegrated reserve of heart + lungsWalking tests, cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET)
Perioperative bottom line PFTs describe physiology, not risk. In elective non-thoracic surgery they are poor predictors of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) — clinical factors perform better. Their real value is in thoracic surgery, disease severity grading, and monitoring progressive neuromuscular respiratory failure.
2 · How measurements are made (what matters clinically) Methods
Pressure
Transducers inside the breathing system convert pressure to an electrical signal — small, accurate, fast enough for respiratory measurement.
Flow
Usually a pneumotachograph: a small known resistance in the circuit; the pressure drop across it relates to flow. Resistance is imperceptible to the patient. Mechanical devices are largely obsolete, but handheld peak-flow meters (variable orifice) remain useful for asthma monitoring.
Volume
Spirometer (water-sealed or dry bellows) · impeller/turbine (e.g. Wright respirometer) · integration of flow over time (standard in anaesthetic machines and ventilators) · body plethysmography · chest/abdominal impedance belts (no mouthpiece; usable during sleep; can detect airway obstruction).
BTPS correction All techniques measure flow/volume at ambient temperature and pressure. Results must be converted to the volume the gas would occupy at body temperature, ambient pressure, saturated with water vapour (BTPS) before comparison with reference values.

Anaesthetic relevance: flow integration is how your anaesthetic machine and ventilator display tidal volume and minute ventilation. Impedance belts and respirometers are the tools available when a mouthpiece is impractical (paediatrics, sleep studies, ICU).

Static volumes are measured when no gas is flowing. Two volumes combined are called a capacity.

Measuring FRC / RV — three techniques

TechniquePrinciplePractical points
Helium dilution
(closed circuit)		 Helium is inert and almost insoluble in blood, so it equilibrates between spirometer and lung without being lost.
FRC = Vspiro × (FHe1 − FHe2) / FHe2		 Start at end-expiration. Connect at maximal exhalation instead → measures RV directly. Result termed FRCHe.
Nitrogen washout Breathe 100% O₂ from FRC; the nitrogen in the lung is washed out over ~7 min. Exhaled N₂ volume ÷ initial alveolar N₂ fraction = FRC. Correct for tissue N₂ excretion. Requires a leak-free circuit and ~7 min of cooperation.
Body plethysmography Patient sits in a sealed fixed-volume box breathing from outside. Pressure changes in the box equate to changes in lung volume (Boyle's law). Measures thoracic gas volume — includes gas trapped behind closed airways. Also needed to interpret resistance measurements.
Trap Gas dilution techniques underestimate lung volume when there is significant air trapping (emphysema, severe asthma) because trapped gas does not equilibrate. Plethysmography gives the larger, more accurate value in these patients.
4 · Dynamic lung volumes & the flow–volume loop Mechanics

Dynamic volumes are continuous measurements of inspiratory or expiratory flow against time or volume. The three highest values from at least three attempts are accepted; the best value is reported. Repeating after bronchodilator assesses reversibility.

FEV1
Forced expiratory volume in 1 second — the workhorse index of airflow obstruction and pulmonary reserve.
FVC
Forced vital capacity — reflects restrictive physiology and effort.
FEV1/FVC
Ratio — the key discriminator between obstructive (<70% / below LLN) and restrictive (normal or high) patterns.
PEF
Peak expiratory flow — effort dependent; useful for serial monitoring, not diagnosis.
Reading the loop in one line Expiratory scooping = small airway closure → obstruction. Symmetrical flattening = fixed large airway lesion. Narrow loop with preserved shape = restriction.
5 · Airway resistance Mechanics

Respiratory system resistance is the sum of the reluctance of the thoracic cage and lung tissue to change shape (tissue resistance) and resistance to gas flow through the airways (airway resistance, Raw).

  • Raw arises mainly from the larger airways (mouth, nose, larynx) — their total cross-sectional area is small compared with the huge combined area of the many small airways.
  • Flow is turbulent in the upper and large airways and becomes more laminar as velocity falls in small airways.
  • Airway calibre (and therefore resistance) varies with lung volume: resistance is low at high lung volume and high near RV.

6 · Transfer factor — TLCO / DLCO Parenchyma

Oxygen must diffuse across alveolar epithelium, interstitium, basement membrane, capillary endothelium, plasma, and the red cell membrane before combining with haemoglobin. The rate constant for this whole process is the transfer factor (TL) — more accurate than "diffusing capacity (DL)" because the final step is a chemical reaction, not diffusion, and the measurement is made at rest (so it is not a "best achieved" capacity).

Units
Europe: TLCO in mmol min⁻¹ kPa⁻¹. North America: DLCO in mL min⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹. ERS/ATS agreed on DLCO for uniformity, but both terms persist.
Gases
Oxygen (rarely used — end-capillary PO₂ cannot be measured directly), carbon monoxide (standard surrogate), nitric oxide (better reflects alveolar–capillary membrane properties; promising for the future).
Why carbon monoxide? CO is a foreign gas — venous blood contains negligible CO. Its reaction with Hb is so rapid and so slowly reversed that pulmonary end-capillary PCO can be assumed to be zero. So you only need to measure CO uptake and alveolar CO concentration.

CO uptake is two processes in series:

  • Membrane conductance (Dm) — diffusion across the available membrane
  • Reactive conductance (θ × Vc) — θ = rate constant for CO + Hb; Vc = volume of Hb in alveolar capillary blood

1 / TLCO = 1/Dm + 1/(θ × Vc)

Key indices

IndexMeaningUnits / notes
kCO (lower case k)Rate constant — the measured rate of change of CO concentrationmmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹
KCO (upper case K)kCO corrected for barometric pressure; rate of CO uptake per unit CO driving pressuremmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹ kPa⁻¹ — the transfer coefficient
VAAlveolar volume containing CO, measured by helium dilution during the single-breath testLitres (BTPS)
TLCOTotal CO uptake per unit time per unit driving pressureTLCO = KCO × VA

Single-breath technique (ERS-standardised)

  1. Exhale fully to RV.
  2. Inhale a mixture of 0.3% CO + helium (inert tracer) to near TLC.
  3. Breath-hold for 8–10 seconds.
  4. Exhale back to RV; after discarding about 750 mL (dead-space washout), sample alveolar gas and analyse.
  5. Helium dilution gives VA and allows calculation of the initial alveolar CO concentration, assuming CO is diluted to the same extent.

Indices obtained: initial and final alveolar CO concentrations, breath-hold time, and VA during breath-hold.

Interpretation — what changes TLCO

FactorEffect on TLCO
Age, sex, height, ethnicityAll affect predicted values — always compare with a matched reference population
HaemoglobinAnaemia lowers TLCO (reduced CO-carrying capacity). Labs report actual and Hb-adjusted values (adjusted to Hb 146 g/L in men; 134 g/L in women and children <15 yr).
Lung volumeTLCO rises with VA
Carboxyhaemoglobin (smoking)Raises the back-pressure → lowers measured TLCO
Inspired oxygen / altitudeAlters the driving pressure and reaction kinetics
Body positionSupine increases TLCO
The most useful interpretation point The primary measurements are KCO and VA; TLCO is their product. The same TLCO can arise from very different combinations:
  • Low KCO + low VA → emphysema (loss of surface area, ventilation–perfusion mismatch)
  • Low VA with preserved KCO → restrictive/chest wall disease
  • Low KCO with near-normal VA → pulmonary vascular disease, anaemia, early ILD
7 · Normal values, % predicted, LLN and z-scores Interpretation

Pulmonary function varies far more with sex, age, height and ethnicity than most blood tests. An individual result is only interpretable against a matched reference population.

  • Historically, normal values came from small, poorly powered studies — mostly Caucasian with a narrow age range — leading to conflicting predicted values.
  • In 2012 the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) produced spirometry reference equations from 74,187 healthy non-smokers (57% female), aged 3–95 yr, worldwide.
  • In 2017 the GLI published all-age reference values for TLCO in Caucasians.
StatisticDefinitionUse
Absolute volume (BTPS)The raw measurementRarely useful alone — too dependent on height and other factors. The old adage "an FEV1 over a litre is fine" is no longer tenable.
% predictedPatient value ÷ reference population mean × 100Standard for grading severity (e.g. GOLD stages)
LLNLower limit of normal — the 5th percentile of the reference populationBelow this = abnormal and clinically important. Recommended threshold.
z-scoreNumber of standard deviations from the reference mean. z = −1.72 means 1.72 SD below the mean.Better than % predicted for expressing how abnormal. z = −1.64 is equivalent to the LLN.
Why this matters at the bedside The same number can be normal for one patient and abnormal for another. Always ask: compared with whom? A result is only meaningful alongside the patient's age, sex, height and ethnicity — and alongside the clinical history and examination.

Test conduct: three attempts for static/dynamic volumes, best value accepted. Repeat after bronchodilator in airway disease to establish reversibility, which has real management implications.

8 · Disease patterns — asthma, COPD, ILD, neuromuscular Diagnosis

Asthma

  • Asthma is a clinical diagnosis; tests confirm variable airflow obstruction or airway inflammation.
  • Spirometry may be normal when asymptomatic. Diagnosis rests on reversibility or rapid changes in FEV1/FVC.
  • More sensitive: challenge tests (methacholine, histamine, mannitol, exercise).
  • Community PEF monitoring with >20% variability strongly suggests asthma.
Anaesthetic relevance None of these diagnostic tests reliably predict perioperative bronchospasm. That is better predicted by asthma symptoms in the previous 30 days.

COPD

  • Also largely clinical, but GOLD recommends spirometry to confirm airflow limitation.
  • Key criterion: post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 70%.
  • Caveat: the fixed 70% ratio over-diagnoses COPD in the elderly. Many suggest treating the ratio as clinically important only if below the LLN (z-score < −1.64) for that patient.

GOLD 2 — moderate50 – 79%
GOLD 3 — severe30 – 49%
GOLD 4 — very severe< 30%

An abnormally low TLCO before surgery Always investigate further. It suggests impaired gas exchange that will be significantly challenged when added to the normal respiratory changes of general anaesthesia.
9 · Preoperative assessment & predicting PPCs Periop
  • Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are common after major surgery, but PFTs do not predict them well.
  • One small study found airflow limitation to be one of seven independent predictors of PPC. Larger, better-validated studies found clinical factors — recent respiratory tract infection, low SpO2 — to be superior.
  • Abnormal PFTs may be an indirect marker of PPC susceptibility, but cannot alone quantify that risk.
NICE NG45 (routine preoperative tests for elective surgery) Routine preoperative spirometry is not recommended for any patient group. Even patients having intermediate or major/complex surgery who are ASA 3–4 due to known or suspected respiratory disease should be tested only after seeking advice from a senior anaesthetist.

In practice, an ASA 3–4 patient with respiratory disease has usually already been assessed by respiratory specialists and has existing PFTs. If not, spirometry may be helpful to:

  • Diagnose and quantify the disease, or
  • Seek evidence of a recent infection that would affect the timing of surgery

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) in risk stratification

CPET objectively quantifies functional capacity to meet the metabolic demands of major surgery. Three measures define physiological reserve:

MeasureWhat it tells youThreshold / note
Peak VO2Maximum oxygen consumption just before exhaustion; normalised to body weight<10 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ or <35% predicted = high risk of perioperative death and cardiopulmonary complications (ERS/ESTS/ACCP)
Anaerobic threshold (AT)Point of disproportionate rise in VCO2 relative to VO2 from lactic acid buffering; ≈ half of peak VO2Less dependent on motivation than peak VO2. Reduced AT → cardiac complications (e.g. aortic aneurysm repair)
VE/VCO2Ventilation required to eliminate a volume of CO2; reflects V/Q relationships and dead spaceLower = better. Elevated VE/VCO2 → PPCs

A resting adult consumes about 3.5 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ = 1 MET.

10 · Lung resection — assessing fitness & calculating ppo Thoracics

Patients presenting for lung resection often have a combination of lung cancer, cardiovascular disease and impaired lung function (shared risk factor: tobacco). The task is to balance short-term surgical risk (especially PPCs) and long-term risk of pulmonary disability against the risk of death from untreated cancer.

Historical note: the "match test" — blowing out a lighted match held 6 inches from an open mouth — was an early bedside measure of FEV1/maximum breathing capacity. Some clinicians held the match at arm's length to correct for height.

The three assessment domains

DomainTestKey thresholds
Respiratory mechanics FEV1 (good indicator of pulmonary reserve) Preoperative FEV1 <60% predicted → significant postoperative complications. Risks rise to ~60% when ppo-FEV1 <30%.
Parenchymal function TLCO TLCO and % ppo-TLCO are independent prognostic factors for long-term survival after curative-intent surgery
Cardiopulmonary reserve Stair climbing, shuttle walk, or formal CPET VO2max 10–15 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ → high risk of complications and mortality vs peak VO2max >20

If peak VO2 unavailable, stair climbing may substitute — but if altitude reached is <22 m, formal CPET with peak VO2 is strongly recommended.

Calculating ppo (predicted postoperative) values

Four methods exist: (i) anatomical calculation, (ii) ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy, (iii) quantitative CT, (iv) dynamic perfusion MRI. ACCP recommends routine ppo calculation for all lung resections; BTS/SCTS and ERS/ESTS recommend it only when preoperative values do not clearly place the patient in a low-risk category.

Anatomical method (best for lobectomy) Right lung = 10 segments, left lung = 9 segments, total = 19.

ppo-FEV1 = preop FEV1 × (1 − number of unobstructed segments resected / total number of unobstructed segments)

Obstructed segments contribute nothing to the preoperative FEV1 or TLCO, so they must be excluded from both numerator and denominator. Determine obstruction from CT or bronchoscopy. TLCO or VO2max can be substituted for FEV1.
Perfusion method (typically for pneumonectomy) Uses perfused rather than aerated lung regions — better accounts for non-functioning tissue (emphysematous regions, tumour-obstructed segments) whose removal will not change FEV1 or TLCO.

ppo-FEV1 = preop FEV1 × (1 − fraction of total perfusion of the lung to be resected)

Requires an extra investigation. TLCO or VO2max may be substituted.

Convert absolute ppo values to % predicted or z-scores to quantify the expected postoperative function and counsel the patient on exercise capacity and infection susceptibility.

Important caveat These methods mostly underestimate the true ppo values. Therefore any result above the required threshold suggests suitability for resection.
11 · Lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) Thoracics

LVRS is a palliative procedure for severe COPD due to emphysema, usually via VATS, typically a wedge excision of emphysematous lung.

Proposed mechanisms of benefit:

  • Improved elastic recoil of remaining lung
  • Reduced chest hyperinflation → improved respiratory muscle efficiency
  • Reduced intrathoracic pressure → improved ventricular filling (less dynamic hyperinflation)

Early attempts had high mortality in these sick patients. The National Emphysema Treatment Trial (NETT) formally assessed safety, efficacy, risk/benefit and cost-effectiveness.

NETT results No overall survival benefit vs maximal medical therapy. Improved quality of life, dyspnoea and exercise tolerance in some patients. Greatest benefit in those with low baseline exercise capacity and heterogeneous emphysema with predominantly upper-lobe disease and relatively preserved lung elsewhere. Patients with preoperative FEV1 or TLCO <20% predicted had an unacceptably high perioperative mortality.

NICE selection criteria

RequirementThreshold
On maximal medical therapy for severe COPDYes
Marked limitation of activities of daily livingYes
FEV1> 20% predicted
TLCO> 20% predicted
PaCO2< 7.3 kPa
Emphysema distributionUpper-lobe predominant on high-resolution CT (HRCT)

HRCT confirms the presence of emphysema and its distribution, and may reveal conditions that contraindicate LVRS. Where HRCT cannot accurately define heterogeneity, quantitative perfusion scanning may be required — on the presumption that unperfused lung is unlikely to contribute to gas exchange and can safely be removed. Homogeneous emphysema is less likely to benefit.

12 · Functional walking tests & CPET Cardiopulmonary

Historically, the ability to climb flights of stairs was a functional assessment of cardiopulmonary status. Perioperative mortality and complications both correlate with an inability to climb steps.

TestProtocolPrimary outcomeNotes
6-minute walk test (6MWT) Self-paced walking for 6 min 6MWD (metres) Secondary data: SpO2, heart rate, recovery time, Borg dyspnoea/fatigue scores, 6-min walk work (6MWD × body weight). Most healthy subjects: 400–700 m.
Incremental shuttle walk test (ISWT) Externally paced shuttles between two cones 10 m apart; pace increases every minute to an audio signal (12 levels); terminated if the subject cannot keep pace Distance walked (completed shuttles) >400 m is linked to VO2max ≥ 15 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹
Endurance shuttle walk test (ESWT) Initial ISWT determines the pace (usually ~85% of maximal ISWT pace); walk as long as possible at that pace Duration Requires a prior ISWT
Globally accepted reference equations from healthy populations for functional walking tests are not yet available — interpret with local norms and the individual's baseline.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET)

Requires more resources than a walking test, but is the gold standard for combined cardiopulmonary and circulatory function. It records a large number of physiological variables; the clinically most useful are:

Peak VO2
Oxygen consumption measured spirometrically from inspiratory/expiratory oxygen concentrations and minute ventilation; the maximum value just before exhaustion. Normalised to body weight (mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹). Related to fitness and motivation — different from VO2max, which is difficult to define except in trained athletes.
Anaerobic threshold
Marked by a disproportionate rise in VCO2 relative to VO2 from lactic acid buffering in muscle. Believed to represent the point at which oxygen delivery to muscle becomes inadequate (an oversimplification, but clinically useful). Occurs at roughly half of peak VO2. Fitter people have a higher AT. Advantage: less dependent on motivation.
VE/VCO2
Volume of ventilation required to eliminate a volume of CO2; usually taken at the AT. Reflects the lungs' ability to meet exercise demands and relates to V/Q relationships including dead space. Lower = better lung health.

14 · 10 high-yield case examples

Case 1 — Preoperative spirometry before elective hernia repair Periop

Scenario. A 68-year-old smoker (ASA 2) is listed for elective inguinal hernia repair. The surgical team requests spirometry "to clear him for surgery." FEV1 returns at 55% predicted.

Answer. NICE NG45 does not recommend routine preoperative spirometry for any patient group. Abnormal PFTs are poor predictors of postoperative pulmonary complications; clinical factors (recent respiratory tract infection, low SpO2, functional status) outperform them. Proceed with surgery and optimise modifiable factors — inhaler technique, smoking cessation, physiotherapy, timing around any recent infection. Do not delay surgery on the basis of spirometry alone.

Case 2 — The same ratio in two different patients Interpretation

Scenario. FEV1/FVC = 0.68. Is this COPD?

Answer. Not necessarily. The fixed 70% threshold over-diagnoses COPD in the elderly. Compare against the lower limit of normal for that individual — i.e. z-score < −1.64. In a 25-year-old, 0.68 is clearly abnormal; in an 80-year-old it may lie within the normal reference range. Always interpret against a matched reference population (GLI equations, 74,187 healthy non-smokers aged 3–95 yr).

Case 3 — Asthma: what actually predicts bronchospasm? Periop

Scenario. A 30-year-old with asthma is asymptomatic, with normal spirometry, listed for elective surgery. Which test predicts intraoperative bronchospasm?

Answer. None of the PFT results. Perioperative bronchospasm is better predicted by asthma symptoms in the previous 30 days. Diagnostic tests such as reversibility, challenge testing, or >20% PEF variability are useful for making the diagnosis but are not known to be associated with perioperative bronchospasm. Optimise control, check inhaler technique, consider steroids if there has been a recent exacerbation, and postpone elective surgery during an active exacerbation or recent respiratory tract infection.

Case 4 — Why is my COPD patient breathless? Mechanics

Scenario. FEV1 45% predicted, FEV1/FVC 0.45, TLCO 48% predicted. The patient is breathless at rest and on minimal exertion.

Answer. Obstructive pattern (GOLD 3, FEV1 30–49%) with reduced TLCO suggesting emphysematous loss of gas-exchange surface area. Also measure RV and FRC — static hyperinflation explains dyspnoea at rest and on exercise and is an independent therapeutic target. A reduced TLCO before surgery should always be investigated further: it flags impaired gas exchange that will be compounded by the normal respiratory changes of general anaesthesia.

Case 5 — ppo-FEV1 after right upper lobectomy Thoracics

Scenario. Preoperative FEV1 = 2.0 L. Right upper lobectomy planned (3 segments). All segments are unobstructed.

Answer. Right lung = 10 segments, left = 9, total = 19.
ppo-FEV1 = 2.0 × (1 − 3/19) = 2.0 × 0.842 = 1.68 L
Then express as % predicted or a z-score for that patient. Critical detail: obstructed (non-contributing) segments must be excluded from both numerator and denominator — the formula uses the number of unobstructed segments resected over the total number of unobstructed segments.

Case 6 — Pneumonectomy: perfusion scintigraphy Thoracics

Scenario. Preoperative FEV1 = 1.8 L. The right lung receives 55% of total perfusion. Right pneumonectomy planned.

Answer. ppo-FEV1 = 1.8 × (1 − 0.55) = 0.81 L. Perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy is preferred for pneumonectomy because it accounts for non-functioning but still ventilated lung. TLCO or VO2max can be substituted into the same equation. Remember: these methods mostly underestimate true ppo values, so a result above the threshold favours resection.

Case 7 — Interpreting CPET thresholds Cardiopulmonary

Scenario. Peak VO2 = 12 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ (40% predicted); AT at 9 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹; VE/VCO2 elevated at the AT.

Answer. Peak VO2 of 10–20 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ (or 35–75% predicted) places the patient in the intermediate group → proceed to split function testing (ppo-FEV1, ppo-DLCO). Peak VO2 <10 mL kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ or <35% predicted would be high risk for perioperative death and cardiopulmonary complications. AT is less dependent on motivation and normally sits at about half of peak VO2. A raised VE/VCO2 reflects poor ventilatory efficiency and V/Q mismatch, and predicts postoperative pulmonary complications (whereas a reduced AT predicts cardiac complications).

Case 8 — Guillain–Barré: when to ventilate ICU

Scenario. A patient with Guillain–Barré syndrome is on the ward. SpO2 is 96% on air and the arterial blood gas is normal.

Answer. Do not be reassured. Falling SpO2 and hypercapnia are very late signs of ventilatory insufficiency, especially on supplemental oxygen. Monitor with serial effort-dependent tests — FVC is the practical choice because it uses all respiratory muscles. An FVC < 20 mL/kg, a 30% decrease in FVC, or falling maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures are associated with a need for mechanical ventilation. Also track cough strength, secretion clearance and bulbar function.

Case 9 — Low TLCO: is it the lung or the blood? Parenchyma

Scenario. TLCO = 55% predicted in a patient with a normal chest radiograph who is anaemic (Hb 95 g/L).

Answer. Anaemia reduces the CO-carrying capacity of blood and therefore lowers TLCO. Laboratories report both the actual and the Hb-adjusted value (adjusted to Hb 146 g/L in men; 134 g/L in women and children <15 yr). Always consider the other modifiers: lung volume, carboxyhaemoglobin from smoking, inspired oxygen concentration, altitude, body position (supine increases TLCO), plus age, sex, height and ethnicity. Remember that KCO and VA are the primary measurements — the same TLCO from different KCO/VA combinations means different things. Low TLCO also occurs in emphysema (loss of surface area) and is severely impaired in interstitial lung disease and fibrosis.

Case 10 — Is this patient a candidate for LVRS? Thoracics

Scenario. A 62-year-old with severe COPD on maximal medical therapy, marked limitation of activities of daily living. FEV1 22% predicted, TLCO 21% predicted, PaCO2 6.5 kPa, upper-lobe-predominant emphysema on HRCT.

Answer. This patient meets the NICE criteria for LVRS assessment: FEV1 and TLCO both >20% predicted, PaCO2 <7.3 kPa, upper-lobe-predominant emphysema on HRCT, on maximal medical therapy with marked ADL limitation. Note that NETT showed no overall survival benefit versus maximal medical therapy, but improved quality of life, dyspnoea and exercise tolerance — benefit is greatest in those with low baseline exercise capacity and heterogeneous upper-lobe disease. A preoperative FEV1 or TLCO <20% predicted carries an unacceptably high perioperative mortality.
