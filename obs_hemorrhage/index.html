1 · Definitions & Epidemiology

Definitions of obstetric haemorrhage
TermDefinition
Primary PPHBlood loss within 24 h of delivery. WHO: ≥500 mL. ACOG (2014): ≥1000 mL with or without clinical signs of hypovolaemia, regardless of mode of delivery.
Minor PPH (RCOG)500–1000 mL
Major PPH (RCOG)>1000 mL — moderate 1001–2000 mL; severe >2000 mL
Secondary PPHAbnormal/excessive bleeding from the birth canal between 24 h and 12 weeks postnatally
Antepartum haemorrhage (APH)Bleeding from 24 weeks' gestation. Major APH 50–1000 mL without shock; massive APH ≥1000 mL and/or any volume with clinical shock
Major obstetric haemorrhage (MOH)Umbrella term for the leading cause of maternal morbidity/mortality — includes major PPH and massive APH
Clinical caveat In women with low body mass (e.g. <60 kg), a lower volume of blood loss may already be clinically significant. Interpret volumes against the individual's circulating blood volume.
Epidemiology & why it matters
  • PPH is the leading cause of maternal death worldwide — responsible for up to 27.1% of maternal deaths, and one of the most preventable.
  • The proportion of maternal deaths attributable to PPH is greater in less developed nations; however, PPH prevalence in the USA rose from 2.9% to 3.2% of deliveries between 2010 and 2014.
  • UK/Ireland (MBRRACE): haemorrhage is the second most common cause of direct maternal death, rate 0.64 per 100,000.
  • Wales (quantitative measurement): PPH >1000 mL in 8.6%; blood loss ≥2000 mL in 1.3%.
  • Increasing incidence of severe PPH requiring transfusion/surgery is driven largely by rising rates of uterine atony.
  • Deaths commonly result from inadequate volume replacement and failure to correct coagulopathy.
  • Severity of haemorrhage was not recognised in 61% (11 of 17) of women who died in one Confidential Enquiry cohort.
Key message Most maternal deaths from haemorrhage are preventable. Early recognition, prompt volume replacement and early correction of coagulopathy save lives.

2 · Physiology of Pregnancy & Maternal Haemostasis

Hypercoagulable state — what changes and why it matters
  • Term pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state from hormone-mediated alterations in coagulation factors.
  • Clotting factor concentrations are increased — except factor XI and factor XIII.
  • Protein S is decreased; protein C remains unchanged.
  • Fibrinogen rises markedly in the third trimester: 3.5–6.5 g/L (vs 2–4 g/L in non-pregnant patients). This acts as a physiological buffer for haemorrhage.
  • Fibrinolysis is downregulated — decreased tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) activity.
  • At delivery, the fibrinolytic system becomes activated, reducing clot stability (D-dimers rise).
Implication A "normal" fibrinogen in a bleeding parturient may be relatively low. A fibrinogen <2 g/L in pregnancy is abnormal and predicts severe PPH.
Blood volume, dilutional anaemia and thrombocytopenia
  • Blood volume rises from 6–8 weeks, peaking at 32 weeks, by 40–50% of non-pregnant values — mediated by ADH and aldosterone with sodium/water retention.
  • Red cell mass increases (↑ erythropoietin) but plasma volume rises more → physiological dilutional anaemia.
  • Combined dilution + increased platelet turnover → gestational thrombocytopenia. Platelet function is unchanged.
  • The uteroplacental unit is a high-flow, low-resistance vascular bed receiving 25% of cardiac output at term — large volumes can be lost rapidly.
  • Uterine involution and placental separation after delivery cause autotransfusion of ~500 mL.
Haemodynamic response to blood loss — why signs are masked

In pregnancy, resting heart rate is higher and mean arterial pressure lower than in the non-pregnant state. Pulse and blood pressure are usually maintained in the normal range until blood loss exceeds 1000 mL.

Blood lossTypical clinical signs
<1000 mLOften no reliable change in pulse or BP
1000–1500 mLTachycardia, tachypnoea, slight recordable fall in systolic BP
>1500 mLSystolic BP <80 mmHg with worsening tachycardia, tachypnoea and altered mental state
  • Shock index (heart rate ÷ systolic BP) identifies women at risk of adverse outcomes (e.g. ICU admission) and compares favourably with conventional vital signs.
  • Take the woman's weight into account — smaller women decompensate faster.

Acquired coagulopathy — consumptive vs dilutional

Dilutional

  • Crystalloid and red cell resuscitation can dilute clotting factors and platelets.
  • Occurs mainly at extreme levels of dilution, such as large-volume resuscitation for massive haemorrhage, or when large volumes of low-fibrinogen products (e.g. FFP) are used without fibrinogen replacement.

Consumptive — localised

  • Occurs at the placental bed: placental abruption, or consumption within uterine clots.
  • Bleeding plus clot formation consumes coagulation factors, especially fibrinogen → hypofibrinogenaemia, thrombocytopenia, reduced clotting factor concentrations.

Consumptive — disseminated (DIC)

  • Associated with severe pre-eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, severe abruption, amniotic fluid embolism (AFE).
  • AFE, uterine rupture and placental abruption may cause early-onset DIC.
Predictor Hypofibrinogenaemia is strongly associated with severe PPH. Fibrinogen is the only marker independently associated with progression to severe PPH — a level <2.0 g/L has a 100% positive predictive value for progression to severe PPH; ≥4.0 g/L has a 79% negative predictive value.

The risk of coagulopathy increases markedly once bleeding exceeds 2000 mL.

Risk factors & where to deliver
  • Risk factors may be antenatal or intrapartum; care plans must be modified as and when risk factors arise.
  • Clinicians must be aware of risk factors and take them into account when counselling women about place of delivery.
  • Women with known risk factors for PPH should only deliver in a hospital with a blood bank on site.
  • However, most cases of PPH have no identifiable risk factors — risk stratification is fraught with difficulty. Universal prophylaxis is therefore required.
  • Antenatal anaemia should be investigated and treated — this may reduce the morbidity associated with PPH.

4 · Recognition & Assessment of Blood Loss

Measuring blood loss — visual estimation is unreliable
  • Visual estimation of peripartum blood loss is inaccurate and usually underestimates the true volume.
  • Clinical signs and symptoms must be included in the assessment of PPH.
  • More accurate methods: blood collection drapes for vaginal delivery, weighing of swabs, and quantitative blood loss (gravimetric or photometric).
  • Quantitative measurement can form part of an alert system to prevent progression of minor to severe bleeding (successfully used in the Welsh Obstetric Bleeding Strategy / OBS Cymru).
  • Written and pictorial guidelines on the labour ward help staff estimate blood loss; clinical reconstructions/simulation encourage earlier diagnosis.
  • Remember: a single point-of-care haemoglobin may be falsely reassuring in acute haemorrhage — clinical evaluation is paramount.
Anaesthetic relevance Do not wait for laboratory results or a haemoglobin value to start resuscitation. The clinical picture is the main determinant of the need for blood transfusion.
Who to call — escalation triggers
  • Minor PPH (500–1000 mL) without clinical shock: alert the midwife in charge, plus first-line obstetric and anaesthetic staff.
  • Major PPH (>1000 mL) with ongoing bleeding or clinical shock: summon a multidisciplinary team involving senior members of staff.
  • For major PPH, call and summon:
    • an experienced midwife (in addition to the midwife in charge)
    • the obstetric middle grade
    • the anaesthetic middle grade
    • the on-call clinical haematologist with experience in major haemorrhage
    • porters for delivery of specimens/blood
  • Alert the consultant obstetrician and consultant anaesthetist, and inform the blood transfusion laboratory.
  • Assign one team member as scribe to record events, fluids, drugs, blood/components and vital signs.
  • The RCOG recommends the consultant obstetrician attend in person when PPH >1500 mL with continuing haemorrhage.
Communication Keep the woman and her birthing partner informed from the outset. Agree locally a standard form of words ("we need compatible blood now", "group-specific blood") and timescales for delivery of components. Senior staff must be receptive to concerns raised by junior staff and midwives.

5 · Preparation & Massive Transfusion Planning

Antenatal optimisation & system readiness
  • Screen for and treat antenatal anaemia. Hb outside the normal UK pregnancy range (110 g/L at first contact, 105 g/L at 28 weeks) should be investigated; consider iron supplementation, and parenteral iron if oral iron fails.
  • Maximise maternal red cell mass antenatally (nutrition, iron, ± recombinant erythropoietin) to reduce the need for transfusion if bleeding occurs.
  • Match every woman to a unit where her clinical needs can be met. Protocols should exist for rapid transfer to a consultant-led unit.
  • In abnormal placentation, prior structured multidisciplinary planning reduces blood loss, transfusion and emergency Caesarean delivery.
  • Identify women who refuse blood products (e.g. Jehovah's Witnesses) early in the antenatal period and counsel appropriately.
  • Every maternity unit should have a multidisciplinary protocol for PPH management.
  • Massive transfusion algorithms should be developed, adapted and rehearsed locally in each obstetric unit.
  • Safe Motherhood Initiative (SMI) themes: readiness, recognition, response, reporting.
  • Training for PPH should be multiprofessional and include team rehearsals / simulation — improves knowledge, practical skills, communication and team performance.
Blood bank readiness
  • Major obstetric haemorrhage protocols must include immediate issue of group O, RhD-negative and K-negative units, switching to group-specific blood as soon as feasible.
  • All delivery units — especially small units without an on-site blood bank — should maintain a supply of group O, RhD-negative blood. Agree locally the minimum number of units.
  • RhD-negative women of childbearing age must only receive RhD-negative blood. Unless known to be K-positive, only K-negative blood should be used.
  • If clinically significant red cell antibodies are present, liaise closely with the transfusion laboratory to avoid delay.
  • In an emergency, standard leucocyte-depleted components should be given — CMV-negative blood is not required during delivery or postpartum.

6 · Resuscitation, Fluids & Transfusion Triggers

Minor PPH (500–1000 mL) without clinical shock
  • Intravenous access — one 14-gauge cannula.
  • Urgent venepuncture (20 mL) for:
    • group and screen
    • full blood count
    • coagulation screen, including fibrinogen
  • Record pulse, respiratory rate and blood pressure every 15 minutes.
  • Commence warmed crystalloid infusion.
Major PPH (>1000 mL) with ongoing bleeding or clinical shock

Immediate priorities

  • A & B — assess airway and breathing; give high-concentration oxygen 10–15 L/min via facemask regardless of measured saturation. If airway is compromised by impaired consciousness, seek anaesthetic assistance urgently.
  • C — evaluate circulation; position the patient flat; keep the woman warm.
  • Transfuse blood as soon as possible if clinically required.
  • Establish two 14-gauge intravenous lines; take a 20 mL blood sample.
  • Consider arterial line monitoring once appropriately experienced staff are available.
  • Consider transfer to intensive therapy unit once bleeding is controlled, or high-dependency monitoring on the delivery suite.
  • Record parameters on a MEOWS chart (modified early obstetric warning score) and escalate promptly when abnormal.
  • Document fluid balance, blood, blood products and procedures.
Left lateral tilt In antepartum cases, use left lateral tilt or uterine displacement to optimise preload to the right side of the heart.
Fluid resuscitation — volumes and choice
  • Until blood is available, infuse up to 3.5 L of warmed clear fluids:
    • initially up to 2 L warmed isotonic crystalloid as rapidly as possible
    • then up to a further 1.5 L warmed colloid (succinylated gelatin)
  • Hydroxyethyl starch should NOT be used — may increase mortality.
  • WHO recommends isotonic crystalloids in preference to colloids for intravenous fluid replacement in PPH.
  • Use the best equipment available to achieve rapid warmed infusion.
  • Do not use special blood filters — they slow infusions.
  • Keep the woman warm — hypothermia exacerbates acidosis.
Principle The nature of the fluid infused is of less importance than rapid administration and warming. Avoid a dilutional coagulopathy by not persisting with clear fluids once blood and components are available.
Red cell transfusion — when and what
  • There are no firm criteria for initiating red cell transfusion. Base the decision on clinical and haematological assessment.
  • Transfusion is almost always required when Hb <60 g/L and rarely required when Hb >100 g/L. In acute haemorrhage, Hb may be normal — clinical evaluation is essential.
  • Serial Hb/haematocrit measurements may help monitor progress; single values may mislead and delay transfusion.
  • Consider repeated measurements of serum lactate and base deficit alongside haematocrit/Hb to assess tissue perfusion and oxygenation.
  • If immediate transfusion is indicated, give emergency group O, RhD-negative, K-negative red cells; switch to group-specific as soon as feasible.

Intraoperative cell salvage

  • Consider for emergency use in PPH associated with Caesarean section and with vaginal delivery.
  • No single serious complication leading to poor maternal outcome has been directly attributed to its use.
  • The SALVO RCT found no benefit from routine use during Caesarean section in women at risk of haemorrhage, and it may increase the risk of maternal alloimmunisation.
  • May be acceptable to patients who would otherwise refuse blood transfusion (e.g. Jehovah's Witnesses).
  • A double-suction technique is often used — one suction for amniotic fluid, another for uncontaminated blood. Requires training and demonstrated proficiency.
Haematological targets during major haemorrhage (BCSH)
ParameterTarget
Haemoglobin>80 g/L
Platelet count>50 × 10⁹/L (transfuse at <75 × 10⁹/L during PPH)
Prothrombin time (PT)<1.5 × normal
Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT)<1.5 × normal
Fibrinogen>2 g/L

The principle is to prevent and treat haemostatic abnormalities during bleeding, but not to correct abnormalities in non-bleeding women.

Fibrinogen: the pivotal target
  • Fibrinogen concentrations <2 g/L are associated with worse outcomes and severe PPH — maintain >2 g/L (or FIBTEM A5 >12 mm).
  • Fibrinogen falls earlier and more rapidly than other coagulation factors and may be reduced to a clinically significant level despite a normal PT/APTT — always measure Clauss fibrinogen as part of the routine coagulation screen.
  • In an active haemorrhage, consider a higher target concentration than 2 g/L.
  • Observational data: fibrinogen 1.0–1.5 g/L is likely too low for adequate haemostasis during ongoing PPH; fibrinogen <3 g/L (especially <2 g/L) is associated with progression of bleeding, increased RBC/component requirements, and need for invasive procedures.
Why FFP alone may not be enough The average fibrinogen concentration in FFP is only 2–2.5 g/L, whereas term pregnancy values approach 5 g/L. FFP can therefore result in relative hypofibrinogenaemia and may worsen haemostasis. Give FFP when factor concentrations are low enough to warrant repletion, and consider concurrent fibrinogen supplementation (cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate).

Evidence on fibrinogen supplementation — what the trials show
TrialDesignFinding
FIB-PPH Fibrinogen concentrate 2 g IV vs placebo in severe PPH No difference in transfusion requirement or estimated blood loss over 6 weeks. Baseline fibrinogen was 4.5 g/L and only 2.2% had fibrinogen <2 g/L → prophylactic fibrinogen is not warranted in the absence of hypofibrinogenaemia.
OBS2 Viscoelastometric-guided fibrinogen concentrate vs placebo; FIBTEM A5 <15 mm triggered 1 g No change in number of blood products transfused. Subgroup with FIBTEM A5 <12 mm: a FIBTEM A5 >12 mm or fibrinogen >2 g/L is likely adequate for haemostasis without additional fibrinogen.
FIDEL Fibrinogen concentrate 3 g vs placebo in persistent PPH after vaginal delivery No benefit in lowering transfusion requirement or blood loss. Did prevent a fall in plasma fibrinogen; no increase in thromboembolic events.
Bottom line Prophylactic/empiric fibrinogen concentrate has no established role in PPH. Use viscoelastic testing to target therapy — this also saves cost given the expense of cryoprecipitate and fibrinogen concentrate.
Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) — practical rules
  • Contains all coagulation factors except platelets, at variable concentrations.
  • Indicated during PPH in cases of overt coagulopathy after fibrinogen repletion has been attempted, or a known consumptive process with evidence of coagulopathy.
  • Empiric plasma transfusion is rarely indicated when cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate is available.
  • RCOG: FFP should be guided by coagulation testing — maintain PT and APTT below 1.5× normal.
  • If bleeding continues after 4 units of RBC and haemostatic results are unavailable → give FFP 12–15 mL/kg until results are known.
  • If PT/APTT >1.5× normal and haemorrhage is ongoing → volumes in excess of 15 mL/kg are likely needed.
  • If PPH has stopped and PT/APTT are normal → no FFP required.
  • Fixed-ratio protocols (1:1 or 6:4 RBC:FFP) derived from trauma data have no evidence of benefit in PPH. Drawbacks: most women have normal coagulation at the time of administration, and there is increased risk of TACO and TRALI.
Platelets & transfusion risks
  • Thrombocytopenia may be gestational, non-pregnancy-related, or indicate a consumptive coagulopathy.
  • Evidence for platelet transfusion is based on expert opinion rather than RCTs.
  • During active bleeding: maintain platelet count >50 × 10⁹/L; transfuse at <75 × 10⁹/L during active PPH, based on laboratory monitoring.
  • Point-of-care viscoelastic testing can determine clot strength based on platelet contribution and may illustrate the need for platelet transfusion faster than traditional clotting tests.

Risks of blood product transfusion

  • Febrile reactions, allergic reactions, haemolytic reactions
  • Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI)
  • Transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO)
  • Infection — platelets are stored at room temperature and carry more infection-related complications than other products

Maternal instability in acute PPH may necessitate liberal transfusion to prevent complications of delayed resuscitation — but clinicians should still know and weigh these risks.

8 · Coagulopathy Monitoring & Viscoelastic Testing

Laboratory tests vs point-of-care viscoelastic testing
  • Laboratory fibrinogen results take approximately 60 minutes. ROTEM can provide specific information about clot formation within 10 minutes.
  • Routine coagulation tests (PT, APTT, Clauss fibrinogen, platelet count) are widely available with well-regulated quality control — but turnaround times are often too slow for acute, rapidly evolving bleeds, and they reflect the past haemostatic status of the woman.
  • Coagulopathies may evolve rapidly — repeated testing (e.g. every 30 minutes) during continued bleeding and observation of trends is more useful than single measurements.
  • Point-of-care viscoelastic testing (TEG / ROTEM) combined with an agreed treatment algorithm has been associated with decreased blood loss and blood product use, both outside and within the obstetric setting.
  • Recommended by the Obstetric Anaesthetists' Association / Association of Anaesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland. However, NICE concluded there is insufficient evidence to recommend routine adoption. If used, agree a quality control protocol with the haematology laboratory.
FIBTEM — how to use it
  • FIBTEM is performed on the ROTEM machine and evaluates the contribution of fibrinogen to clot strength.
  • This is achieved by adding cytochalasin D, which eliminates the contribution of platelets to the clot.
  • FIBTEM A5 = clot amplitude measured after 5 minutes.
  • FIBTEM A5 is an independent predictor of PPH >2500 mL; a FIBTEM A5 <10 mm is associated with prolonged bleeds.
  • FIBTEM amplitude correlates with serum fibrinogen concentration: FIBTEM A5 of 12 mm ≈ fibrinogen 2 g/L.
Targets Fibrinogen >2 g/L or FIBTEM A5 >12 mm. The value of viscoelastic testing here is the ability to rapidly identify low-fibrinogen states or hyperfibrinolysis and allow targeted replacement.

9 · Tranexamic Acid (TXA)

Mechanism, dose and the WOMAN trial

Mechanism

  • At delivery there is a marked increase in fibrinolysis (increased D-dimers).
  • TXA reduces bleeding by inhibiting the breakdown of fibrin and fibrinogen, stabilising blood clots.

Dose

  • 1 g IV (WHO recommended fixed dose, given over 10 minutes), with a second 1 g dose after 30 minutes if bleeding continues.
  • RCOG: consider intravenous TXA 0.5–1.0 g, in addition to oxytocin, at Caesarean section to reduce blood loss in women at increased risk of PPH.

WOMAN trial

  • International double-blind, randomised, multicentre study of TXA vs placebo in women with PPH; treatment group received 1 g IV plus a further 1 g 30 min later if bleeding continued.
  • No difference in the composite primary endpoint of death from all causes or hysterectomy.
  • Secondary endpoint — death due to bleeding was significantly reduced in the TXA group, with greatest effect when given within 3 hours of birth.
  • Absolute risk reduction of death from bleeding: 0.368%; number needed to treat ≈ 271.
  • No statistically significant difference in thromboembolic events — this was the first study adequately powered for this endpoint.
  • Many participating hospitals were in low-resource countries, so findings may not be fully generalisable to well-resourced settings.
Prophylactic TXA — what the evidence shows
  • Vaginal delivery: a large multicentre trial found prophylactic TXA 1 g did not reduce the incidence of PPH.
  • Caesarean delivery (2021 trial): prophylactic TXA 1 g decreased the rate of PPH and reduced calculated estimated blood loss by ~100 mL — but there was no significant difference in blood transfusion, hysterectomy, reoperation for bleeding, or use of additional uterotonics. Nausea/vomiting was significantly higher with TXA. >70% of patients underwent Caesarean delivery without labour, putting them at lower baseline risk.
  • Caesarean delivery (2023 trial): TXA 1 g IV vs placebo in scheduled or unscheduled operative delivery — no difference in maternal death or blood transfusion, no difference in estimated blood loss >1 L, and similar frequency of thromboembolic events.
  • Pharmacokinetics: peak concentrations after 3 minutes, with antifibrinolysis sustained for about 1 hour after dosing. Peak concentrations were lower than those associated with seizure and thrombosis risk.
Summary Limited evidence that prophylactic TXA prevents clinically significant haemorrhage in vaginal or Caesarean delivery. However, 1 g is an appropriate dose to ensure antifibrinolytic activity while avoiding concentrations associated with seizure and thrombosis risk. TXA should be considered in the management of established PPH.

Practical oxytocin pharmacology for the anaesthetist
  • Oxytocin and carbetocin are recommended as first-line uterotonic prophylaxis and treatment for PPH.
  • Give oxytocin initially as a small bolus, then as a titrated infusion.
  • Major guidelines recommend 5–10 IU after vaginal and Caesarean delivery — but 5 IU is the maximum allowable IV bolus in the UK. There is evidence these drugs are as effective for prophylaxis at much lower doses.
  • Oxytocin receptor desensitisation: prolonged exposure to oxytocin desensitises myometrial receptors, so oxytocin fails to stimulate effective contractions. Labouring women with oxytocin augmentation require much greater doses than those at elective Caesarean delivery — consider them separately.
  • Extrauterine effects are most serious on the cardiovascular system. Effects correlate strongly with both dose and speed of administration.
Critical safety point During MOH, oxytocin given in the context of reduced intravascular volume may precipitate rapid decompensation, and deaths have occurred in this situation. Resuscitate volume first, and give any uterotonic slowly.
RCOG sequence for atonic PPH — mechanical & pharmacological

When uterine atony is perceived to be the cause, institute the following in turn until bleeding stops:

  1. Palpate the uterine fundus and rub it up to stimulate contractions
  2. Ensure the bladder is empty (Foley catheter, leave in place)
  3. Oxytocin 5 IU by slow IV injection (may repeat dose)
  4. Ergometrine 0.5 mg by slow IV or IM injection (contraindicated in hypertension)
  5. Oxytocin infusion — 40 IU in 500 mL isotonic crystalloid at 125 mL/h (unless fluid restriction is necessary)
  6. Carboprost 0.25 mg IM, repeated at intervals of not less than 15 minutes, to a maximum of 8 doses (use with caution in asthma)
  7. Misoprostol 800 µg sublingually
Timing If significant atonic haemorrhage continues after a third dose of carboprost without improvement (i.e. ≥30 minutes after the first dose), consider transfer to theatre for examination under anaesthesia, anticipating the possible need for laparotomy and/or hysterectomy.
Prophylaxis at delivery — RCOG recommendations
  • Uterine massage is of no benefit in the prophylaxis of PPH.
  • Prophylactic uterotonics should be routinely offered in the management of the third stage of labour in all women — they reduce the risk of PPH.
  • Women without risk factors delivering vaginally: oxytocin 10 IU IM is the agent of choice. A higher dose is unlikely to be beneficial.
  • Caesarean section: oxytocin 5 IU by slow IV injection to encourage uterine contraction and decrease blood loss.
  • Ergometrine–oxytocin may be used in the absence of hypertension in women at increased risk of haemorrhage, as it reduces the risk of minor PPH (500–1000 mL) — but carries a five-fold increased risk of nausea/vomiting and blood pressure elevation.
  • For women at increased risk, a combination of preventative measures may be superior to oxytocin alone.
  • Consider IV TXA 0.5–1.0 g, in addition to oxytocin, at Caesarean section in women at increased risk of PPH.
  • Delayed cord clamping (≥1 minute, ideally ≥2 minutes) is beneficial to the newborn; routine early cord clamping as part of active management can no longer be recommended.

11 · Other Haemostatic Drugs & Topical Agents

Desmopressin (DDAVP)
  • Synthetic arginine vasopressin; stimulates release of vWF from endothelial cells.
  • von Willebrand disease subtypes: type 1 = quantitative defect; type 2 = qualitative defects; type 3 = no vWF.
  • Most clotting factors including vWF increase during pregnancy, but those with severe type 1 vWD may benefit from DDAVP in the setting of PPH.
  • Patients with type 2 and type 3 vWD would have limited benefit (qualitative defect / absent vWF).
  • Contraindicated in the 2B subtype of vWD.
  • Can cause severe hyponatraemia — restrict free water for 24 h after use.
  • Because the mechanism is release of stored vWF, tachyphylaxis occurs rapidly — repeat dosing in a short time frame is likely to be ineffective.
  • No current indication for desmopressin during PPH outside these specific bleeding-disorder contexts.
Prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC)
  • Contains the vitamin K-dependent clotting factors II, VII, IX and X. Available as 3-factor and 4-factor forms.
  • 4-factor PCC contains higher concentrations of FVII relative to the 3-factor formulation, plus protein S and C, and heparin.
  • Current use is limited to treatment of haemorrhage in patients needing urgent reversal of vitamin K antagonists.
  • Its role in PPH is yet to be defined — there is only a case report of use in PPH.
  • In vitro evidence suggests the doses required to support haemostasis are much lower than those in current prescribing information for warfarin reversal.
  • Theoretical advantages over FFP: more precise factor dosing, without the risks of TACO, TRALI or citrate toxicity.
Recombinant activated factor VII (rFVIIa)
  • Approved by the European Medicines Agency in 2022 for women with severe PPH unresponsive to uterotonic drugs.
  • Binds tissue factor, mediating activation of factor X and converting prothrombin to thrombin at the site of local injury.
  • Limited evidence for efficacy and safety in PPH, with concern regarding thrombotic risk.
  • Registry of 113 women across nine European countries: 81% received a single dose; clinically positive response rate >80%; 4 patients developed VTE, 1 developed myocardial infarction (though the patient had cardiac arrest before rFVIIa was given), 1 developed a skin rash. Reported doses varied; the most common was ≤7.2 mg (≈90 µg/kg or less for an 80 kg woman). rFVIIa failed to treat PPH in 13.8% of cases.
  • Not recommended as first-line therapy. RCOG reserves it for life-threatening PPH; it is not a substitute for other management strategies. Hospitals should prepare local guidance for intractable PPH involving haematologists.
  • In amniotic fluid embolism, use has been studied and found to be associated with worse outcomes than cohorts not receiving rFVIIa.
  • Off-label placebo-controlled trial analysis: rates of all arterial thromboembolic events were increased in patients receiving rFVIIa, including coronary arterial events — more pronounced in patients aged ≥65 years. No significant difference in venous thromboembolic events.
  • Effectiveness is markedly diminished by hypothermia, acidosis and low platelets — effective resuscitation towards normal physiology is a prerequisite.
  • Use with caution in hypercoagulable pregnant patients.
Topical haemostatic agents

These provide local haemostasis through thrombin or fibrin deposition to stabilise clot formation. They are useful during Caesarean delivery with bleeding at the surgical site, but may not be sufficient for haemostasis during a severe PPH.

Flowable agents

  • Floseal (Baxter) and Surgiflo (Johnson & Johnson) — indicated in gynaecological procedures.
  • Combine gelatine particles and thrombin. Thrombin acts at the end of the clotting cascade, converting fibrinogen to fibrin to assist local clot formation.
  • Effective even in the setting of factor deficiencies or platelet dysfunction.
  • Potential adverse effects: compression of structures, infection, allergic reaction.

Fibrin sealants

  • Tisseel (Baxter) and Evicel (Ethicon) — typically contain fibrinogen, thrombin and an antifibrinolytic such as TXA.
  • Create a stable clot by inhibiting fibrinolysis.
  • Biodegradable; not associated with foreign body reactions or tissue necrosis. The fibrin clot is typically absorbed within 14 days after placement.
Team communication Have a discussion with the surgical team about topical haemostatic agents in the setting of ongoing PPH, especially during operative delivery.

12 · Mechanical & Surgical Interventions

Principles & first-line mechanical measures
  • Clinicians should be prepared to use a combination of pharmacological, mechanical and surgical methods to arrest PPH, directed towards the causative factor.
  • Bimanual uterine compression is recommended as a temporising measure until definitive care is available for PPH due to uterine atony after vaginal delivery — it stimulates contraction of uterine muscle fibres.
  • "Rubbing up the fundus" and emptying the bladder represent first-line management of atonic PPH (professional consensus, no published trials).
  • Compression of the aorta may be a temporary but effective measure to allow time for resuscitation and arrival of surgical support.
  • If pharmacological measures fail to control the haemorrhage, surgical interventions should be initiated sooner rather than later.
  • The use of pharmacological agents other than those detailed should not delay recourse to surgery.
Intrauterine balloon tamponade
  • An appropriate first-line 'surgical' intervention for most women where uterine atony is the only or main cause of haemorrhage.
  • Tamponade using hydrostatic balloon catheters has superseded uterine packing for atonic PPH.
  • Devices described: Foley catheter, Bakri balloon, Sengstaken–Blakemore oesophageal catheter, condom catheter. The urological Rusch balloon is described as preferable by virtue of larger capacity, ease of use and low cost.
  • Scottish Confidential Audit of Severe Maternal Morbidity: of 339 women with estimated blood loss ≥2500 mL, balloon tamponade was used in 82 cases and successfully avoided hysterectomy in 75 (91%).
  • Effective in 97% of cases of PPH in resource-poor settings (systematic review).
  • The "tamponade test": a positive test (control of PPH after balloon inflation) indicates laparotomy is not required; a negative test (continued PPH) is an indication to proceed to laparotomy.
  • No clear evidence on duration — in most cases 4–6 hours of tamponade should be adequate. Ideally remove during daytime hours, in the presence of appropriate senior staff, because of the risk of rebleeding.
Haemostatic compression sutures
  • Most commonly used at Caesarean delivery to arrest haemorrhage, particularly in refractory uterine atony.
  • B-Lynch suture (1997): requires hysterotomy for insertion — particularly suitable when the uterus has already been opened for Caesarean section.
  • Hayman technique (2002): modified compression suture that does not require hysterotomy.
  • Double vertical compression sutures are effective for PPH due to atony and placenta praevia — possibly a dual action of reducing uterine blood flow and compressing the bleeding surface.
  • Prospective population-based study of 211 women: overall failure rate leading to hysterectomy was 25%. No difference between B-Lynch, modified B-Lynch and other techniques. Risk factors for hysterectomy included increasing age and vaginal delivery. A delay of 2–6 hours between delivery and compression suture was independently associated with a four-fold increased risk of hysterectomy.
  • Scottish audit: hysterectomy averted in 16 of 21 (76%) women with blood loss ≥2500 mL treated with brace suturing.
  • Compression sutures are associated with a low complication rate. Higher risk of uterine ischaemia when combined with vessel ligation. No negative impact on fertility reported.
  • Keep a laminated diagram of the brace suture technique in theatre.
Vascular ligation, embolisation & hysterectomy

Stepwise uterine devascularisation

  • Successive ligation of: (i) one uterine artery, (ii) both uterine arteries, (iii) low uterine arteries, (iv) one ovarian artery, (v) both ovarian arteries.
  • Original case series of 103 patients with intractable PPH not responding to medical management was effective in all cases without hysterectomy.

Internal iliac artery ligation

  • Requires a high degree of surgical skill and training; may be associated with ureteric injury — involve a senior gynaecologist or vascular surgeon.
  • Case series of 84 women: hysterectomy was subsequently required in 33 (39%).
  • Subsequent fertility and pregnancy outcomes do not appear to be impaired.

Interventional radiology

  • Offers a minimally invasive, fertility-preserving method to treat refractory haemorrhage.
  • Retrospective study of 251 patients: arterial embolisation arrested bleeding in 86.5% (217/251). Caesarean delivery, DIC and transfusion of >10 units of packed red cells were related to failed embolisation.
  • Selective arterial occlusion using balloons may stem blood flow to the common internal iliac artery or aorta, either to prevent or treat PPH.
  • REBOA (resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta) has resulted in better outcomes than standard therapy without REBOA, but is associated with serious complications including ischaemia.
  • Complications of embolisation: ischaemia, thrombosis, arterial rupture.
  • Follow-up studies suggest no impairment of menstruation, fertility or obstetric outcomes.
  • Logistically, where equipment or an interventional radiologist is unavailable, uterine balloon tamponade is the more appropriate first-line treatment.

Hysterectomy

  • The definitive last resort to control obstetric haemorrhage; associated with significant morbidity.
  • Decision should be made by an experienced consultant clinician, and preferably discussed with a second experienced clinician when feasible.
  • Early recourse is recommended, especially where bleeding is associated with placenta accreta or uterine rupture.
  • Do not delay until the woman is in extremis, or while less definitive procedures with which the surgeon has little experience are attempted.
  • Subtotal hysterectomy is the operation of choice in many instances, unless there is trauma to the cervix or a morbidly adherent placenta in the lower segment.

13 · Anaesthetic Considerations

The anaesthetist's role in PPH
  • Management of PPH requires a multidisciplinary approach. The anaesthetist plays a crucial role in maintaining haemodynamic stability and, if necessary, determining and administering the most appropriate method of anaesthesia.
  • A senior anaesthetist should be consulted early to help assess, initiate and continue prompt resuscitation — bringing expertise in fluid and transfusion therapy and in managing critically ill patients.
  • Anaesthetists should be familiar with the range of mechanical and surgical options available to treat haemorrhage.
  • Women who suffer MOH should be managed in a high-dependency area with the capacity to deliver high-quality maternal critical care, in the presence of the infant where possible.
Choice of anaesthetic technique
  • Women may need examination under anaesthesia to evaluate the cause of haemorrhage, repair genital tract tears, drain a haematoma, or perform other interventions to control bleeding.
  • Central neuraxial anaesthesia has become the technique of choice in the obstetric population and has resulted in a reduction in maternal mortality.
  • However, general anaesthesia may be preferable to regional anaesthesia in cases of:
    • haemodynamic instability
    • compromised consciousness
    • questionable coagulation status
  • General anaesthesia in obstetric patients is associated with increased morbidity and mortality compared with regional anaesthesia due to physiological changes in pregnancy — but this must be weighed against the risks of regional anaesthesia in an unstable or coagulopathic patient.
  • Choose induction agents that avoid cardiovascular decompensation — 'cardiostable' agents such as ketamine may be preferred.
  • Invasive arterial pressure monitoring allows detection of rapid shifts in arterial pressure and close titration of vasopressors and fluid balance.
  • Central venous access should be considered if vasopressors might be needed; it also provides a route for rapid fluid replacement. Use ultrasound to make the procedure safer.
  • An obstetric early warning score system (MEOWS) helps in early identification of continuing bleeding, especially when it is not apparent.

14 · Post-PPH Care, Secondary PPH & Governance

Monitoring, HDU/ICU and documentation
  • Continuous physiological monitoring is necessary, with parameters recorded over time on a flow chart to give good visual cues on clinical progress.
  • Continually re-evaluate the woman's physiological condition even when bleeding appears to have stopped — this is essential to recognise continuing bleeding.
  • Major PPH monitoring protocol: temperature every 15 minutes; continuous pulse, BP and respiratory rate (oximeter, ECG, automated BP); Foley catheter to monitor urine output.
  • Consider transfer to intensive care once bleeding is controlled, or monitoring at a high-dependency unit on the delivery suite, depending on severity.
  • A prospective audit found the majority of women with major PPH received high-dependency care on the labour ward, with only 21% admitted to intensive care — care may be better provided by obstetricians and anaesthetists on the labour ward.
  • Accurate documentation is essential: staff in attendance and arrival times, sequence of events, drugs with timing and sequence, time of surgical intervention, maternal condition throughout, and timing of fluids and blood products given.
  • The scribe is crucial — the structured proforma acts as a checklist of available interventions.
Debriefing & longer-term sequelae
  • Offer the woman (possibly with her birthing partner) an opportunity to discuss the events surrounding the haemorrhage at a mutually convenient time.
  • Major PPH can be traumatic and has been associated with subsequent post-traumatic stress disorder and postnatal depression / fear of further childbirth.
  • Discussion of future pregnancy should include the likelihood of recurrent PPH and any fears regarding pregnancy and childbirth.
  • Arrange appropriate investigations as necessary — e.g. testing for coagulopathies if there are other indicators, and screening for Sheehan syndrome (postpartum hypopituitarism secondary to hypotension).
Secondary PPH (24 h – 12 weeks)
  • Causes are numerous: endometritis, retained products of conception (RPOC), subinvolution of the placental implantation site.
  • Management includes assessment of haemodynamic status, assessment of blood loss, and evaluation of the woman's concerns.
  • Assess vaginal microbiology — high vaginal and endocervical swabs — and start appropriate antimicrobial therapy when endometritis is suspected.
  • Where endometritis is confirmed, clindamycin plus gentamicin is appropriate; once uncomplicated endometritis has clinically improved with IV therapy, there is no additional benefit from further oral therapy.
  • Pelvic ultrasound may help exclude retained products, although the diagnosis is unreliable — reported sensitivities 44–94% and specificities 16–92%. An echogenic mass in the uterine cavity and an anteroposterior cavity diameter above the 90th centile (~25 mm on days 1–7 postpartum) is associated with RPOC.
  • Take clinical findings into account (degree of bleeding, whether the cervical os is open) before deciding on surgery.
  • Surgical evacuation of retained placental tissue should be undertaken or supervised by an experienced clinician — it carries a risk of uterine perforation (1.5%) and Asherman's syndrome. Consider ultrasound guidance.
  • Uterotonics (misoprostol, ergometrine) have been recommended although evidence is limited. Transcatheter arterial embolisation and balloon tamponade have been used for ongoing bleeding.
Risk management & audit standards
  • Every maternity unit should have a multidisciplinary protocol for the management of PPH.
  • All staff involved in maternity care should receive training in the management of obstetric emergencies, including PPH.
  • Training for PPH should be multiprofessional and include team rehearsals. Simulation training improves knowledge, practical skills, communication and team performance; training in a simulation centre did not further improve outcome compared with training at a local unit.
  • All cases of PPH involving a blood loss greater than 1500 mL should be the subject of a formal clinical incident review.

Auditable topics

  • Proportion of women screened for antenatal anaemia (100%)
  • Proportion of women offered uterotonics for the third stage of labour (100%)
  • Proportion of women undergoing assessment of risk factors for PPH when presenting in labour (100%)
  • Appropriate documentation of management, especially timing of events (100%)
  • Notification to the risk management team of PPH with blood loss >1500 mL (100%)
  • Proportion of the multidisciplinary team who have undergone skills drills training in PPH (100%)

16 · Ten High-Yield Case Examples

1 Minor PPH after spontaneous vaginal delivery — atonic uterus
Scenario A 28-year-old P1 delivers vaginally after a 6-hour labour. Estimated blood loss 750 mL. She is mildly tachycardic (HR 105), BP 118/72, uterus soft on palpation. Placenta and membranes appear complete.
Immediate actions
  • This is minor PPH (500–1000 mL) without clinical shock.
  • IV access with one 14-gauge cannula; send 20 mL blood for group & screen, FBC, coagulation screen including fibrinogen.
  • Record pulse, RR and BP every 15 minutes; commence warmed crystalloid.
  • Rub up the fundus; ensure the bladder is empty (Foley).
  • Oxytocin 5 IU slow IV (may repeat); consider ergometrine 0.5 mg if not hypertensive; start oxytocin infusion 40 IU in 500 mL at 125 mL/h.
  • Consider TXA 1 g IV.
Teaching point Visual estimation underestimates blood loss — reassess continuously. A soft uterus plus ongoing bleeding is atony until proven otherwise. Escalate to the multidisciplinary team if bleeding continues or the patient becomes unstable.
2 Placental abruption with early consumptive coagulopathy
Scenario A 33-year-old at 36 weeks presents with painful vaginal bleeding and a tense, tender uterus. Fetal death is confirmed. She is tachycardic and oozing from IV puncture sites. Initial fibrinogen is 1.4 g/L.
Key issue Abruption causes localised consumptive coagulopathy at the placental bed, consuming coagulation factors — especially fibrinogen. This can progress to DIC and is a classic setting for hypofibrinogenaemia. Bleeding plus clot formation consumes factors rapidly.
Management
  • Anticipate massive haemorrhage — activate the massive transfusion protocol and call senior help early.
  • Because coagulopathy is likely, consider early FFP (12–15 mL/kg) rather than waiting for laboratory results.
  • Replace fibrinogen to maintain >2 g/L — 2 pools cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate ≈60 mg/kg.
  • Give TXA 1 g IV, repeat at 30 min if bleeding continues.
  • Cross-match at least 4 units; transfuse to target Hb >80 g/L.
  • If available, use ROTEM FIBTEM A5 to guide fibrinogen replacement (target >12 mm).
  • Prepare for expedited delivery and possible surgical intervention.
Teaching point AFE, uterine rupture and placental abruption may all cause early-onset DIC. In these conditions, do not wait for laboratory confirmation before treating coagulopathy. (Note: rFVIIa has been associated with worse outcomes in AFE.)
3 Placenta accreta spectrum at elective Caesarean — anticipated MOH
Scenario A 38-year-old with two previous Caesarean sections has a planned Caesarean at 36 weeks for placenta praevia with suspected accreta. MRI suggests percreta.
Preparation
  • Structured multidisciplinary planning reduces blood loss, transfusion and emergency Caesarean delivery — involve obstetrics, anaesthesia, radiology, haematology and transfusion medicine.
  • Ensure delivery in a unit with an on-site blood bank; cross-match adequate units; activate massive transfusion protocol.
  • Consider cell salvage, and discuss with the patient beforehand.
  • Consider preventive/selective arterial balloon occlusion or interventional radiology support.
  • Senior anaesthetist to plan technique — general anaesthesia may be preferable if haemodynamic instability is anticipated; invasive arterial monitoring is advisable.
  • Consider IV TXA 0.5–1.0 g at Caesarean section in women at increased risk of PPH.
Teaching point For abnormal placentation, the battle is won before the incision. Aortic cross-clamping and REBOA are temporising measures. Early recourse to hysterectomy is recommended for placenta accreta — do not delay while less definitive procedures are attempted.
4 Refractory atony after labour augmentation — oxytocin receptor desensitisation
Scenario A woman augmented with an oxytocin infusion for 8 hours has a Caesarean delivery. Despite 10 IU oxytocin IV bolus and a 40 IU infusion, the uterus remains atonic with ongoing bleeding.
Key issue Prolonged oxytocin exposure causes oxytocin receptor desensitisation — the myometrium fails to respond to further oxytocin. Labouring women with oxytocin augmentation require much greater doses than those at elective Caesarean delivery.
Management
  • Escalate to agents that act on different receptors: prostaglandins (carboprost 250 µg IM/intramyometrial every 15 min, max 8 doses — caution asthma; misoprostol 800 µg sublingually) and/or ergometrine 200–500 µg IM (contraindicated in hypertension).
  • These are unaffected by oxytocin desensitisation.
  • Proceed to mechanical measures — bimanual compression, then intrauterine balloon tamponade, then compression sutures.
  • Give TXA 1 g IV and correct coagulopathy.
Teaching point If the uterus does not respond to escalating oxytocin, switch drug class rather than simply increasing the oxytocin dose. If significant atonic bleeding continues after a third dose of carboprost, consider theatre for examination under anaesthesia.
5 Oxytocin bolus in the hypovolaemic patient — iatrogenic decompensation
Scenario A woman has lost an estimated 1800 mL after a prolonged labour. She is tachycardic with a systolic BP of 85 mmHg. A colleague administers 10 IU oxytocin as a rapid IV bolus. She becomes profoundly hypotensive with ST-T changes on the monitor.
Key issue Oxytocin's extrauterine effects are most serious on the cardiovascular system, and correlate strongly with dose and speed of administration. In the context of reduced intravascular volume, a rapid oxytocin bolus can precipitate rapid decompensation — and deaths have occurred in this situation.
Management
  • Stop the bolus; support with rapid volume resuscitation and blood products.
  • Consider vasopressor support; obtain invasive arterial monitoring.
  • Give any further oxytocin slowly and in reduced dose (≤5 IU IV slowly, the maximum allowable IV bolus in the UK).
  • Reduce oxytocin infusion volume in women at risk of fluid overload/hyponatraemia (e.g. pre-eclampsia) due to its antidiuretic-like effect.
Teaching point Resuscitate volume first, then give uterotonics slowly. The same applies to carbetocin — deliver slowly in haemodynamic instability or cardiovascular disease.
6 Viscoelastic-guided fibrinogen replacement (FIBTEM A5 8 mm)
Scenario Ongoing PPH with estimated blood loss 2000 mL despite uterotonics. Laboratory fibrinogen is pending (sent 20 min ago). ROTEM shows FIBTEM A5 = 8 mm.
Interpretation
  • FIBTEM assesses the fibrinogen contribution to clot strength (cytochalasin D eliminates platelet contribution).
  • FIBTEM A5 <10 mm is associated with prolonged bleeds and is an independent predictor of PPH >2500 mL.
  • A5 12 mm ≈ fibrinogen 2 g/L — the target.
Action
  • Give 2 pools cryoprecipitate (raises fibrinogen ≈1 g/L) or fibrinogen concentrate ≈60 mg/kg (typically 2–4 g).
  • Recheck FIBTEM; target A5 >12 mm.
  • Remember: FFP is a poor fibrinogen source (2–2.5 g/L) and may worsen relative hypofibrinogenaemia in a parturient whose baseline is ~5 g/L.
Teaching point Do not give fibrinogen concentrate empirically to a patient with normal fibrinogen — the FIB-PPH trial showed no benefit when baseline fibrinogen was 4.5 g/L. Viscoelastic testing allows targeted replacement and cost savings.
7 Massive haemorrhage, 8 units RBC given, no laboratory results
Scenario A woman with uterine rupture has received 8 units of packed red cells and remains haemorrhagic. Coagulation results and platelet count are still unavailable.
Action
  • Give 2 pools of cryoprecipitate and 1 pool of platelets empirically (RCOG).
  • If bleeding continues and no haemostatic results are available, FFP should have been started after 4 units of RBC at 12–15 mL/kg and a 6:4 RBC:FFP ratio maintained until results are available.
  • Give TXA 1 g IV (repeat after 30 min if bleeding continues).
  • Keep the patient warm; correct calcium; monitor lactate and base deficit.
  • Targets: Hb >80 g/L, platelets >50 × 10⁹/L, PT/APTT <1.5× normal, fibrinogen >2 g/L.
Teaching point Fixed-ratio transfusion protocols derived from trauma have no proven benefit in PPH and increase TACO/TRALI risk — but when no results are available and bleeding is ongoing, empirical component therapy is appropriate. Order components as soon as the need is anticipated: thawing causes delay.
8 PPH in a Jehovah's Witness refusing blood products
Scenario A 31-year-old Jehovah's Witness with a documented advance decision refusing allogeneic blood has a PPH of 1500 mL after Caesarean delivery. She is tachycardic with a systolic BP of 95 mmHg.
Management
  • Identify and counsel such patients early in the antenatal period — plan ahead rather than in crisis.
  • Maximise red cell mass antenatally: iron supplementation, parenteral iron, ± recombinant erythropoietin; treat anaemia.
  • Use intraoperative cell salvage — it may be acceptable to patients who would not otherwise accept transfusion. Use a double-suction technique.
  • Give TXA 1 g IV early and repeat after 30 min if bleeding continues.
  • Use uterotonics aggressively, and proceed to mechanical/surgical measures (balloon tamponade, compression sutures, vessel ligation, hysterectomy) sooner rather than later.
  • Minimise iatrogenic blood loss: small-volume sampling tubes, closed circuits, avoid unnecessary blood tests.
  • Consider high-flow oxygen, permissive hypotension, and early HDU/ICU involvement.
Teaching point Respect the patient's autonomy, but do not let refusal of blood delay definitive surgical control of bleeding. Surgical intervention is often the most effective "blood conservation" measure available.
9 Hypertensive and asthmatic patient — choosing a uterotonic
Scenario A woman with severe pre-eclampsia and well-controlled asthma has an atonic PPH after Caesarean delivery. She is already on labetalol and magnesium sulphate.

10 Secondary PPH at day 10 — endometritis and retained products
Scenario A woman presents 10 days post-vaginal delivery with heavy, offensive vaginal bleeding, lower abdominal pain and a temperature of 38.2 °C. The cervical os is open.
Assessment
  • Assess haemodynamic status and quantify blood loss; assess the woman's concerns.
  • Take high vaginal and endocervical swabs for microbiology.
  • Perform pelvic ultrasound to look for retained products of conception — but remember the diagnosis is unreliable (sensitivity 44–94%, specificity 16–92%). An echogenic mass with cavity diameter >90th centile (~25 mm on days 1–7) suggests RPOC.
Management
  • If endometritis is suspected, start antimicrobial therapy — clindamycin plus gentamicin. Once uncomplicated endometritis has clinically improved with IV therapy, there is no additional benefit from further oral therapy.
  • Surgical evacuation of retained placental tissue should be undertaken or supervised by an experienced clinician — risks include uterine perforation (1.5%) and Asherman's syndrome. Consider ultrasound guidance.
  • Uterotonics (misoprostol, ergometrine) have been used with limited evidence.
  • For ongoing bleeding, transcatheter arterial embolisation and balloon tamponade have been employed.
Teaching point Secondary PPH is a different disease from primary PPH. Infection and retained tissue dominate — take swabs, treat sepsis, and involve a senior clinician for any surgical evacuation.
