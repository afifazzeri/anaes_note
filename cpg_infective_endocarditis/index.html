2Diagnosis Essentials & Modified Duke Criteria

Clinical suspicion — triggers

  • Fever + new/changed murmur, or fever of unexplained origin with a history of heart disease or previous IE.
  • Unexplained embolic phenomena — brain/liver/spleen (left-sided) or lung (right-sided).
  • Focal neurological signs of unknown aetiology.
  • Heart failure that cannot be accounted for.
  • Haemorrhages in unusual locations.

Pre-existing risk factors: previous IE · pre-existing cardiac disease · prosthetic valve or prosthetic cardiac material · intracardiac device · IVDU · chronic IV access (dialysis catheters, chemoports, neonatal CVCs) · congenital heart disease · elderly or immunocompromised · diabetes, HIV, malignancy.

Fever may be absent or blunted Temperature may be normal or subnormal in subacute cases, the elderly and the immunocompromised. A normal temperature does not exclude IE.

Major criteria

  • Blood culture positive for IE — typical organisms (VGS, S. bovis, HACEK, S. aureus, community-acquired enterococci without a primary focus) from 2 separate cultures; or persistently positive cultures; or single positive Coxiella burnetii / phase 1 IgG >1:800.
  • Evidence of endocardial involvement — oscillating intracardiac mass, abscess, new partial dehiscence of prosthetic valve, or new valvular regurgitation. TEE recommended for prosthetic valves / complicated IE.

Minor criteria

  • Predisposition — heart condition or IVDU
  • Fever >38 °C
  • Vascular phenomena — major arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial haemorrhage, conjunctival haemorrhage, Janeway lesions
  • Immunological phenomena — glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, rheumatoid factor
  • Microbiological evidence not meeting a major criterion; or serological evidence of active infection
Limitations of Duke criteria Lower accuracy in early disease, prosthetic valve endocarditis (TTE sensitivity 36%, TEE 82%) and pacemaker/defibrillator lead IE (TTE 23%, TEE 94%). If clinical suspicion is high but criteria give "possible IE": repeat TTE/TEE, advanced microbiology, cardiac CT, cerebral MRI, SPECT/CT or 18F-FDG PET/CT.

3Blood Cultures & Microbiology — Practical Rules
  • Timing: any time — bacteraemia in IE is continuous. No need to wait for fever spikes. Collect at 30-minute intervals between samples.
  • Number: at least 3 sets. If the initial 3 sets are negative at 24 h, obtain 2 more (total 5).
  • Volume: 10 mL per bottle in adults; 1–3 mL per paediatric bottle. Volume is the single most important factor in recovery.
  • Bottles: each set = 1 aerobic + 1 anaerobic. Anaerobic culture is vital for nutritionally variant streptococci and facultative anaerobes such as Enterobacteriaceae.
  • Site: separate peripheral venepunctures. Avoid central venous or indwelling catheters (higher contamination).
  • Asepsis: 70% isopropyl alcohol, then chlorhexidine gluconate in alcohol over ~5 cm, rub ≥30 s, allow to dry, do not re-palpate.
  • Transport: to laboratory within 2–4 h; do not refrigerate inoculated bottles.
  • Repeat cultures 3–4 days after starting treatment to document clearance of bacteraemia. If still positive on adequate therapy → look for metastatic infection.
Skin contaminants — interpret with care CoNS, Bacillus spp., Corynebacterium spp., Propionibacterium spp., viridans streptococci, Aerococcus, Micrococcus. These may be significant if grown from ≥2 cultures drawn on separate occasions.

Blood culture–negative IE (BCNIE)

No growth after ≥3 independent samples and 5 days of incubation. Three main causes:

  1. Partially treated IE — most common (prior antimicrobials).
  2. Fastidious organisms — Brucella, Nocardia, nutritionally variant streptococci, fungi, Legionella pneumophila (extend incubation to 2 weeks; special media).
  3. True BCNIE — intracellular bacteria not routinely culturable: Bartonella spp., Coxiella burnetii, Tropheryma whipplei.

Histopathology

Send resected valve tissue, endocardial vegetation and excised abscess wall for HPE and culture. Formalin for HPE (10% formalin, 3–4× tissue volume, minimum 30 mL); normal saline for culture — separate containers. Special stains: Gram (Brown-Hopps/Brown-Brenn), Giemsa, acridine orange, Warthin-Starry (Bartonella), Ziehl-Nielsen (AFB), Gimenez (Coxiella, Legionella), PAS (Tropheryma, fungi), Gomori-Grocott (fungi).

When to do TEE

  • Poor/suboptimal transthoracic window (obesity, COPD, previous sternotomy).
  • High clinical suspicion but negative TTE (e.g. typical IE organism on cultures).
  • Community-acquired Staphylococcus bacteraemia with no obvious focus.
  • All prosthetic valves and prosthetic material (conduits).
  • High-risk features — new AV block on ECG suggesting periannular extension.
  • Peri-operative assessment of valve dysfunction, perivalvular extension and surgical success.
  • CIED infection — TEE required regardless of TTE findings.

Echo findings suggestive of IE

Vegetation · abscess · fistula · perforation · valve leaflet aneurysm · new partial dehiscence of a prosthetic valve.

Measure vegetation/abscess size by largest diameter in ≥2 dimensions, not circumference, and document the view used.

A negative echo does not exclude IE Repeat TTE or TEE within a week if suspicion persists — or earlier in S. aureus bacteraemia. False positives occur with thrombus, fibroelastoma, Lambl's excrescences, cusp prolapse, chordal rupture, degenerative/myxomatous disease, strands, Libman-Sacks lesions, antiphospholipid syndrome, marantic vegetations, Chiari network.

Other imaging modalities

  • MSCT — perivalvular extension, abscess/pseudoaneurysm/fistula anatomy, extracardiac complications (pulmonary, CNS, intra-abdominal, peripheral mycotic aneurysms), pre-operative coronary assessment, aortic root definition. Limitations: radiation, contrast nephrotoxicity, poor sensitivity for valve perforation.
  • MRI — more sensitive than CT for cerebral embolic lesions; 60–80% of acute IE patients have cerebral embolic lesions, mostly silent. Detecting cerebral lesions in neurologically asymptomatic patients adds 1 minor Duke criterion. May influence timing of surgery.
  • Nuclear imaging — 18F-FDG PET/CT and radiolabelled WBC SPECT/CT can upgrade "possible" to "definite" IE and are useful in prosthetic valve endocarditis (ESC 2015 added abnormal uptake around a prosthesis implanted >3 months as a new major criterion). Availability is limited locally.
Anaesthetic implication Intra-operative TEE is recommended for all IE cases undergoing surgery — to define the mechanism and severity of valve dysfunction, detect perivalvular extension, and assess the result of repair/replacement before separation from bypass.

7ICU Monitoring of the IE Patient

Daily clinical monitoring

  • Fever: usually resolves within a few days of appropriate therapy, but may recur in ~30%. If fever persists beyond 10 days, evaluate for suppurative complications — abdominal, pulmonary or intracardiac abscess.
  • Examine regularly for heart failure, embolic events, ongoing sepsis and neurological sequelae.

ECG — daily

Conduction defects = perivalvular extension until proven otherwise Daily ECG to monitor rhythm and detect conduction defects. Especially important in prosthetic valve IE and native valve IE, where infection can extend to the conduction pathways abruptly. New AV block, particularly with aortic valve involvement, is a sign of abscess formation.

Laboratory

  • CRP and ESR — daily in the acute period, then at least twice weekly.
  • FBC — white cell count, haemoglobin, platelets (thrombocytopenia).
  • Renal profile — acute renal failure is a complication of IE and may require dialysis.
  • Liver function tests; coagulation profile if anticoagulated.
  • Antimicrobial peak and trough levels as appropriate; assess for clinical features of toxicity.

Blood cultures

Repeat 3–4 days after commencing treatment to document eradication of bacteraemia. Persistently positive cultures on adequate levels of appropriate antimicrobials → look for metastatic infection.

Echocardiography during treatment

  • Repeat TTE if there is any change in clinical condition.
  • Assess haemodynamics and the cause of heart failure.
  • Look for uncontrolled infection — paravalvular abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula (TEE).
  • Monitor vegetation size and mobility (embolic risk).
  • Resolution: vegetations gradually shrink, become less mobile and more echogenic — but may never disappear.
  • Rarer complications: purulent pericarditis, coronary obstruction (regional wall motion abnormality), myocarditis (global hypokinesia).
  • Baseline pre-discharge TTE in uncomplicated cases.

Outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT)

Minimum 2 weeks of inpatient therapy first. Then consider OPAT if the patient is medically stable, with no heart failure, no neurological signs, no renal impairment, and no high-risk echocardiographic features. Requires an established OPAT service with trained staff and protocols — daily nurse review and doctor review once or twice weekly. Weekly checklist: clinical review for heart failure, embolic events, recurrent fever, new neurological signs, drug rash; compliance and vascular access check (thrombophlebitis); co-morbidity control; FBC, CRP, renal profile, LFTs; confirm planned antimicrobial duration and orders. As required: ECG, therapeutic drug monitoring, INR, echocardiogram.

8Complications — Recognition & Management
Refer to a Specialist Centre All IE patients who develop cardiac complications should be referred to a centre with cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery services — most need advanced imaging, surgical intervention and cardiac intensive care.

A. Heart failure

  • Most common complication — up to half of all cases; the most important predictor of mortality.
  • Usually caused by valvular dysfunction, not myocardial failure. More common with aortic than mitral dysfunction. Intracardiac fistulae are a less common cause.
  • Presentation: dyspnoea, pulmonary oedema, cardiogenic shock.
  • Echo: acute regurgitation — chamber size may be normal or only slightly enlarged, ejection fraction may be normal. Look for extensive leaflet destruction, massive regurgitation, abscess/pseudoaneurysm, perforation, secondary mitral lesions, aneurysm/fistula (best with TEE). In chronic regurgitation with superimposed IE, chambers are enlarged with impaired systolic function.
  • NT-proBNP is useful for diagnosis and monitoring therapy effectiveness.
Drug warning — aortic regurgitation Beta-blockers should not be used in aortic regurgitation — they increase diastolic time and therefore regurgitant volume. They may also cause harm in acute heart failure.
Anaesthetic implications — acute severe AR Maintain a relatively fast heart rate and avoid bradycardia (bradycardia prolongs diastole and worsens regurgitation). Avoid excessive afterload; vasodilators are useful. Positive-pressure ventilation reduces LV afterload and can be beneficial. Avoid IABP in aortic regurgitation. Have rapid pacing available. These patients may decompensate precipitously on induction.

B. Persistent infection & perivalvular extension

If fever persists and blood cultures remain positive after 7–10 days of therapy, consider:

  • Inadequate or inappropriate antimicrobial therapy.
  • Resistant microorganisms.
  • Complications — perivascular extension, extracardiac septic embolisation.
  • Thrombophlebitis / infected intravenous lines.

Actions: daily ECG for conduction abnormalities (AV block); TEE in persistent fever — especially prosthetic valves, aortic valve IE and S. aureus; ECG-gated cardiac CT for aortic root abscess or perivascular extension if TEE is unavailable.

Paravalvular extension is the most frequent cause of uncontrolled infection and carries a very high hospital mortality of up to 41%. Abscesses are more common in native aortic valve IE (near the membranous septum and AV node) and in PVE, where the annulus rather than the valve is usually the primary source.

C. Systemic embolism

  • Risk 20–50%. Highest in the first days after starting antimicrobials; falls rapidly after 2–3 weeks.
  • Left-sided IE → brain and spleen. Right-sided and pacemaker lead IE → pulmonary embolism.
  • Risk factors: vegetation >10 mm (>15 mm higher), mobility, anterior mitral valve leaflet location, increasing vegetation size on therapy, S. aureus, S. bovis, fungal endocarditis, previous embolism, multivalvular IE, elevated CRP.
  • Embolisation may occur before diagnosis, within the first 2–4 weeks of therapy, or late (up to 15–30 weeks) with poor antimicrobial response.
  • Distal emboli → metastatic infection or abscess (liver, kidneys, mesenteric vessels). Osteomyelitis in 2–6%, more common in IVDU.
  • 18F-FDG PET/CT is useful for detecting peripheral embolic and metastatic events when other imaging is negative.

D. Neurological complications

Symptomatic in 25–40%; most common in left-sided IE and early in the course. Types: ischaemic stroke/TIA, intracranial haemorrhage, mycotic aneurysm, cerebral abscess, meningitis.

9Anticoagulation & Antiplatelet Management
Core principle Cerebral haemorrhage is a major determinant of poor outcome in IE and occurs through: acute pyogenic arteritis from uncontrolled infection, haemorrhagic transformation of embolic infarcts, or rupture of mycotic aneurysms.

Antiplatelet therapy

  • Aspirin has not been shown to reduce mortality or morbidity in IE — initiation as treatment for IE is not recommended.
  • If the patient was on long-term aspirin before diagnosis, it may be continued if there are no bleeding complications.
  • The role of clopidogrel has not been extensively investigated.

Warfarin — native valve IE

SituationAction
No cerebrovascular ischaemic or haemorrhagic eventWarfarin may be continued if anticoagulated for non-valvular indications (AF, DVT)
Cerebrovascular ischaemic or haemorrhagic eventStop warfarin to prevent haemorrhagic transformation or expansion of the haemorrhage. May restart 2–3 weeks after the event or once the septic period is over. Stop all anticoagulation — NOACs and LMWH included. Their role in this context is not established.

Warfarin — mechanical prosthetic valve IE (MPVIE)

MPVIE carries higher mortality and poorer prognosis than native valve IE. Weigh the risk of cerebral haemorrhage on continued anticoagulation against thromboembolic risk if stopped.

Higher thromboembolic risk with: mitral prosthesis (vs aortic), non-bileaflet valves, early post-operative period (<3 months), atrial fibrillation, LV dysfunction, left atrial dilatation, previous thromboembolism, hypercoagulable states (e.g. pregnancy).

Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Clearly document the anticoagulation plan and who authorised it before any procedure.
  • Remember rifampicin will reduce warfarin effect and destabilise the INR — expect to increase the warfarin dose and monitor more frequently.
  • In MPVIE due to S. aureus, assume a high cerebral haemorrhage risk even without neurological signs.
10Surgical Indications & Timing — The Theatre Clock

Indications for surgery

  1. Severe valvular incompetence, heart failure or haemodynamic instability I/B — life-saving; improves survival.
  2. Persistent infection, uncontrolled sepsis or paravalvular extension I/B — fever or positive cultures persisting despite appropriate therapy for 5–7 days after excluding extracardiac causes.
  3. Fungal or multiresistant endocarditis I/B — fungal IE responds poorly to medical therapy (mortality up to 70%); multiresistant organisms; non-HACEK Gram-negatives; S. aureus without a favourable early response.
  4. Very large vegetations (>10 mm) or previous systemic embolism I/B — particularly with other embolic risk factors.

Why early surgery? Early intervention improves survival, prevents progressive heart failure/cardiogenic shock/multiorgan failure, decreases progression of infection and abscess formation, reduces systemic embolism, and increases the likelihood of valve repair rather than replacement. A meta-analysis of 8,141 patients showed early surgery within 2 weeks of diagnosis improved both early mortality and long-term survival.
Septic shock caveat In cardiogenic shock, surgery should be undertaken within 24 hours. In septic shock, it may be reasonable to delay 48 hours to allow stabilisation and better sepsis control — cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass in septic shock carries excessively high operative mortality.

Preventing systemic embolism

Most emboli occur before diagnosis and in the first 2 weeks; the rate falls significantly after 2–3 weeks of therapy. A randomised trial of 76 patients with left-sided native valve IE and vegetations >10 mm showed a lower composite of hospital death, embolic events and recurrent endocarditis at 6 months with early surgery within 48 hours (3% vs 23%) — largely driven by fewer embolic events. Surgery for embolism prevention should be performed urgently in the first few days of antimicrobial therapy, when risk is highest.

Right-sided endocarditis

5–10% of cases, mostly tricuspid; isolated pulmonary valve involvement is rare. Usually resolves with conservative treatment — surgery is uncommon. Avoid prosthetic valves where possible (most patients are IVDU with high risk of re-infection).

Indications for surgery IIa/C: persistent infection despite 2 weeks of appropriate antimicrobials · persistent vegetation >20 mm with recurrent septic pulmonary emboli · massive/worsening tricuspid regurgitation causing right heart failure unresponsive to medical therapy · failure or complications of percutaneous removal of infected pacing wires.

Prosthetic valve endocarditis — additional indications

Staphylococcal endocarditis I/C; severe prosthetic valve dysfunction or dehiscence I/C. About half of PVE patients undergo surgery, more commonly early PVE (within the first year). Recurrent PVE after surgery occurs in up to 18%.

Surgical principles

  • Valve repair preferred over replacement where possible — improves in-hospital and long-term survival, preserves ventricular function, eliminates prosthetic valve complications, reduces risk of infecting the new prosthesis.
  • Complete removal and radical debridement of all infected and necrotic material, leaving only healthy tissue that will hold suture.
  • Avoid excessive manipulation of the heart before cardiopulmonary bypass — prevents embolisation of vegetations.
  • Reconstruct with autologous or bovine pericardium, or Dacron, avoiding prosthetic material if possible.
  • Send vegetations/cardiac tissue for Gram stain, culture and HPE.
  • Prosthesis choice based on usual considerations — age, life expectancy, co-morbidities, anticoagulation compliance. No difference in infection risk between mechanical and bioprosthetic valves.
  • Consider bioprosthetic rather than mechanical replacement if mycotic cerebral aneurysms are identified pre-operatively, to avoid post-operative anticoagulation.
  • Continue antimicrobials to complete a total course of 6 weeks in most cases.
Pre-operative workup Investigate and treat the primary source; treat extracardiac sepsis (splenic and paravertebral abscesses) by splenectomy or percutaneous drainage to avoid infecting the new valve; investigate and manage cerebrovascular events; dental consultation. Imaging of brain, chest, abdomen, pelvis and maxillofacial area. MRA of the brain may be considered before cardiac surgery in patients without neurological complications who are at high risk of mycotic aneurysm.
Anaesthetic implications — IE cardiac surgery
  • Use CT-guided coronary angiography rather than conventional catheter angiography in aortic valve IE — reduced embolic risk.
  • Anticipate periannular abscess: annulus reconstruction, possible need for root replacement with homograft or stentless root.
  • Anticipate temporary or permanent pacing — periannular abscess and debridement can cause AV block.
  • Mitral valve repair may need pericardial patch, artificial neochordae; complete subvalvular apparatus preservation if replacement is needed.
  • Tricuspid: repair preferred; bioprosthesis reasonable in IVDU if anticoagulation compliance is a concern.
  • Where abscess cavities exist, they must be drained, necrotic tissue excised, and fistulous tracts closed.

12Specific Situations — CIED, TAVI/TPVI, Pregnancy, Paediatrics, CHD

Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED)

  • Serious, high mortality; incidence rising with wider device use and older population.
  • Risk factors: immunosuppression (steroids, diabetes, renal failure), oral anticoagulation, ageing/co-morbidity, failure of periprocedural prophylaxis, device revision/replacement, amount of indwelling hardware, operator experience, S. aureus bloodstream infection.
  • Presentation is vague and non-specific — a high index of suspicion is mandatory. Always consider CIED IE with unexplained fever in a patient with a device.
  • Staphylococci (especially CoNS) account for 60–80%. Methicillin resistance varies. Polymicrobial infection occurs.
  • Investigations: 3 or more sets of blood cultures before antimicrobials. TTE may show lead vegetation and tricuspid involvement, but a negative TTE does not exclude CIED IE — proceed to TEE. Both are preferable (TTE gives a baseline). If suspicion remains high with negative TTE and TEE, consider radiolabelled leucocyte scintigraphy or 18F-FDG PET/CT. Repeated TEE is not necessary after completing treatment.
  • Treatment: at least 4–6 weeks of antimicrobials. Complete hardware removal is advocated; if required, implant a temporary device until infection resolves. Blood cultures within 24 hours after hardware removal to confirm clearance. Reimplant on the contralateral side. Assess the need for reimplantation after extraction.
CIED recommendations I/C 3+ sets of blood cultures before antimicrobials · TEE regardless of blood culture or TTE findings · antimicrobials before and after extraction for definite CIED IE and isolated pocket infection · complete device and lead removal when no other source is found · reassess need for reimplantation after extraction · postpone reimplantation for days to weeks during antimicrobial therapy · a "temporary" ipsilateral device can be considered in pacemaker-dependent patients needing antimicrobials before reimplantation IIb/C · temporary pacing is not routinely recommended III/C · 18F-FDG PET/CT may be done with positive cultures and negative echo IIb/C.

Transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation (TPVI)

  • Early PVE incidence ≤1%. Clinical diagnosis requiring high suspicion.
  • Beyond Duke criteria: any increase in RVOT gradient unexplained by a structural complication (e.g. stent fracture), valvular involvement, or new-onset pulmonary regurgitation should raise high suspicion.
  • Risk factors: male sex, dental treatment, septic skin wound, reactivation of treated fungal infection, previous medically treated IE, multiple stents in RVOT, distorted RVOT anatomy, Contagra conduit and Melody valve (higher risk than homograft).
  • Fever (80%) and heart failure (22%) are the most common initial symptoms in transcatheter valve PVE.
  • Management: refer to a specialist centre. Antimicrobials for at least 6 weeks based on the organism (usually S. aureus). Surgical explantation of the infected conduit valve is recommended. In-hospital mortality associated with PVE episodes was 7.1%. Follow-up at a specialist centre.
  • Lifelong pre-procedural prophylactic antimicrobials are proposed for TPVI patients with previous IE or complex RVOT anatomy.

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation / replacement (TAVI/TAVR)

  • PVE rates similar to surgical groups in the PARTNER trial (1.0% transcatheter vs 1.5% surgical).
  • Typical organisms: Enterococcus spp., CoNS, S. aureus, Streptococcus spp.
  • Prolonged fever, valve murmur or new regurgitation → TTE; consider TEE because acoustic shadows limit TTE views.
  • Risk factors: diabetes mellitus, poor oral hygiene, non-adherence to dental prophylaxis.
  • First-line treatment is targeted antimicrobial therapy; surgery remains an option to be discussed in the context of complications.

Pregnancy

Refer all pregnant patients with IE to a Specialist Centre Multidisciplinary team: cardiologist, ID physician, obstetrician, obstetric anaesthetist, neonatologist — from the moment of diagnosis.
  • General IE management is the same as for non-pregnant patients.
  • Surgical indications are the same as for non-pregnant patients — but cardiac surgery should be delayed wherever possible until after delivery.
  • Produce a detailed pregnancy and delivery care plan.
  • Cardiac patients at risk should receive pre-pregnancy counselling on good skin and oral hygiene, and be referred for professional oral hygiene care throughout pregnancy.

Paediatric IE

  • Frequency is increasing due to improved survival in CHD and hospitalised neonates. Rheumatic heart disease is no longer a common cause in children except in some developing countries.
  • Three groups: children with CHD, children with acquired valvular heart disease, and neonates with normal heart structures.
  • Classical extracardiac signs (Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, splinter haemorrhages) are uncommon in children. More commonly seen are septic emboli to abdomen, brain and lungs.
  • Presentation may be indolent and indistinguishable from septicaemia, or acute and fulminant.
  • Diagnosis uses the modified Duke criteria; a high clinical suspicion is necessary. S. aureus bacteraemia is a major criterion irrespective of cause. Remove central venous catheters in prolonged bacteraemia before diagnosing IE.
  • TTE is more sensitive (97%) in young patients (weight <60 kg). TEE is an important adjunct for obesity, prosthetic grafts/valves/conduits, chest deformities, hyperinflated lungs or compromised respiratory function.
  • Management is predominantly medical; weight-based dosing is essential, especially in neonates. Remove or change all long-standing central venous catheters.
  • Mortality is highest in premature infants (31%) and in S. aureus IE.

Congenital heart disease (CHD)

Predisposing risk factors: complex cyanotic CHD (unrepaired and/or palliated, e.g. aortopulmonary shunts) · repaired CHD with prosthetic material including conduits, bioprosthetic homografts and/or CIED · repaired CHD with residual lesions · within 6 months of cardiac surgery or transcatheter device intervention · VSD with associated valve or outflow tract anomalies · age <3 years.

13Antimicrobial Prophylaxis
Key principle Antimicrobial prophylaxis is not routinely recommended for all cardiac patients. It is limited to those at highest risk of IE or of adverse outcome from IE. Maintaining good oral and skin hygiene is emphasised over prophylaxis.

Cardiac conditions with increased risk of IE — prophylaxis indicated

  • Prosthetic cardiac valves, or prosthetic material used for cardiac valve repair
  • Native valvular heart disease, including established rheumatic heart disease
  • Previous IE
  • Unrepaired cyanotic CHD, including palliative shunts and conduits
  • Completely repaired CHD with prosthetic material or devices — for the first 6 months after the procedure
  • Repaired CHD with residual defects at or adjacent to the site of a prosthetic patch or device (which inhibit endothelialisation)
  • Cardiac transplant recipients who develop cardiac valvulopathy

1510 High-Yield Case Examples
Case 1 · IVDU with tricuspid endocarditis and septic pulmonary emboli

Stem
A 28-year-old man who injects drugs presents with fever, pleuritic chest pain and multiple cavitating lung lesions. Blood cultures grow MSSA. Echo shows a large tricuspid vegetation.

Key issues

  • Right-sided MSSA native valve IE — cloxacillin 12 g/day IV in 4–6 divided doses.
  • Duration may be shortened to 2 weeks only if all criteria are met: good response, no metastatic infection or empyema, no cardiac/extracardiac complications, no prosthetic or left-sided involvement, vegetation <20 mm, no severe immunosuppression (CD4 <200).
  • Septic pulmonary emboli and pneumonia are common and do not alter treatment duration.
  • Surgery is uncommon; avoid prosthetic valves — most patients are IVDU with high re-infection risk.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Difficult IV access, opioid tolerance, potential for withdrawal — plan analgesia carefully.
  • Lung pathology may complicate ventilation; expect hypoxia and possible need for lung-protective strategies.
  • If surgery is required: indications are persistent infection >2 weeks despite therapy, vegetation >20 mm with recurrent septic emboli, or right heart failure from massive tricuspid regurgitation.
  • Rehabilitation for IVDU and education on relapse are part of discharge planning.
Case 2 · Acute aortic regurgitation with cardiogenic shock

Stem
A 62-year-old man with fever and a new early diastolic murmur becomes hypotensive and develops pulmonary oedema. Echo shows a perforated aortic leaflet with massive regurgitation and a vegetation.

Key issues

  • Heart failure is the most common complication of IE and the strongest predictor of mortality.
  • Cardiogenic shock from severe valve dysfunction = emergency surgery within 24 hours.
  • Echo may show normal chamber size and normal ejection fraction in acute regurgitation — do not be reassured by a normal EF.
Anaesthetic implications
  • Avoid beta-blockers — they prolong diastole and increase regurgitant volume.
  • Avoid bradycardia; maintain a relatively fast heart rate.
  • Avoid excessive afterload; vasodilators are useful.
  • Positive-pressure ventilation reduces LV afterload and may be beneficial.
  • Avoid IABP in aortic regurgitation.
  • Have rapid pacing and full cross-matched blood available — these patients decompensate abruptly on induction.
Case 3 · Mitral IE with a 14 mm vegetation and a recent embolic stroke

Stem
A 45-year-old woman with mitral valve IE and a 14 mm mobile vegetation develops sudden right-sided weakness. MRI shows a small cortical infarct without haemorrhage.

Key issues

  • Vegetation >10 mm = excess embolic risk; surgery indicated to prevent embolisation.
  • Avoid IV tPA or streptokinase, antiplatelets and warfarin in ischaemic complications of IE.
  • Clinically silent or small lesions should not delay cardiac surgery.
  • Risk of neurological deterioration post-cardiac surgery: ~20% in the first 3 days, 20–50% between days 4–14, 6–10% between days 15–28, <1% after 28 days.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Imaging within 24 hours of new neurological features.
  • Anticoagulation decisions must be individualised by the multidisciplinary IE team.
  • If urgent surgery is needed for heart failure or uncontrolled infection and the infarct is small without haemorrhage, surgery can proceed after ~2 weeks.
  • Remember the embolic risk is highest in the first days of antimicrobial therapy — early surgery is preventive.
Case 4 · Mechanical prosthetic valve IE due to S. aureus with intracranial haemorrhage

Stem
A 55-year-old man with a mechanical mitral valve on warfarin develops S. aureus bacteraemia and then a sudden reduction in consciousness. CT shows an intracerebral haemorrhage.

Key issues

  • Stop anticoagulation. Avoid anticoagulation for the first 2 weeks — the risk of further bleeding and haematoma expansion is highest then.
  • After 2 weeks, it is reasonable to restart cautiously with unfractionated heparin in most cases, guided by aPTT, balancing thromboembolic risk from the mechanical prosthesis.
  • If the neurosurgical team judges bleeding risk high, make a consensus team decision.
  • Postpone cardiac surgery for 4 weeks after clinically significant haemorrhage.
  • MPVIE due to S. aureus has a much higher risk of cerebral haemorrhage — even without neurological complications, warfarin can be stopped at diagnosis and substituted with unfractionated heparin for 2 weeks.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Document clearly who authorised stopping/restarting anticoagulation and when.
  • If surgery becomes imminent, consider conversion to heparin.
  • NOACs and LMWH should be stopped in the event of ischaemic stroke or intracerebral haemorrhage — their role here is not established.
Case 5 · CIED infection with lead vegetation

Stem
A 70-year-old with a permanent pacemaker presents with unexplained fever 4 months after a generator change. Blood cultures grow CoNS. TTE is unremarkable.

Key issues

  • CIED IE must always be considered with unexplained fever in a patient with a device — presentation is often vague.
  • Take 3 or more sets of blood cultures before antimicrobials.
  • A negative TTE does not exclude CIED IE — TEE is required regardless of blood culture or TTE findings (TEE sensitivity 94% vs TTE 23%).
  • If suspicion remains high with negative TTE and TEE, consider radiolabelled leucocyte scintigraphy or 18F-FDG PET/CT.
  • At least 4–6 weeks of antimicrobials; complete hardware removal is advocated; blood cultures within 24 hours after removal; reimplant on the contralateral side.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Pacemaker-dependent patients need a bridging strategy — a "temporary" ipsilateral device can be considered, but temporary pacing is not routinely recommended.
  • Reassess the need for reimplantation after extraction; postpone reimplantation for days to weeks during antimicrobial therapy.
  • Repeated TEE is not necessary after completing treatment.
Case 6 · Candida prosthetic valve endocarditis

Stem
A 60-year-old with a bioprosthetic aortic valve and recent prolonged ICU stay develops persistent fever and a new paravalvular leak. Blood cultures grow Candida albicans.

Key issues

  • Fungal IE responds poorly to medical therapy — mortality up to 70%.
  • Valve replacement is mandatory, combined with antifungal therapy.
  • Continue antifungal therapy for ≥6 weeks after surgery, longer with perivalvular abscess.
  • If valve replacement is not possible → lifelong suppressive fluconazole 400–800 mg daily.
  • Antifungals: amphotericin B deoxycholate 0.6–1.0 mg/kg/day ± flucytosine 25 mg/kg q6h, or lipid amphotericin 3–5 mg/kg/day, or an echinocandin (micafungin 150 mg, caspofungin 150 mg, anidulafungin 200 mg daily).
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Amphotericin is nephrotoxic and causes dose-related marrow toxicity — avoid in renal failure, prefer lipid formulations, monitor creatinine closely.
  • Anticipate difficult redo cardiac surgery with dense adhesions and possible root reconstruction.
  • Perivalvular abscess may require annulus reconstruction and carries high hospital mortality (up to 41%).
Case 7 · Elderly patient with enterococcal native valve IE and renal impairment

Stem
An 82-year-old with chronic kidney disease (CrCl 35 mL/min) has enterococcal mitral valve endocarditis. Creatinine is already elevated.

Key issues

  • For fully penicillin-susceptible Enterococcus with renal impairment (CrCl ≤50 mL/min) or in the elderly, the preferred regimen is ampicillin 2 g IV q4h PLUS ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h for 6 weeks — avoiding gentamicin nephrotoxicity.
  • This combination is not active against E. faecium.
  • Ceftriaxone should never be used alone for enterococcal infection — intrinsically resistant.
  • For native valve enterococcal IE, duration depends on symptom duration: <3 months → ampicillin 4 weeks + gentamicin 2 weeks; >3 months or prosthetic valve → both 6 weeks.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Acute renal failure is itself a complication of IE and may require dialysis — plan vascular access accordingly.
  • Dose-adjust all antimicrobials for renal function (see Section 11).
  • Careful fluid balance and avoidance of further nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast, aminoglycosides).
Case 8 · Penicillin-allergic patient with viridans streptococcal native valve IE

Stem
A 40-year-old with documented anaphylaxis to penicillin has native mitral valve IE due to penicillin-susceptible viridans streptococci.

Key issues

  • Use vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV q8–12h (max 2 g/dose) — target trough 10–15 mg/L for VGS. Duration 4 weeks (native valve).
  • Cephalosporins must be avoided with immediate-type penicillin hypersensitivity (anaphylaxis, angioedema, urticaria).
  • Cefazolin has inadequate blood-brain barrier penetration — watch for treatment failure if there is a brain abscess complicating MSSA IE.
  • Vancomycin is inferior to cloxacillin for MSSA endocarditis — use vancomycin for staphylococcal disease only when there is genuine immediate-type penicillin hypersensitivity or MRSA.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Infuse vancomycin over ≥60 minutes — rapid infusion causes histamine-mediated hypotension and "vancomycin flushing".
  • Monitor trough levels, especially with any change in renal function.
  • Check for cross-reactivity history carefully before any peri-operative antibiotic choice.
Case 9 · IE in pregnancy

Stem
A 26-year-old, 24 weeks pregnant, with known rheumatic heart disease presents with fever and a new murmur. Echocardiography confirms mitral valve IE.

Key issues

  • Refer to a Specialist Centre with a multidisciplinary team: cardiologist, ID physician, obstetrician, obstetric anaesthetist and neonatologist — from the moment of diagnosis.
  • General IE management is the same as for non-pregnant patients.
  • Surgical indications are the same as for non-pregnant patients, but cardiac surgery should be delayed wherever possible until after delivery.
  • Produce a detailed pregnancy and delivery care plan; the management of IE in pregnancy is also addressed in the Heart Disease in Pregnancy CPG (2016).
  • Provide pre-pregnancy counselling on good skin and oral hygiene; refer for professional oral hygiene care throughout pregnancy.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Anticipate the combined challenges of aortocaval compression, altered drug pharmacokinetics, and a potentially difficult airway.
  • If surgery cannot be delayed, plan for cardiopulmonary bypass in pregnancy with fetal monitoring and a multidisciplinary delivery plan.
  • Avoid beta-blockers in aortic regurgitation — relevant if the lesion involves the aortic valve.
Case 10 · Neonate with a central venous catheter and suspected IE

Stem
A premature neonate in NICU with a long-standing central venous catheter develops recurrent fever, thrombocytopenia and a new murmur.

Key issues

  • IE in the first month of life accounts for 7.3–18% of paediatric IE cases, driven by prolonged indwelling central venous catheters.
  • Common organisms: S. aureus, CoNS, Gram-negative species and Candida spp. — the last is associated with hyperalimentation and high glucose concentrations.
  • Mortality is highest in premature infants (31%) and in S. aureus IE.
  • Left-sided structures are usually affected.
  • Remove or change all long-standing central venous catheters — removal is advised in prolonged bacteraemia before diagnosing IE.
  • TTE is highly sensitive (97%) in children weighing <60 kg; TEE is an adjunct for prosthetic material, poor windows or complications.
  • Use weight-based antimicrobial dosing — never exceed the normal maximum adult dose.
Anaesthetic / ICU implications
  • Aminoglycoside trough targets in neonates are higher: <4 µmol/mL (<2 mcg/mL) in NICU/PICU versus <2 µmol/mL (<1 mcg/mL) generally.
  • Classical peripheral stigmata of IE are uncommon in children — septic emboli to abdomen, brain and lungs are more typical.
  • The presentation may be indistinguishable from septicaemia; maintain a high index of suspicion in any neonate with a central line and persistent fever.
16Outcome, Follow-Up & Relapse
  • After adequate antimicrobial therapy ± surgery, resolution occurs in most patients. Close monitoring with clinical examination and echocardiography is encouraged, especially in the first year.
  • Follow-up frequency depends on clinical status at discharge, residual valvular dysfunction, and the presence/severity of intra- and extracardiac complications.
  • Refer back to a cardiac centre if the patient develops relapse/reinfection, requires advanced imaging to quantify valvular or structural damage, or requires surgical intervention.

Surveillance should monitor for: relapse or reinfection (if febrile, take 3 sets of blood cultures before starting empirical antimicrobials) · worsening clinical status and progression of heart failure · severity and progression of valvular regurgitation (timing of surgery) · delayed antimicrobial toxicity (vestibular/auditory toxicity, Clostridioides difficile colitis).

Relapse and reinfection occur in 2–6%. A high rate of relapse is associated with S. aureus, Enterococcus and Gram-negative organisms such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa; IVDU with pre-treatment symptoms >3 months; previous IE; prosthetic material and CHD.

Factors increasing relapse rate: inadequate antimicrobial treatment (duration, dose, resistant organism) · polymicrobial infection in IVDU · empirical treatment in BCNIE · periannular extension · persistent metastatic foci · prosthetic valve IE · chronic dialysis. Treatment for relapse/reinfection is as for primary IE — up to 42 days.

Reinforce at follow-up Oral care and dental hygiene · rehabilitation for high-risk behaviour (IVDU) · good skin hygiene · discourage body piercing and tattoos.
