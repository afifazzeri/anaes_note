Golden rule: the mitral valve and the left ventricle are interdependent — what helps one can harm the other.
⭐ Key Points (the 60-second version)
  • Mitral valve and LV function are interdependent. In mitral regurgitation (MR), measured LV ejection fraction is falsely high.
  • Vasopressors should be used with caution in severe MR (they increase the regurgitant fraction).
  • New minimally invasive and percutaneous techniques are now available to repair the mitral valve.
  • Vasodilating drugs should be used with caution in mitral stenosis (fixed cardiac output → profound hypotension).
One-liner haemodynamics
Stenosis = fixed cardiac output → keep rate low-normal, preload normal, afterload up.
Regurgitation = "full, fast and forward flow" → keep rate high-normal, preload full, afterload down.
🧬 Anatomy of the Mitral Valve

The mitral valve separates the left atrium from the left ventricle and is an integral part of the high-pressure systemic circulation. Any pathology of the valve can therefore have critical physiological effects that the anaesthetist must anticipate. Anaesthesia alters the way the heart, valve and circulation interact.

The valve is a complex structure: two main leaflets, an annulus, tendinous cords and papillary muscles connecting the leaflets to the LV.

Annulus
  • A saddle-shaped structure shaped more like a "D" than an "O".
  • The aortic valve is wedged into the straight side of the D — there is fibrous continuity between the two valves, so one can easily be affected by surgery on the other.
  • The circumflex artery follows the line of the annulus as it passes anteriorly around the base of the heart → at risk during any mitral valve surgery.
  • Damage or ligation causes lateral wall ischaemia — an important area to assess on perioperative echocardiography.
Leaflets
  • Anterior leaflet hangs from the straight side of the D-shaped annulus — it is more superior and medial than truly anterior.
  • Posterior leaflet is C-shaped and divided into three scallops: P1, P2, P3; the opposing anterior segments are A1, A2, A3.
  • Free edges do not meet exactly — there is ~5 mm of overlap that seals the valve as the leaflet bodies push outwards like sails in systole.
  • At the corners, two commissures ensure a seal.
Papillary muscles & chordae
  • During systole, chordae tether the free edges of the leaflets to the two papillary muscles and prevent billowing back into the LA.
  • Muscles are anterolateral and posteromedial, sitting under the commissures; each passes chordae to both leaflets.
  • Anterolateral muscle: dual blood supply (left anterior descending + circumflex).
  • Posteromedial muscle: supplied solely by the right coronary artery in the more common right-dominant circulation.
Because it usually has a single supply, the posteromedial muscle is much more likely to infarct and rupture — e.g. in an inferior myocardial infarction.
🔴 Mitral Stenosis (MS)

A normal mitral valve has an area of > 4 cm², but symptoms are usually only present once stenosis is moderate–severe. Grading into mild, moderate or severe is based on both the mean pressure gradient across the valve and the valve area.

Aetiology & epidemiology
  • Rheumatic fever is by far the largest cause worldwide — although relatively rare in developed countries, where degenerative calcification or endocarditis are more likely causes.
  • Progression ≈ 0.1–0.3 cm² per year, greatly increased with repeated valve inflammation (e.g. recurrent rheumatic fever).
  • Inflammation → thickened, nodular valve that can fuse at the leaflet and commissural edges.
  • Chordae may become shortened or thickened → blood flowing around the cords on entering the LV causes a subvalvular obstruction.
  • Much less common causes: infiltrating diseases, congenital deformities, multi-system disease (e.g. sarcoidosis).
Signs & symptoms
  • Generally well tolerated — patients remain asymptomatic for many years.
  • Compensation is due to the compliant left atrium dilating and keeping pulmonary venous pressures stable.
  • As disease progresses, compensation is overcome → pulmonary hypertension develops.
  • Then: pulmonary oedema, worsening dyspnoea, fatigue, frequent lower respiratory tract infections.
  • Signs are often delayed further by a reduction in activity — seek them carefully.
  • Pregnancy and new atrial fibrillation cause rapid progression of pathophysiology and symptoms.
Murmur: low rumbling diastolic murmur, best heard in expiration at the apex with the bell of the stethoscope.

  • Mean gradient > 5 mmHg = moderate; > 10 mmHg = severe.
  • Valve area is usually derived using several techniques — simply tracing the area on the echo software can be unreliable.
  • Normal area 4–6 cm²; symptoms may only develop once < 2.0 cm²; < 1 cm² = severe.
Medical treatment
Atrial fibrillation
  • Up to 40% of patients with severe MS develop AF.
  • Increased ventricular rate ↓ diastolic time and ↑ atrial pressure → worse symptoms.
  • Rate control reduces symptoms and is generally easier than rhythm control in a heart with dilated atria and fibrosis.
  • Achieved with β-blockers or calcium channel blockers.
Dyspnoea
  • Diuretics reduce dyspnoea.
  • Long-acting nitrates can also provide some relief.
Anticoagulation
  • Little evidence to justify anticoagulation in MS alone.
  • Anticoagulate with warfarin (target INR 2–3) or heparin if: AF, prior embolic events, or demonstrated left atrial thrombus.
Surgical treatment
  • Surgical options (open or percutaneous) are the treatment of choice in severe symptomatic MS.
  • Percutaneous mitral commissurotomy (PMC) is preferred when leaflets are mobile and free of calcium — a balloon is passed across the valve and inflated, splitting the fused commissural edges.
  • PMC achieves MV area > 1.5 cm² with no worse than moderate MR in 80% of cases; emergency surgery rate < 1%.
  • Surgery is recommended with: atrial thrombus, heavy valve calcification, or when another open cardiac procedure is required.
  • Mitral valve replacement is now preferred due to improved valve and clinical outcomes — operative mortality 3–5%, but long-term outcomes highly variable and related to patient factors (bi-ventricular function, pulmonary pressures, AF).
💉 Anaesthetic management of MS in non-cardiac surgery
Preoperative assessment
  • MS is generally well tolerated — insidious signs need to be carefully sought.
  • Suggestive symptoms: dyspnoea, fatigue, frequent lower respiratory tract symptoms.
  • Murmur: low rumbling diastolic, best heard in expiration at the apex with the bell.
  • Ongoing elevated PA pressures strain the RV and eventually cause right heart failure — look for raised JVP and peripheral oedema.
  • As the LA dilates, AF is common. Patients on warfarin may need conversion to heparin — if warfarin is for AF alone, a short period without anticoagulation carries minimal risk.
Investigations
  • Exercise tolerance testing — objective measure that may unmask poor functional ability in patients who have reduced activity.
  • Echocardiography — severity, developing pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction. In general the LV continues to function well.
  • ECG — AF is common; p-mitrale may be seen if still in sinus rhythm.
In asymptomatic patients with systolic PA pressure < 50 mmHg, non-cardiac surgery is considered safe.

Postoperative management
  • Same fundamentals apply in the postoperative period.
  • Best achieved in a high-dependency unit unless surgery is extremely minor.
  • Instruct recovery staff to avoid fluid boluses.
  • Any hypotension mandates anaesthetic review and aggressive treatment.
Post-cardiac surgery (repair / replacement)
  • Preload well maintained; afterload reduced to promote forward flow.
  • The right heart may still need support — PVR reduction and inotrope infusion.
  • Surgical alteration of the mitral annulus can affect how the left heart contracts.
  • Combined with the effects of open heart surgery and cross-clamping with cardioplegia → the left heart is also at risk of failure in this period.
🤰 Management of MS in pregnancy
  • Due to the increase in blood volume and heart rate, MS is poorly tolerated and frequently first presents in pregnancy.
  • Normal pregnancy ↑ cardiac output by ~50%; the fixed CO of MS worsens pressures through the pulmonary circulation and into the right heart.
  • Risk of decompensation depends on severity. Anything worse than moderate (valve area < 1.5 cm²) frequently results in heart failure — usually in the 2nd or 3rd trimester and progressive.
  • Medical: aggressive control of tachycardia and AF — β-blockers are the mainstay; add diuretics if pulmonary congestion develops.
  • If symptoms are not controlled medically, percutaneous commissurotomy is the surgical treatment of choice (avoids bypass and its risk to the fetus).
Delivery
  • Haemodynamic goals: afterload maintenance, heart rate & rhythm control, care with fluids.
  • Vaginal delivery is well described but must be carefully managed.
  • Neuraxial analgesia placed early to block tachycardia from sympathetic stimulation.
  • Block achieved slowly; hypotension managed with α-agonists.
  • Epidurals are titratable; a mixture utilising opioids achieves analgesia with fewer haemodynamic effects.
  • Assisted second stage — limits labour duration and Valsalva.
  • Caesarean section considered in patients with pulmonary hypertension or NYHA III/IV symptoms despite maximal medical therapy.
Post-delivery — "flash" pulmonary oedema
Post-delivery, patients with MS are at risk of pulmonary oedema. This can occur precipitously from decompression of the inferior vena cava plus autotransfusion due to uterine compression — a sudden increase in preload.
  • Initial management: head-up positioning and 100% oxygen.
  • May require intubation and ventilation if severe, with sufficient PEEP.
Oxytocin — administer with great care (systemic vasodilation + propensity to increase PVR).
Ergometrine is contraindicated (pulmonary vasoconstricting effects).
🟢 Mitral Regurgitation (MR)

MR can be acute or chronic in onset, and primary or secondary in nature.

  • Primary MR — pathology of the valve preventing normal closure.
  • Secondary MR — LV dysfunction affecting the closing of the mitral valve (also known as "functional" MR).

In either case the left atrium dilates as blood is ejected back into it — this can be significant and AF is common. Overload of the pulmonary circulation causes dyspnoea, although frank pulmonary oedema is usually only seen in acute MR and in severe chronic disease. The LV is volume overloaded, with dilation of the ventricle further worsening MR through annular dilatation.

Acute MR
  • Caused by any disruption to the normal mechanism of the valve.
  • Examples: vegetations on the leaflets in infective endocarditis; chordae rupture in pre-existing degenerative disease; papillary muscle rupture due to an ST-elevation MI (usually inferior territory).
  • The LA is unable to compensate acutely for the increased pressure of refluxing blood.
  • Pressure is transmitted back into the pulmonary circulation → patients present with sudden-onset dyspnoea and need rapid stabilisation and treatment.
Chronic MR
Chronic primary MR
  • Abnormality of the leaflets prevents normal closure.
  • Major cause: degenerative disease resulting in leaflet prolapse.
Chronic secondary (functional) MR
  • Valve anatomy is normal but function is impaired due to LV pathology.
  • Causes: dilation of the mitral annulus with LV dilatation (leaflets cannot meet), or dyskinesia of the LV after ischaemia/infarction.
  • Systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior mitral leaflet into the LVOT can occur in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, especially when hypovolaemic → outflow obstruction plus simultaneous regurgitation across the distorted valve.

  • Grading severity is complex and involves several techniques.
  • Transoesophageal echocardiography is the gold standard; transthoracic echo is sufficient if good images are acquired.
  • Determination uses effective regurgitant orifice area, regurgitant volume and regurgitant fraction, correlated with the narrowest part of the regurgitant jet (vena contracta).
Medical treatment
Acute MR
  • Reduce filling pressures — nitrates or diuretics.
  • Reduce afterload — vasodilators or an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) as a bridge to definitive treatment.
  • Vasodilators promote forward flow into the aorta and reduce the regurgitant fraction, but use can be limited by hypotension → add inotropes or IABP.
IABP physiology: deflates during systole → reduces afterload and therefore the work of the heart. Inflates during diastole → increases pressure in the aortic root → increases coronary perfusion pressure.
Chronic MR
  • With signs of heart failure: standard heart failure management — β-blockers, ACE inhibitors, aldosterone antagonists, plus diuretics where heart failure is present.
  • If cardioversion is attainable, it will often immediately reduce MR severity.
  • No proven benefit of any medical therapy for MR without LV dysfunction — and when such dysfunction develops, it is generally an indication for surgery.
Surgical treatment

The decision on when to operate is complex — it depends on whether the MR is primary or secondary, the patient's condition, response to therapy, and development of sequelae such as heart failure or AF.

Primary MR — evidence to operate is clear
  • Severe and acute (e.g. ruptured papillary muscle) → surgery indicated.
  • Chronic and symptomatic, with no contraindications → repair clearly indicated.
  • Asymptomatic → operate only if clear consequences of the MR are seen: LVEF < 60%, or development of AF or pulmonary hypertension.
  • Surgery is generally avoided if heart failure is severe (LVEF < 30%).
Secondary MR — less evidence-based
  • In patients already undergoing CABG, repair of severe MR is indicated; whether to repair moderate MR is less obvious.
  • Symptomatic with severe LV failure → benefits of surgery are questionable unless there is some reversibility of the underlying problem (e.g. revascularisation of viable myocardium).
  • Surgery is also considered in symptomatic patients with moderate LV failure if medical therapy has failed.
Repair vs. replacement
  • For primary MR, valve repair is invariably preferred if at all possible — significantly reduced operative mortality, better preservation of LV function, and avoids the long-term consequences of prosthetic valves.
  • For secondary/ischaemic MR, repair results in reduced early mortality but a higher degree of recurrent MR long term; otherwise long-term outcomes of repair vs replacement are similar.
Minimally invasive & percutaneous surgery
  • Minimally invasive: repair/replacement via a small right thoracotomy and several instrument ports, with a femoral incision for bypass cannulation; standard techniques under endoscopic view. Safety and efficacy similar to midline sternotomy in experienced hands.
  • Robotic telemanipulators (since 1998): dexterity helps place sutures precisely in difficult positions. Bypass and cross-clamp times are longer, but time to discharge and return to work are shorter. Long-term outcome data awaited.
Mitraclip evidence: trials show efficacy in moderate-severe or severe MR in patients deemed too high risk for surgery. It is less effective at reducing MR in the short term but has similar outcomes at 12 months, with improved LVEF, NYHA score and quality of life vs baseline. In the randomised EVEREST trial, 20% required further surgical intervention. Its role is expanding in high-risk surgical patients.
💉 Anaesthetic management of MR in non-cardiac surgery
Preoperative assessment
  • Acute MR can present as florid cardiac failure with severe pulmonary oedema.
  • Chronic MR can be tolerated for many years, with symptoms only developing as the LV starts to fail.
  • Seek signs of pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure — dyspnoea, fatigue, frequent chest infections (pulmonary hypertension); peripheral oedema and raised JVP (right heart failure).
  • The pansystolic murmur of MR is frequently very soft, and its intensity is unrelated to severity.
  • There may be a displaced and forceful apex beat.
Investigations
MR — key investigations
TestWhat to look for
Echocardiography Grades lesion severity. LV function is harder to assess — because blood is ejected through both the aortic and regurgitant mitral valves, EF appears higher than normal. LVEF ≈ 70% is "normal" in MR (cf. 55% normally); < 60% represents LV dysfunction.
ECG Seek signs of ischaemia, especially in functional MR. Acute/new-onset MR may be due to recent papillary muscle rupture — inferior MI is the likeliest cause, so ST changes in leads II, III and aVF may suggest the diagnosis (the history may be vague).
Blood tests BNP is useful in prognostication — a low plasma BNP has a high negative predictive value for developing further complications.
Exercise testing Symptoms of chronic MR are often masked by the patient simply reducing exertion. Formal testing (e.g. cardiopulmonary exercise testing) is of clear utility if echo findings do not match the symptom history, and may give a clearer picture of ventricular function.
If LV function is preserved and the patient is asymptomatic, non-cardiac surgery can be performed with no additional risk — even if the MR is severe. Risk increases only when LV function is severely impaired (< 30%) or the patient is symptomatic — then medical optimisation must be assured before proceeding.

Postoperative management
  • Recovery staff should be made aware of the cardiac diagnosis.
  • The same perioperative principles should be adhered to.
Post-cardiac surgery (after valve replacement)
  • There is no low-pressure system for the LV to eject into — it is suddenly subjected to a higher workload.
  • Poor function may be unmasked — consider inotropes and inodilators.
  • Minimise SVR if possible to allow forward flow and "offload" the ventricle.
  • If an IABP was needed before operation, it is generally left in place until haemodynamic and metabolic stability are achieved.
🤰 Management of MR in pregnancy
  • Pregnancy mimics the haemodynamic goals for regurgitant lesions: ↑ heart rate, ↑ blood volume, ↓ SVR — all promoting forward flow.
  • Because of this, pregnancy is generally well tolerated, even if MR is severe, as long as LV function is preserved.
  • Patients with impaired LV function or severe symptomatic MR are at increased risk of heart failure.
  • Diuresis is commonly used to limit fluid overload — usually all the medical therapy needed.
Delivery
  • Vaginal delivery is generally possible with epidural anaesthesia.
  • Assisted second stage to avoid spikes in systemic vascular resistance seen with pushing.
📋 Summary

Anaesthetists are an integral part of any team undertaking surgical management of a patient with mitral valve disease. An understanding of the pathophysiology allows the anaesthetist not only to engage in decisions regarding treatment, but to anticipate and manage the consequences of surgery and anaesthesia.
