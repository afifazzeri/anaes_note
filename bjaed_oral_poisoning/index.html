Oral Poisoning — Quick Reference

For anaesthetists & intensivists · clinical notes only · collapsible & searchable

Key points
  • The mainstay of treatment of poisoning is resuscitation and supportive care.
  • Consider poisoning in any patient with bizarre behaviour, a reduced conscious level, or unexplained physiological instability.
  • Ipecac is no longer recommended in the treatment of acute poisoning.
  • Activated charcoal should be given within 60 min of ingestion of poison, but may have an effect for up to 2 h or longer post-ingestion.
  • Acetaminophen (paracetamol) levels are mandatory in all cases of adult overdose.
Initial approach to the poisoned patient
  • All cases are managed as acute medical emergencies using an ABC approach, regardless of the agent used.
  • Rare exceptions:
    • Organophosphates — health-care workers must first protect themselves from the agent.
    • Cyanide — the antidote for cyanide is required immediately.
  • Every case requires: resuscitation · risk assessment · substance identification · specific treatment (if available) · a period of observation.
  • A focused, detailed poisoning history and examination identify specific physical signs; then use antidotes and laboratory tests selectively.
  • Most poisoned patients require supportive treatment only.

Risk assessment

  • Obtain specific information from ambulance personnel or witnesses about the nature, timing and amount of drug or poison.
  • One-third of cases involve more than one toxin; alcohol is a common contributing factor.
  • Check the patient's clothing for notes or blister packets — a clue to the quantity and type of drug ingested.
  • In deliberate self-harm, there may be previous hospital admissions to aid diagnosis.
  • Contact the general practitioner for previous history — family or witness sources are often unreliable.
Supportive care and monitoring
  • Monitor suspected exposure in an appropriate clinical environment.
  • Where coma is inevitable, intubate pre-emptively or at the first sign of deterioration in level of consciousness.
  • Tracheal intubation protects the airway and allows early administration of activated charcoal via a nasogastric tube if required.
  • Patients at risk of cardiac instability and acidosis: continuous ECG and invasive arterial pressure monitoring with regular blood gas analysis.
  • Check body temperature and serum glucose; give i.v. dextrose if required.

Investigations
  • Acetaminophen level is mandatory in all cases of adult overdose — take at 4 h post-exposure.
  • Salicylate levels are not recommended in the asymptomatic patient.
  • Screening for substances of abuse: rapid commercial urine kits.
  • Other laboratory tests: full blood count, urea and electrolytes, lactate, liver function tests, coagulation studies.
  • Specific agents (e.g. methanol, carbamazepine): plasma levels 4-hourly to refine risk assessment and gauge response to enhanced elimination.
  • Chest radiograph — may show pulmonary oedema, suggestive of poisoning with narcotics or salicylates.
  • Abdominal radiograph — can identify packets of drugs in "body packers".
  • Radiological investigations are otherwise rarely required in acute poisoning.
Gastric decontamination
Much debate surrounds the "decontamination triangle" of forced emesis, gastric lavage and single-dose activated charcoal. Decontamination strategies are not without side-effects — consider the risk:benefit ratio before administration.
AACT / EAPCCT: gastrointestinal decontamination should not be administered routinely.

Induced emesis (ipecac)

  • Ipecac induces vomiting by direct gastrointestinal effects and central nervous system actions.
  • Dose: 30 ml in adults followed by 240 ml water. Emesis typically occurs within 20 min and persists 30–120 min.
  • Compared with single-dose activated charcoal, ipecac conveys no benefit, whether given alone or combined with charcoal.
  • Side-effects: prolonged time in the Emergency Department and increased incidence of aspiration pneumonitis.
  • Its use is no longer recommended.

Gastric lavage

  • Large-bore orogastric tube (30–40 F); instill and re-aspirate several litres of water until no more pill fragments are identified.
  • Patients must be able to protect their own airway.
  • Drug absorption is not reduced if lavage is commenced 1 h or more after ingestion.
  • Paradoxically, one study suggested lavage promotes post-pyloric transfer of poison into the small intestine, where it is more rapidly absorbed.
  • No data demonstrate improved clinical outcome; use is associated with significant morbidity and mortality — gastrointestinal perforation and aspiration.

Activated charcoal (AC)

  • Most drugs and chemicals are absorbed by AC; weak van der Waals forces bind the substance in the GI tract. Numerous particles provide a large surface area that prevents further absorption.
  • Give orally, or nasogastrically via a 16 F tube in the intubated patient.
  • Charcoal : toxin ratio 10 : 1; usual dose 25–50 g, or 1 g kg−1 in a child.
  • Give within 1 h of poison ingestion. Efficacy is time-dependent: nearly 90% reduction in absorption at 30 min, falling to 30% at 1 h. Reduces acetaminophen absorption up to 2 h after ingestion.
  • Unpalatable and can cause vomiting — in children it can be mixed with ice cream. Warn patients that stools will be black.
  • Not recommended in poisoning with: lithium, iron, alcohol, methanol, ethylene glycol, petroleum distillates, corrosives, acids, or alkalis.
  • Repeated doses of 25 g every 4–6 h can benefit poisoning with slow-release formulations: salicylate, barbiturates, theophylline, quinine, digoxin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, dapsone. Multiple doses interrupt the enterohepatic circulation, reducing plasma levels and duration of toxicity.
  • Major but rare adverse effect of repeat dosing: acute bowel obstruction from charcoal concretions, particularly likely in the presence of an anticholinergic ileus.

Whole bowel irrigation (WBI)

  • Aims to reduce the time available for ingested substances to be absorbed.
  • Polyethylene glycol (PEG) 1.5–2 litres per hour, best administered through a 12 F feeding tube. Elevate the head of the bed to 45° to prevent aspiration.
  • If emesis occurs, discontinue for 30 min and restart at half the normal rate. Metoclopramide can help as an anti-emetic due to its prokinetic effects.
  • Administer until the effluent is clear.
  • Not used routinely. Consider where poisoning includes sustained-release or enteric-coated tablets, or drugs for which charcoal is ineffective: alcohols, boric acid, cyanide, iron, lithium, hydrocarbons, acids, alkalis.
  • Used with some success in body packers, heavy metal and battery ingestion.
  • Contraindicated in: unprotected airway, haemodynamic instability, bowel obstruction, bowel perforation, or ileus.
Increased elimination

Alkaline diuresis

  • Enhances elimination of weak acids such as salicylates and some herbicides.
  • Sodium bicarbonate is administered; measure urine pH and keep it 7.5–8.5.
  • Weak acids become charged in alkaline urine, producing a concentration gradient that draws more toxin into the renal tubular system.
  • Hypokalaemia can result — correct aggressively.
  • Use with caution in patients with renal impairment or cardiac disease.

Haemodialysis

  • Helpful in ethylene glycol, methanol, lithium, theophylline and salicylate poisoning.
  • Usefulness depends on the pharmacological properties of the ingested drug. The drug or poison should have:
    • low volume of distribution (< 1 litre kg−1)
    • low molecular weight (< 500 Da)
    • low protein binding
    • low water solubility
Acetaminophen (paracetamol)
  • Responsible for 30,000 UK hospital admissions a year and 345 deaths per annum. Often one component of a mixed overdose.
  • Produces the toxic metabolite NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine), which is neutralized by glutathione. Depletion of glutathione results in NAPQI toxicity to hepatocytes.
  • Patients particularly at risk:
    • Chronic alcoholics
    • Those taking antiepileptic drugs — carbamazepine, phenytoin (enzyme inducers)
    • Patients receiving anti-tuberculosis treatment (produce more NAPQI)
    • Reduced glutathione stores: HIV, malnutrition, terminal malignancy

Management

  • A recent Cochrane review concluded that activated charcoal is more effective than gastric lavage or ipecac in reducing acetaminophen absorption.
  • N-acetylcysteine (NAC) should be given, but the optimum dosing regimen remains uncertain.
  • NAC may reduce mortality in patients with fulminant liver failure.
  • The superiority of NAC over methionine is unproven; 5% of patients receiving NAC develop anaphylactoid reactions.
  • Current Toxbase advice: high-risk patients should receive NAC at lower plasma acetaminophen concentrations.
  • Patients who develop fulminant liver failure may be offered life-saving liver transplantation; selection criteria need refinement based on long-term outcome data.

Salicylates
  • Presentation: tinnitus, deafness, hyperventilation, epigastric pain, vomiting, hyperthermia, sweating, dehydration, respiratory alkalosis, metabolic acidosis, and electrolyte disturbances.
  • Agitation and confusion may indicate developing cerebral oedema, which can be fatal.
  • Treatment: rehydration, treatment of acid–base disturbance, and close monitoring of plasma levels.
  • Activated charcoal should be administered as soon as possible, even in delayed presentations.
  • The use of multi-dose activated charcoal is debatable, but should be considered if the plasma salicylate level continues to increase, or if a slow-release preparation has been taken.
  • Alkalinization of the urine should be considered in patients with signs of toxicity or with plasma levels > 300 mg litre−1.
  • In patients with levels > 700 mg litre−1, haemodialysis should be considered.
Benzodiazepines
  • Deaths associated with benzodiazepine overdose are due to mixed overdose, especially alcohol and other drugs.
  • Clinical manifestations: drowsiness, respiratory depression, dysarthria and ataxia.
  • Coma is not common but is most often seen in the elderly or in patients who have ingested alcohol or other drugs.
  • Treatment is supportive.
  • Flumazenil is controversial — it has many side-effects and is rarely indicated. Adverse effects: ventricular tachycardia, raising intracranial pressure, withdrawal in chronic abusers, and seizures if used in the presence of tricyclic antidepressants.
  • It can be used to reverse benzodiazepine coma so as to avoid intubation, but this should be limited to situations of benzodiazepine-only overdose where no other drugs have been taken.
Opiates / opioids
  • Increasing doses progressively produce euphoria → pinpoint pupils → sedation → respiratory depression → apnoea.
  • Complications: hypotension, convulsions, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, and compartment syndrome from prolonged immobility.
  • Where rhabdomyolysis is suspected, measure serum CK and urinary myoglobin.
  • Naloxone: 100 µg boluses i.v. to a maximum of 2 mg; aim to reverse the opiate effect and reverse respiratory depression.
  • The antidote can precipitate an acute agitated withdrawal state — staff should be mindful of their own safety.
  • Routes: i.v., i.m., s.c., or tracheal.
  • Short half-life (20 min if given i.v.) — an infusion may be needed, since respiratory depression may reoccur.
  • Rarely causes ventricular dysrhythmias, hypertension, and drowsiness at very high doses.
Tricyclic antidepressants (TCA)
  • Cause 250 fatalities each year in the UK.
  • Predominant cause of death: cardiac depression by sodium channel blockade with resultant decrease in cardiac output.
  • Presentation: sinus tachycardia, mydriasis, coma, hyperreflexia, convulsions, ECG changes, and hypotension.
QRS interval > 120 ms indicates cardiac toxicity and is predictive of ventricular arrhythmias and seizures.
  • Treatment: prevent absorption using activated charcoal.
  • Sodium bicarbonate — loading dose 8.4% 1–2 ml kg−1, followed by an infusion or intermittent bolus.
  • Sodium bicarbonate should be given even in the absence of a significant metabolic acidosis — it helps stabilize the Na channels in the myocardium and prevent cardiotoxicity.
  • Convulsions: benzodiazepines initially; anaesthesia with propofol may be required.
  • Phenytoin is the anti-arrhythmic of choice; glucagon can help if there is evidence of myocardial depression.
Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI) & serotonin syndrome
  • Drugs include citalopram, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, sertraline.
  • Most common features of overdose: nausea, vomiting, agitation, tremor, nystagmus, drowsiness, dysrhythmias, and mild hypertension.
  • Convulsions have been reported up to 10 h post-ingestion.
  • Co-administration with drugs that release serotonin or affect its reuptake — cocaine, tricyclics, MAOIs, MDMA — may result in serotonin syndrome.

Serotonin syndrome

  • Triad: altered mental status, neuromuscular hyperactivity, and autonomic instability.
  • Similar in presentation to neuroleptic malignant syndrome — hyperpyrexia, acidosis, arrhythmias and rhabdomyolysis are seen.
  • Treatment is supportive but should include activated charcoal up to 1 h post-ingestion.
  • Convulsions: benzodiazepines and phenytoin.
  • Cyproheptadine and chlorpromazine are 5-HT2A antagonists and have been used successfully, but there are no controlled trials supporting either agent.
  • Suspected rhabdomyolysis: urinary alkalinization and volume replacement may reduce renal failure. Fluid resuscitation including 225 mmol of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate over 2 h is given to increase urine pH to > 7.
  • If renal failure occurs, haemodialysis or haemofiltration is required.
Methanol and ethylene glycol
  • Poisoning results in a severe high anion gap metabolic acidosis.
  • Both are metabolized via alcohol dehydrogenase, forming acids — formic (methanol), glycolic and oxalic (ethylene glycol) — which accumulate and are responsible for neurological damage and death.
  • Ethanol (oral or nasogastric) can be used because of its greater affinity for alcohol dehydrogenase. However, maintaining plasma alcohol levels in the correct range is difficult and time-consuming, particularly if the patient is undergoing dialysis.
  • Fomepizole blocks the metabolism of methanol and ethylene glycol and can be injected 12-hourly. It is expensive and not widely available.
  • Folate deficiency in primates predicts a poor outcome in methanol toxicity — give folate 1 mg kg−1 day−1 for 48 h.

Summary / take-home
  • Acute poisoning is relatively common and causes significant morbidity and mortality.
  • NPIS and Toxbase provide a 24 h information service for all aspects of poisoning.
  • Treatment of poisoning remains largely supportive.
  • Few drugs have antidotes — treatment is aimed at reducing further absorption, increasing elimination, and treating the side-effects.
  • Gastric decontamination with activated charcoal is time-dependent but can significantly reduce drug absorption.
  • Forced emesis and gastric lavage are no longer recommended.
