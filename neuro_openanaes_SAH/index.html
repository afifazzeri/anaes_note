▸Clinical Presentation & Focal Localising SignsDiagnosis

Classic: sudden-onset severe headache ("thunderclap", "worst headache of my life"). Half of patients lose consciousness. Also nausea/vomiting, nuchal rigidity, photophobia.

Examination: hypertension and signs of meningismus are common.

Neurological findingLikely source
Third nerve (CN III) palsyPosterior communicating artery aneurysm
Sixth nerve (CN VI) palsyElevated ICP
Hemiparesis and aphasiaMiddle cerebral artery aneurysm
Retinal / pre-retinal haemorrhagesElevated ICP
Bilateral leg weakness and abuliaAnterior communicating artery aneurysm
Brainstem signsBasilar artery aneurysm
Do not miss Initial haemorrhage is the primary cause of death, followed by rebleeding. About 10–15% die before hospitalisation and 25% die within the first 48 hours.
▸Diagnostic PathwayDiagnosis
  • Non-contrast CT is the cornerstone. Sensitivity is highest in the first 6 hours and falls to 58% by day 5.
  • Lumbar puncture if CT is normal. Classic findings: elevated opening pressure, elevated red blood cell count, and xanthochromia from haemoglobin breakdown products.
  • Once SAH is confirmed, determine the aetiology with angiography — digital subtraction angiography (DSA) or CT angiography.
Reminder aSAH is a neurological emergency. Elevated blood pressure on presentation is associated with greater haematoma expansion, neurological deterioration, death and dependency.

▸Aneurysm Locations & Relative IncidenceAnatomy

Risk factors for rupture Aneurysm size and location (larger, posterior circulation), smoking (most important preventable), hypertension, genetic/family history, prior aSAH, moderate-to-heavy alcohol use, sympathomimetics (methamphetamine, cocaine), oestrogen deficiency, antithrombotic therapy.
▸Cardiac Complications — Neurogenic Stunned MyocardiumComplication
  • SAH can cause large, reversible myocardial "stunning" injury; mechanism is thought to be catecholamine-mediated.
  • Troponin commonly rises, typically below the diagnostic threshold for myocardial infarction. Peak troponin correlates with severity of neurological injury and myocardial dysfunction.
  • Echocardiography resembles Takotsubo cardiomyopathy: apical and midventricular akinesis with preserved base; ejection fraction commonly 10–30%. Variant forms with midventricular or basal akinesis also occur.
  • Dysfunction is reversible with a good recovery profile.
  • ECG: classic deep "canyon" T waves, nonspecific T-wave changes, QT prolongation, ST-segment depression, U waves.
  • QTc > 550 ms occurs especially in severe SAH and is associated with malignant ventricular rhythms including torsades de pointes.
Management Supportive. Enhanced invasive cardiovascular monitoring (echocardiography, pulmonary artery catheter) and cardiovascular medications — inotropes, vasopressors, antiarrhythmics. If ventricular function is adequate, non-ischaemic ECG patterns need no specific intervention beyond attention to the possibility of dysrhythmias.
Anaesthetic caution Beta-blockers (labetalol, esmolol) should be used with caution in stress-induced cardiomyopathy.
▸Early Management — Oxygenation, Ventilation & EVDEarly

Initial management focuses on stabilising life-threatening conditions, optimising cerebral perfusion, and minimising further neurological injury.

Oxygenation & ventilation

  • Ensure adequate oxygenation, ventilation and a patent airway.
  • Hypoxia worsens brain injury — avoid. It induces cerebral vasoconstriction and ischaemia.
  • Both hypo- and hypercarbia worsen neurological outcome. Hypercarbia causes cerebral vasodilation → intracranial hypertension and decreased cerebral perfusion.

Indications for tracheal intubation

  • Comatose patient with GCS ≤ 8
  • Hypoxia or hypoventilation
  • Haemodynamic instability
  • Deep sedation or general anaesthesia needed for imaging

Restoration of cerebral perfusion

  • Early external ventricular drain (EVD) lowers raised ICP and restores cerebral perfusion; it also facilitates surgical exposure during clipping.
  • Common thresholds for EVD placement: ventriculomegaly with GCS ≤ 12, and Hunt & Hess grade ≥ 2.
▸Blood Pressure Control & NimodipineEarly

Blood pressure control

  • Strict control is critical: maintain SBP < 160 mmHg or MAP < 110 mmHg.
  • Common pharmacological choices: intravenous nicardipine, esmolol, labetalol, clevidipine.
  • Avoid nitroprusside — risk of increased cerebral blood volume and ICP.
  • Avoid hypotension as well.

Nimodipine

  • 60 mg orally or via nasogastric tube every 4 hours — improves outcomes in aSAH; standard of care.
  • Start within 48 hours of aSAH and continue for 21 days.
  • Proposed neuroprotective mechanisms: dilation of small arteries not visible on angiograms; reduction of calcium-dependent excitotoxicity; decreased platelet aggregation.
▸Anticoagulation Reversal, Seizure Prophylaxis & Supportive CareEarly

Reversal of anticoagulation

  • Discontinue all antithrombotic agents and reverse anticoagulation as needed.

Seizure prophylaxis

  • Prophylactic antiseizure medications after aSAH are controversial — they have been associated with worse neurological and cognitive outcomes.

General supportive measures

  • Adequate analgesia for headache and neck pain
  • Maintenance of euvolemia with crystalloids and monitoring of urine output
  • DVT prophylaxis with intermittent pneumatic compression devices

▸Anaesthetic Goals for Surgical ClippingAnaesthesia
  • Preventing rebleeding
  • Maintaining cerebral perfusion
  • Facilitating surgical exposure by preventing / managing brain swelling
  • Facilitating intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM)
  • Facilitating temporary aneurysm clipping
  • Anticipating and managing aneurysm rupture
  • Ensuring smooth emergence and neurological assessment
▸Induction of AnaesthesiaAnaesthesia
  • Primary goal: avoid hypertension in response to laryngoscopy and intubation — it increases the risk of rebleeding.
  • Strategies: ensure adequate anaesthetic depth before laryngoscopy; use short-acting opioids or antihypertensive agents alongside induction drugs.
  • Hypotension following induction increases the risk of cerebral ischaemia and should be aggressively treated with vasopressors.
  • A pre-induction arterial line allows continuous blood pressure monitoring — consider in most patients, especially those with cardiac dysfunction, elevated troponin levels, and haemodynamic instability.
  • Avoid hypo- and hypercarbia during bag-mask ventilation.
  • Succinylcholine may be used for rapid sequence induction, if needed.

▸Anaesthesia Maintenance & Haemodynamic TargetsAnaesthesia
  • Choice of agents depends on neurological status, comorbidities, treatment approach (clipping vs coiling) and the need for IONM. A balanced technique with inhaled and intravenous agents is commonly used.
  • Propofol maintains coupling between cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO₂) and cerebral blood flow (CBF).
  • Inhaled anaesthetics have a dose-dependent effect on CBF: at < 1 MAC they decrease CBF; at higher doses they cause cerebral vasodilation with uncoupling of CMRO₂ and CBF. The vasodilatory effect can be minimised by hyperventilation.

Haemodynamic goals

SBP < 160 mmHgBefore aneurysm secured
MAP < 110 mmHgBefore aneurysm secured
CPP > 60–70 mmHgMaintain throughout
Avoid hypertension before the aneurysm is secured Critical periods: placement of skull pins, patient positioning, and surgical stimulation.
During temporary clip placement Raise blood pressure by 10–20% to ensure perfusion through collateral vessels. Blood pressure can be normalised after the aneurysm is secured.
▸ICP Management & Brain RelaxationICP

Strategies to facilitate surgical exposure:

  • Maintaining an adequate depth of anaesthesia and analgesia
  • Optimising haemodynamic parameters
  • Optimising patient positioning to facilitate cerebral venous drainage
  • Maintaining normocarbia to moderate hypocarbia (PaCO₂ 30–35 mmHg). Brief hyperventilation to PaCO₂ < 30 mmHg may be used if other ICP-reduction manoeuvres fail.
  • Hyperosmolar therapy — intravenous mannitol, furosemide, or hypertonic saline
  • CSF drainage via an external ventricular drain
▸Temporary ClippingIntraop

A temporary clip may be placed on the feeding vessel to facilitate dissection and accurate placement of a permanent clip around the neck of the aneurysm.

Tolerance Clip application for a duration of up to 10 minutes is usually well tolerated without ischaemia of the middle cerebral artery territory.

Strategies to prevent ischaemic damage

  • Inducing temporary hypertension to promote collateral flow (10–20% increase in SBP)
  • Avoiding prolonged temporary clipping (usually > 10 minutes)
  • Reducing CMRO₂ using burst suppression
  • Using IONM to detect ischaemia and alert to a signal change
Not recommended Intraoperative hypothermia for neuroprotection is not recommended. Similarly, hyperthermia should be avoided.
Glycaemic control Intraoperative hyperglycaemia worsens neurological outcomes and should be avoided. No specific threshold is advised, but periodic glucose monitoring and maintaining blood glucose between 80 and 180 mg/dL is recommended.
▸Adenosine-Induced Temporary Flow ArrestIntraop
  • Useful in controlling bleeding if the aneurysm ruptures intraoperatively before clipping — the induced flow arrest provides a clear surgical field and allows the surgeon to control the source of bleeding.
  • A negative dromotropic and chronotropic agent with rapid onset and short duration; causes hypotension and can cause brief asystole of variable duration.
  • Dose 0.3–0.5 mg/kg → approximately 1 minute of moderate hypotension.
  • Recovery may be preceded by transient arrhythmias including atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, or atrial flutter.
  • May cause bronchospasm in patients with reactive airways disease.
Avoid in Coronary artery disease or abnormalities of the cardiac conduction system.
Check the result Intraoperative ICG video or conventional angiography are useful to check the status of the aneurysm and the patency of nearby arteries after clipping.
▸Emergence & Postoperative VentilationAnaesthesia
  • Ensure a smooth emergence without coughing, straining, and hypertension.
  • Coughing and straining increase intrathoracic pressure and cause transient increases in both cerebral arterial and venous pressure — can cause oedema, bleeding, and a rise in ICP.
  • Short-acting opioids can be titrated to spontaneous ventilation towards the end of the procedure. Remifentanil or dexmedetomidine infusions may be continued throughout emergence to reduce airway reactivity.
  • Avoid nitrous oxide — increased risks of pneumocephalus and postoperative nausea/vomiting.
  • Lidocaine 1–1.5 mg/kg IV is effective for reducing airway reflexes.
  • Hypertension increases the risk of intracerebral haemorrhage after craniotomy and should be aggressively treated.

Agents for emergence hypertension

ClassAgents

Deferring extubation — discuss with the neurosurgical team

Indications for postoperative ventilation include (but are not limited to):

  • Preoperative intubation
  • Low preoperative GCS
  • Extremely long surgery (> 8–10 h)
  • Posterior circulation / posterior cranial fossa, or surgery close to the brainstem
  • High risk for the development of cerebral oedema

The anaesthetic is still weaned off to allow a clinical neurological evaluation.

Key safety net In patients where planned extubation cannot be performed within 1 hour post-surgery, an immediate head CT should be performed.
▸Anaesthetic Considerations for Endovascular CoilingAnaesthesia

Goals are similar to surgical clipping with the following exceptions:

  • IONM is not commonly used
  • Interventions to reduce brain swelling are not needed
  • Patient immobility, especially during coil deployment, is critical
  • Heparin is commonly administered for anticoagulation and may need to be reversed

Although monitored anaesthesia care may be used for diagnostic angiography, general anaesthesia (GA) is usually preferred for endovascular coiling.

Advantages of GADisadvantages of GA
  • Immobility during the procedure
  • Patient comfort during a stimulating procedure
  • Easier to control comorbidities or neurological condition
  • Airway protection and control of ventilation (PaCO₂ manipulation, apnoea)
  • Haemodynamic instability during intubation, extubation, or both
  • Anaesthetic-induced hypotension causing cerebral hypoperfusion
  • Inability to monitor the patient's neurological status
  • Complications from coughing and straining during extubation
  • Delayed emergence or impaired neuro exam due to residual anaesthetic drugs
With GA, the same requirements for induction/intubation and emergence/extubation apply as during open craniotomy for aneurysmal clipping.
▸Early Complication — RebleedingComplication
  • Patients with aSAH are at increased risk of early rebleeding — 4–14% in the first 24 hours.
  • Characterised by acute deterioration of neurological status accompanied by the appearance of new haemorrhage on head CT.
  • To reduce risk, the ruptured aneurysm should be treated via surgical clipping or endovascular coiling as soon as possible, ideally within the first 24 hours after symptom onset.
▸Early Complication — Vasospasm & Delayed Cerebral IschaemiaComplication
  • Cerebral vasospasm is a devastating complication of aSAH.
  • Typically occurs between 3 and 14 days post-haemorrhage and results in delayed cerebral ischaemia.
  • Monitoring and treatment of vasospasm to prevent delayed cerebral ischaemia is a mainstay of critical care management — along with maintenance of adequate CPP and prevention of brain herniation.
  • Nimodipine (see above) is standard of care for outcome improvement.
▸Early Complication — Hydrocephalus & Elevated ICPComplication
  • Affects 20–30% of patients with aSAH, from obstruction of CSF flow and decreased CSF absorption.
  • CSF diversion via an external ventricular drain is often needed.
  • About 20% of patients need long-term CSF diversion (ventriculoperitoneal shunt) for chronic hydrocephalus.
▸Sodium Disorders — Hyponatraemia (CSW vs SIADH) and Diabetes InsipidusComplication

Hyponatraemia

  • Occurs in up to 30% of patients with aSAH.
  • More likely due to cerebral salt-wasting syndrome (CSW) from release of natriuretic peptide than SIADH.
Cerebral salt wasting (CSW)SIADH
Triad / features Hyponatraemia, volume contraction, high urine Na⁺ (> 50 mmol/L); correlated with symptomatic vasospasms Normovolaemia or mild hypervolaemia
Treatment Management: administration of isotonic and/or hypertonic fluids, using intravascular normovolaemia and normonatraemia as the end point. SIADH is treated by volume restriction.

Cause is often iatrogenic, related to hyperosmolar therapy (hypertonic saline or mannitol).

Central diabetes insipidus

  • Can be caused by hypothalamic/pituitary ischaemia from delayed cerebral ischaemia related to vasospasm, or reduced cerebral perfusion in the setting of intracranial hypertension.
  • Hypernatraemia in the subacute phase of SAH is associated with poor outcomes.
  • ADH is synthesised in the supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus and transported to the supraoptic-hypophyseal tract in the posterior pituitary.
  • Rarely arises intraoperatively — usually occurs 12–48 hours postoperatively.
  • Clinical picture: polyuria with rising serum sodium and serum osmolality.
  • Diagnosis: urine output > 250 mL for 2 hours, Na > 140, and low urine osmolality or urine specific gravity < 1.005.
  • Treatment: intravenous DDAVP (desamino D-arginine vasopressin) 1–2 mcg and volume repletion.
▸Cardiac Complications — Critical Care ManagementICU
  • Serious cardiac complications associated with SAH include stress-induced cardiomyopathy and associated arrhythmias.
  • Supportive measures:
    • Enhanced invasive cardiovascular monitoring (echocardiography, pulmonary artery catheter, etc.)
    • Cardiovascular medications — inotropes, vasopressors, and antiarrhythmic medications
Caution Beta-blockers (labetalol, esmolol) — advised caution in stress-induced cardiomyopathy.
