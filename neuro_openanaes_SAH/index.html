📌1. Overview, Epidemiology & Natural History
  • Aneurysm = localised arterial dilation from weakening of the vessel wall. Intracranial vessels are predisposed because of a thinner tunica media and adventitia.
  • Ruptured intracranial aneurysms cause ~85% of non-traumatic SAH; high mortality and morbidity.
  • General prevalence 3–5% (varies by region). Mean age at diagnosis ~50 yrs. Female preponderance 1.24 : 1.
  • ~85% of patients with unruptured aneurysms are asymptomatic — often found incidentally.
  • Large unruptured aneurysms may compress brain/cranial nerves → headache, visual disturbance, dilated pupil, imbalance.

Risk factors for rupture (PHASES components) Size • Location on posterior circulation or anterior cerebral arteries • Japanese or Finnish ethnicity • Hypertension • Age >70 yrs • Previous SAH from another aneurysm.

Modifiable & non-modifiable risk factors for SAH

  • Smoking — most important preventable risk factor.
  • Hypertension — major risk factor.
  • Aneurysm size and location (larger + posterior circulation = higher risk).
  • Genetic risk / family history; prior aSAH.
  • Moderate–heavy alcohol use; sympathomimetics (methamphetamine, cocaine).
  • Oestrogen deficiency; antithrombotic therapy.
🔬2. Anatomy & Pathophysiology
  • Most intracranial aneurysms occur at the circle of Willis, typically at bifurcation points where turbulent flow creates maximal mechanical stress.
  • Pathophysiology is multifactorial: genetic factors + haemodynamic stress, extracellular matrix degradation, and inflammatory responses.
  • Chronic high wall shear stress → endothelial dysfunction and media thinning.
  • Imbalance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their tissue inhibitors → breakdown of elastin and collagen → reduced vessel integrity.
  • Inflammatory cell–mediated remodelling contributes to formation and rupture risk.

🚨4. Clinical Presentation & Grading

Classic presentation of aSAH

  • Sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache — often "worst headache of my life"; reaches maximal intensity instantly.
  • Mechanism: ICP equating arterial pressure as blood occupies the subarachnoid space.
  • Accompanying features: loss of consciousness (~50%), seizure, nausea/vomiting, focal neurological deficits, meningism, photophobia, nuchal rigidity.
  • Sentinel/warning headache precedes the major bleed in 10–43% of cases — a missed sentinel bleed is a lost opportunity.

Diagnosis

  • Non-contrast head CT is the cornerstone. Sensitivity highest in the first 6 hours; falls to ~58% by day 5.
  • If CT is negative and suspicion remains → lumbar puncture: elevated opening pressure, elevated RBC count, xanthochromia.
  • Once SAH confirmed, determine aetiology with DSA (gold standard) or CT angiography.
  • CTA can miss small aneurysms (<3 mm sensitivity ~61%) — DSA is indicated when clinical concern is high and CTA is negative/inconclusive.
Early rebleeding 4–14% within the first 24 hours. Characterised by acute neurological deterioration with new haemorrhage on CT. To reduce risk, secure the aneurysm as soon as possible — ideally within 24 hours of symptom onset.
⚖️5. Treatment Modalities — Clipping vs Coiling

Treatment is tailored to aneurysm characteristics and the patient. Goal of all therapies: obliterate the aneurysm from the circulation and reduce risk of rupture/rebleeding.

FeatureEndovascular coilingSurgical clipping
ApproachCoils into aneurysm via intra-arterial catheterMetal clip across aneurysm neck via craniotomy
InvasivenessLess invasive; quicker recoveryMore invasive
RecurrenceHigher recurrence rateLower recurrence rate
MorbidityLower postoperative morbidityHigher postoperative morbidity
Best forPosterior circulation; elderly; high-grade basilar apex; narrow-neckComplex morphology; wide neck; MCA location; younger patients (durability); large intraparenchymal haematoma
LimitationsCoil migration / incomplete occlusion in wide-neck; adjuncts (stent, balloon, flow diverter) may be neededSurgical access; not for all locations

Evidence highlights

  • ISAT: coiling improved survival and reduced dependency at 1 year (23.7% vs 30.6%); long-term outcomes at 5 years were similar.
  • Long-term: small but higher incidence of rebleeding with coiling; higher incidence of seizures with clipping.
  • 2023 AHA/ASA: modality choice is "highly nuanced"; coiling recommended over clipping for ruptured posterior circulation aneurysms amenable to coiling.
  • Clipping may be preferred in patients <40 years for durability of treatment.
  • For good-grade anterior circulation aneurysms equally suitable for both, primary coiling is recommended in preference to clipping to improve 1-year functional outcome — but both are reasonable for favourable long-term outcome.

Alternative / salvage options

  • Aneurysm wrapping (wall reinforcement) — for aneurysms not amenable to clipping.
  • Parent artery sacrifice ± bypass revascularisation — giant, fusiform, or dissecting aneurysms.
  • Stent-assisted coiling / flow diverters — for wide-neck aneurysms not amenable to clipping or primary coiling; note higher thrombogenicity requiring dual antiplatelet therapy and higher haemorrhagic risk in the acute phase.
Timing Surgical or endovascular treatment of the ruptured aneurysm should be performed as early as feasible, preferably within 24 hours of onset. Complete obliteration is indicated whenever feasible to reduce rebleeding and retreatment risk.
Systems of care Timely transfer to a high-volume centre with a dedicated neurocritical care unit and a multidisciplinary team (experienced cerebrovascular surgeons / neurointerventionalists) is associated with lower mortality and better functional outcomes.
🎯6. Anaesthetic Goals & Cerebral Physiology (TMP vs CPP)
The core tension TMP = MAP − ICP (drives aneurysm rupture) and CPP = MAP − ICP (drives brain perfusion). Same equation, opposing physiological goals.

Goals of anaesthesia

  • Minimise changes in TMP of the aneurysm — avoid abrupt rises in MAP.
  • Maintain adequate CPP — avoid hypotension.
  • Provide optimal brain relaxation — facilitate surgical exposure and reduce retraction injury.
  • Smooth induction and emergence with strict blood pressure control.
  • Facilitate intraoperative neuromonitoring and temporary clipping.
  • Anticipate and manage aneurysm rupture.
  • Prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting; minimise pain.

Blood pressure targets

ScenarioTarget
Unruptured aneurysm, or good-grade SAH with normal ICPSystolic BP ≤ patient's normal systolic BP. Increasing MAP raises TMP and rupture risk.
Poor-grade SAH with intracranial hypertensionA higher MAP target is often required to maintain CPP because ICP is elevated.
Unsecured ruptured aneurysm (any)SBP <160 mmHg or MAP <110 mmHg; avoid severe hypotension (MAP <65 mmHg) and BP variability.
After aneurysm securedNormalise BP; CPP >60–70 mmHg.
Historical practice — now abandoned Induced and permissive hypotension were once routine to reduce blood loss and improve the surgical field. Improved microsurgical access and concerns about cerebral ischaemia and postoperative organ dysfunction have led to a decline in both practices.

Ventilation & CO2

  • Normocapnia is the default target (PaCO2 30–35 mmHg).
  • Hyperventilation lowers ICP but may increase TMP and rupture risk — use judiciously, briefly, and only if other ICP measures fail.
  • Hypercapnia must be avoided — causes cerebral vasodilation, swelling, and raised ICP.
  • Avoid hypoxia and hyperoxia extremes; both hypo- and hypercarbia worsen neurological outcomes.

Head pinning

  • Application of cranial pins is highly noxious and provokes a hypertensive response.
  • Blunt with local anaesthetic infiltration at pin sites, scalp blocks, or additional anaesthetic agent/opioid before pin application.
🩺7. Preoperative Preparation & Intraoperative Monitoring

Preoperative optimisation (aSAH)

  • Oxygenation & ventilation: ensure patent airway, adequate oxygenation and ventilation. Avoid hypoxia (worsens brain injury) and both hypo-/hypercarbia.
  • Indications for tracheal intubation:
    • Comatose patient with GCS ≤8
    • Hypoxia or hypoventilation
    • Haemodynamic instability
    • Deep sedation or general anaesthesia needed for imaging
  • Restoration of cerebral perfusion: early EVD lowers raised ICP and restores perfusion; also facilitates surgical exposure.
    Common thresholds for EVD: ventriculomegaly with GCS ≤12; Hunt & Hess grade ≥2.
  • Nimodipine 60 mg PO/NG every 4 hours — start within 48 hours of aSAH and continue for 21 days. Standard of care; improves outcomes.
  • Reverse anticoagulation — discontinue all antithrombotics; emergency reversal with appropriate agents.
  • Seizure prophylaxis — not routine; controversial and associated with worse neurological/cognitive outcomes. Consider in high-risk features.
  • Supportive: adequate analgesia for headache/neck pain; maintain euvolemia with crystalloids and monitor urine output; DVT prophylaxis with intermittent pneumatic compression.

Intraoperative monitoring

💉8. Induction, Maintenance & Anaesthetic Agents

Induction

  • Primary goal: maintain haemodynamic stability and minimise rapid changes in arterial pressure.
  • Avoid hypertension in response to laryngoscopy/intubation — it increases the risk of rebleeding.
  • Strategies: ensure adequate anaesthetic depth prior to laryngoscopy; use short-acting opioids or antihypertensives alongside induction drugs.
  • Judicious doses of an opioid + hypnotic + neuromuscular blocking drug. Laryngoscopy performed with care; supplement with additional anaesthetic/opioid as needed.
  • Hypotension following induction increases the risk of cerebral ischaemia and should be aggressively treated with vasopressors.
  • Avoid hypo- and hypercarbia during bag-mask ventilation.
  • Succinylcholine may be used for rapid sequence induction if needed.

Maintenance

  • Choice depends on neurological status, comorbidities, treatment approach, and need for IONM.
  • A balanced technique with both inhaled and intravenous agents is commonly used.
  • TIVA is often considered ideal because of:
    • Superior cerebral haemodynamics
    • Minimal interference with IONM
    • More efficient burst suppression
  • There is no strong evidence favouring volatile agents or TIVA for intracranial aneurysm surgery — but TIVA is preferred when IONM is used.
  • Propofol maintains coupling between CMRO2 and CBF. Inhaled anaesthetics have a dose-dependent effect on CBF: at <1 MAC they decrease CBF; at higher doses they cause cerebral vasodilation and uncoupling of CMRO2 and CBF.
  • The cerebral vasodilatory effect of volatiles can be minimised by hyperventilation.
  • Avoid hypertension before the aneurysm is secured. Critical periods: skull pin placement, patient positioning, surgical stimulation.

🧠9. Brain Relaxation, ICP & Metabolic Control

A "relaxed brain" protects against retraction injury and compression-induced ischaemia, and facilitates surgical exposure.

Strategies for intraoperative brain relaxation

  • 30° head-up positioning — facilitates cerebral venous drainage.
  • Maintaining adequate depth of anaesthesia and analgesia.
  • Optimising haemodynamic parameters.
  • Normocarbia to moderate hypocarbia (PaCO2 30–35 mmHg). Brief hyperventilation to PaCO2 <30 mmHg may be used if other ICP-reduction manoeuvres fail — but beware compromised cerebral perfusion.
  • Avoid excessive concentrations of volatile agents (inherent vasodilatory effects).
  • Osmotic agents: mannitol or hypertonic saline. Both reduce ICP and increase CBF/brain relaxation.
    • Mannitol — potent diuretic; can cause hypovolaemia and hypotension.
    • Hypertonic saline — increases serum sodium, minimal diuresis, may increase BP.
  • CSF drainage via external ventricular drain.
  • Avoid hypoosmotic fluids; isosmotic and sometimes hyperosmotic fluids are favoured.

Metabolic control

ParameterTarget / rationale
Glucose Target 80–180 mg/dL. Both hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia are linked to increased vasospasm risk and poor outcomes. Intraoperative hyperglycaemia is associated with long-term cognitive and neurological changes.
Temperature Normothermia. The IHAST trial showed intraoperative hypothermia did not improve neurological outcome after craniotomy in good-grade aneurysmal SAH. Hyperthermia should also be avoided.
Sodium Maintain normonatraemia; monitor for CSW vs SIADH (see postoperative section).
Volume status Euvolemia. Hypovolaemia may contribute to DCI; hypervolaemia has no benefit and is potentially harmful.
✂️10. Surgical Approaches & Temporary Clipping

Surgical approaches & positioning

ApproachIndicationPositioning
Pterional (fronto-temporo-sphenoidal) craniotomy Anterior circulation aneurysms; basilar apex and superior cerebellar artery aneurysms. May involve modified orbitozygomatic osteotomies for deep-seated aneurysms. Supine, ipsilateral shoulder raised, head turned to the opposite side.
Retrosigmoid craniotomy Posterior circulation aneurysms. May be augmented with C1 laminectomy and occipital endoscopy for a "far-lateral" approach. "Park bench" lateral position, head rotated towards the opposite shoulder.
Keyhole (mini-craniotomy ≤3 cm) Supraorbital, lateral supraorbital, pterional mini-craniotomies. Reverse cone-shaped corridor with endoscopic technique. Shorter operative times, less blood loss, shorter stays — but long-term outcomes not well established and reduced visualisation/manoeuvrability. Varies by approach.

Temporary clipping

  • Temporary clips are placed on the parent artery proximal to the aneurysm to induce temporary vessel occlusion.
  • Closing pressure is much lower than permanent clips: 0.88–1.01 N — minimises vessel trauma.
  • Indications:
    • Large, complex aneurysms with fragile walls
    • Aid dissection and delineate morphology
    • Reduce rupture risk and reduce aneurysm turgor before permanent clip application
    • Suction decompression techniques, sac remodelling, readjustment of permanent clips
    • Control of bleeding in intraoperative rupture
  • Prolonged application → ischaemia of the corresponding arterial territory and worse neurological outcome.
Safe duration The maximal permissible duration remains undefined (studies report 0–93 min). A safe limit is generally considered ≤7 minutes, beyond which ischaemia risk increases. Clip application of up to 10 minutes is usually well tolerated in the MCA territory. Intermittent application is safer than a single continuous prolonged clip.

Strategies to reduce focal ischaemia during temporary clipping

  • Induced hypertension: increase MAP by 20–30% above baseline (or SBP by 10–20%) to increase collateral blood flow.
  • Reduce CMRO2 / burst suppression for prolonged temporary clipping:
    • Thiopental commonly used
    • Alternatives achieving isoelectric EEG: propofol, etomidate, sevoflurane
  • Critical: the protective drugs must be given before application of temporary clips. There is no evidence that supplemental drugs given during temporary clipping improve outcomes.
  • Intermittent clipping with intervals to allow reperfusion — safer than a single continuous prolonged occlusion.
  • When IONM (SSEP/MEP) is used, subsequent clip application typically waits until signals return to baseline.
  • During removal, staggered release of clips creates intermittent reperfusion — ischaemic post-conditioning, reducing reperfusion injury.
Caution on clip removal Removing the temporary clip exposes the unsecured aneurysm to significant shear stress, risking rupture. The optimal reperfusion interval between clippings is unknown.

After the aneurysm is secured

  • Blood pressure can be normalised.
  • Hemodynamic goals: SBP <160 mmHg, MAP <110 mmHg, CPP >60–70 mmHg.
⚡11. Intraoperative Neurophysiological Monitoring & ICG Angiography

Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM)

Used for prompt detection of cerebral ischaemia, particularly during temporary clip application. Detection facilitates corrective actions: augmentation of MAP, retractor adjustment, and clip manipulation to restore blood flow.

ModalityWhat it monitorsNotes
SSEPs
(somatosensory evoked potentials)		 Dorsal column–medial lemniscal pathway — cortical electrical activity in response to peripheral nerve stimulation. Also thalamic subcortical activity (posterior cerebral artery territory) via median nerve SSEP. Useful for anterior and posterior circulation aneurysms. Somatosensory cortex and internal capsule lie within ACA and MCA supply.
MEPs
(motor evoked potentials)		 Corticospinal tract — stimulate motor cortex, record from corresponding peripheral muscles. Detects subcortical ischaemia (corona radiata / internal capsule) from inadequate perforator perfusion. Direct cortical or transcranial electrical stimulation. Recording electrodes contralateral to the surgical site.
BAEPs
(brainstem auditory evoked potentials)		 Brainstem auditory pathways. Useful for posterior circulation aneurysm surgery. Acoustic stimulus via ear insert; recording near mastoid/earlobe.

Indocyanine green (ICG) videoangiography

  • Imaging technique to confirm clip placement and complete obliteration of the aneurysm sac.
  • Uses infrared imaging for real-time visualisation of blood flow during surgery.
  • ICG injected through a peripheral vein; a surgical microscope with integrated near-infrared camera visualises dye fluorescence.
  • Dose: 0.2–0.5 mg/kg (maximum 5 mg/kg).
  • Advantages over DSA: faster, no extensive logistical support.
  • Limitations: poor visualisation of thrombosed and calcified vessels or aneurysms.
  • Transient oxygen desaturation is common after ICG injection.
  • DSA remains the gold standard for intraoperative evaluation of clip placement.
🩸12. Intraoperative Aneurysm Rupture & Circulatory Arrest

Rupture rates by phase

1.1%During exposure
15.2%During aneurysm dissection
6.7%During clip application / manipulation
up to 40%Mortality if not managed swiftly

Risk factors for intraoperative rupture

  • Hypertension
  • Pre-existing rupture of the aneurysm
  • Anterior communicating artery location
  • Larger sac size
Prevention — team preparation A rupture simulation during the preoperative briefing helps ensure team members are prepared for prompt, coordinated action. Close communication with the surgical team is essential, and arterial pressure targets should be clearly communicated.

Management of intraoperative rupture

  1. Communicate clearly with the surgical team; state BP targets aloud.
  2. Brief period of controlled hypotension — a 10–20% reduction in MAP may be necessary to improve aneurysm visualisation and facilitate temporary clipping.
  3. Bolus of anaesthetic agent (propofol or thiopental) — lowers arterial pressure and further reduces CMRO2.
  4. Prepare for high blood loss — ensure large-bore access and blood products available.
  5. Consider adenosine-induced flow arrest if visualisation remains inadequate.

Adenosine-induced temporary flow arrest

  • Induces a brief cardiac standstill, creating a global reduction in CBF and cessation of flow to the aneurysm.
  • Provides a bloodless surgical field — useful during intraoperative rupture, and in unruptured aneurysms at the skull base where temporary clipping is challenging.
  • Rapid onset and offset. Mechanism: action on A1 receptors → depressed sinoatrial node activity, slowed AV nodal conduction, reduced ventricular automaticity; also vasodilatory.
  • Cardiovascular effects range from AV nodal blockade, bradycardia and profound hypotension, to brief sinus arrest followed by spontaneous return of rhythm.
  • Recovery may be preceded by transient arrhythmias — atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, atrial flutter.
ParameterDetail

🔄13. Anaesthetic Considerations for Endovascular Coiling

Anaesthetic goals are similar to surgical clipping, with the following exceptions:

  • IONM is not commonly used.
  • Interventions to reduce brain swelling are not needed.
  • Patient immobility is critical, especially during coil deployment.
  • Heparin is commonly administered for anticoagulation and may need to be reversed.
General anaesthesia — advantagesGeneral anaesthesia — disadvantages
  • Immobility during the procedure
  • Patient comfort during a stimulating procedure
  • Easier control of comorbidities / neurological condition
  • Airway protection and control of ventilation (PaCO2 manipulation, apnea)
  • Haemodynamic instability during intubation and/or extubation
  • Anaesthetic-induced hypotension causing cerebral hypoperfusion
  • Inability to monitor the patient's neurological status
  • Complications from coughing/straining during extubation
  • Delayed emergence or impaired neuro exam from residual anaesthetic drugs

Monitored anaesthesia care may be used for diagnostic angiography, but GA is usually preferred for endovascular coiling. The same requirements for induction/intubation and emergence/extubation apply as during open craniotomy.

Rupture during endovascular therapy

  • Rare, but the increasing reliance on endovascular techniques has increased the incidence of procedure-related rupture.
  • Many cases can be managed with rescue endovascular techniques; some require emergency conversion to surgical clipping.
  • Life-threatening event requiring urgent transfer to the operating theatre — significant logistical and clinical challenges.
Key contrast with surgical rupture Unlike intraoperative rupture during surgical clipping — where controlled hypotension may be used to enhance visualisation — rupture during endovascular therapy necessitates an augmented arterial pressure to preserve adequate CPP.

Management of endovascular rupture / emergency transfer

  • Most patients will already be anaesthetised with tracheal intubation; if not, intubate expeditiously and in a neuroprotective manner.
  • Induction should minimise acute changes in arterial pressure; maintain sedation during transfer.
  • Expect a surge in ICP: 30° head-up positioning, osmotherapy, and a normal PaCO2 may help.
  • Pupil assessment is often the most useful indicator of impending uncal and transtentorial herniation.
  • Continuous communication between teams is essential to avoid delays and ensure stability during transfer.
🌅14. Emergence & Extubation

Goals

  • Smooth emergence without coughing, straining, or hypertension.
  • Coughing and straining increase intrathoracic pressure and cause transient increases in cerebral arterial and venous pressure → oedema, bleeding, raised ICP.
  • Ensure a smooth emergence to allow early neurological assessment.

Techniques

  • Short-acting opioids titrated to spontaneous ventilation towards the end of the procedure.
  • Remifentanil or dexmedetomidine infusions may be continued throughout emergence to reduce airway reactivity.
  • Lidocaine 1–1.5 mg/kg IV — effective for reducing airway reflexes.
  • Avoid nitrous oxide — increased risk of pneumocephalus and PONV.
  • Dexmedetomidine used during the procedure or prior to closing can reduce the response to emergence and hypertension in the PACU.

Post-emergence hypertension

Hypertension increases the risk of intracerebral haemorrhage after craniotomy and should be aggressively treated.

ClassAgentsCautions
Beta-blockersLabetalol, esmololCaution in stress-induced cardiomyopathy

Deferring extubation

Discuss with the neurosurgical team. The anaesthetic is still weaned off to allow a clinical neurologic evaluation.

Indications for postoperative ventilation
  • Preoperative intubation
  • Low preoperative GCS
  • Extremely long surgery (>8–10 hours)
  • Posterior circulation / posterior cranial fossa / close to the brainstem
  • High risk for development of cerebral oedema

If planned extubation cannot be performed within 1 hour post-surgery → perform an immediate head CT.

🏥15. Postoperative Care & Neurological Deterioration

Patients are typically admitted to a neurointensive care or high-dependency area for close neuro-observation.

Most common causes of postoperative deterioration Hydrocephalus · Cerebral oedema · Delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI) · Seizures

Hydrocephalus

  • Affects ~20–30% of patients with aneurysmal SAH.
  • Mechanism: blood in the ventricles obstructs CSF flow and impairs CSF reabsorption.
  • Any neurological deterioration postoperatively should prompt a CT brain scan.
  • Treatment: external ventricular drain, lumbar drain, or permanent CSF shunt. Urgent CSF diversion (EVD and/or lumbar drainage) for acute symptomatic hydrocephalus.
  • ~20% of patients need long-term CSF diversion (ventriculoperitoneal shunt) for chronic hydrocephalus.
  • Use an EVD bundled protocol addressing insertion, management, education, and monitoring to reduce infection and complications.

Cerebral oedema

  • Follows a biphasic pattern after aneurysmal SAH.
  • Complex pathophysiology: direct ischaemic insult at initial haemorrhage + failure of the blood–brain barrier.
  • May present as late neurological decline; associated with poor prognosis.
  • Diagnosis: imaging (CT, MRI) or ICP monitoring.
  • No specific treatment beyond strict neuroprotective strategies.
  • Refractory raised ICP → decompressive craniectomy may be necessary.

Delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI) & vasospasm

Closely related but two distinct entities.

Delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI)Vasospasm
Definition Focal neurological impairment (e.g. hemiparesis, aphasia) or a decrease of ≥2 points on the GCS, lasting ≥1 hour, not apparent immediately after aneurysm occlusion and not attributable to other causes. An angiographic diagnosis — radiological evidence of arterial narrowing.
Relationship Can occur without vasospasm; up to 25% of delayed cerebral infarcts do not correspond with areas of arterial narrowing. Can lead to DCI, but clinical symptoms/signs are apparent in only ~30% of patients with vasospasm.
Timing Occurs in ~30% of patients, mostly between days 4 and 14; incidence peaks days 3–14. Peak risk post-bleed days 6–10.
Mortality — ~21%

Pathophysiology of DCI

  • Release of cytokines — endothelin-1, IL-6, IL-1β — activated by oxyhaemoglobin in the subarachnoid space → vasoconstriction.
  • Also implicated: blood–brain barrier disruption, microthrombosis, cortical spreading depolarisation/ischaemia, failure of cerebral autoregulation, capillary transit-time heterogeneity.
  • Affects up to 70% of patients with aneurysmal SAH; can also occur after clipping of unruptured aneurysms.

Monitoring & detection

ModalityRole
Serial neurological examination (GCS / NIHSS)Frequent assessment is recommended to monitor DCI and secondary complications. A decrease in GCS by ≥2 points is associated with clinical DCI.
Transcranial Doppler (TCD)Non-invasive bedside technique; MCA vasospasm usually defined as mean CBF velocity ≥120 cm/s and Lindegaard ratio ≥3. High negative predictive value (~92%). Operator dependent; limited by temporal bone window.
CTA / CT perfusion (CTP)Useful when vasospasm suspected or neurological examination limited. CTA sensitivity ~91% for central vasospasm. CTP can predict DCI; positive predictive value ~0.67.
Continuous EEG (cEEG)Useful in high-grade aSAH to predict DCI. Alarms: decreasing relative alpha power variability, decreasing alpha-to-delta ratio, worsening focal slowing, late-onset epileptiform abnormalities.
Invasive neuromonitoringBrain tissue oxygen (PbtO₂), cerebral microdialysis (lactate/pyruvate ratio, glutamate) — may be considered in high-grade aSAH.

Management of DCI / vasospasm

InterventionDetail
Nimodipine Class 160 mg enteral every 4 hours. Early initiation prevents DCI and improves functional outcomes. Standard of care.
Euvolemia Class 2aMaintain euvolemia — beneficial in preventing DCI. Hypervolemia is potentially harmful (excess morbidity).

Seizures

  • Prophylactic antiseizure medications are not routinely recommended; may be reasonable for patients at high risk:
    • Ruptured MCA aneurysm
    • High-grade SAH
    • Intraparenchymal haemorrhage
    • Hydrocephalus
    • Cortical infarction
    • Raised ICP
  • Levetiracetam is often preferred because of its favourable safety profile.
  • Phenytoin should not be used — it increases the metabolism of nimodipine and is associated with higher morbidity and mortality in SAH, plus poorer cognitive outcomes.
  • New-onset seizures after aSAH: treatment with antiseizure medication for 7 days is recommended.
  • cEEG monitoring is reasonable in patients with fluctuating neurological examination, depressed mental state, or high-risk features, to detect nonconvulsive seizures.

Hyponatraemia

  • Occurs in up to 30% of patients with aSAH.
  • More likely due to cerebral salt-wasting (CSW) from release of natriuretic peptide than SIADH.

The cause is often iatrogenic, related to hyperosmolar therapy (hypertonic saline or mannitol).

Diabetes insipidus

  • Caused by hypothalamic/pituitary ischaemia from DCI related to vasospasm or reduced cerebral perfusion in the setting of intracranial hypertension.
  • Rarely arises intraoperatively; usually occurs 12–48 hours postoperatively.
  • Clinical picture: polyuria with rising serum sodium and serum osmolality.
  • Diagnosis: urine output >250 mL for 2 hours, Na >140, low urine osmolality or urine specific gravity <1.005.
  • Treatment: IV DDAVP (desamino D-arginine vasopressin) 1–2 µg and volume repletion.
  • Hypernatraemia in the subacute phase of SAH is associated with poor outcomes.

Cardiac complications

  • SAH can result in large reversible myocardial "stunning" — neurogenic stunned myocardium, similar to Takotsubo cardiomyopathy.
  • Troponin commonly increases (usually below the diagnostic threshold for MI); peak levels correlate with severity of neurological injury and myocardial dysfunction.
  • Mechanism is likely catecholamine-mediated.
  • Severe acute heart failure can develop with ejection fractions commonly 10–30%; akinesis at apex and midventricle with preserved base.
  • ECG changes: classic canyon T waves, nonspecific T-wave changes, QT prolongation, ST-segment depression, U waves.
  • Prolonged QT (>550 ms) is associated with increased malignant ventricular rhythms including torsades de pointes.
  • Supportive measures: enhanced invasive cardiovascular monitoring (echocardiography, pulmonary artery catheter), inotropes, vasopressors, antiarrhythmics.

Other medical complications

  • Respiratory failure / ARDS: implement a standardised ICU care bundle in patients ventilated >24 hours (lung-protective low tidal volume ventilation, moderate PEEP, early enteral nutrition, standardised antibiotics for hospital-acquired pneumonia, systematic approach to extubation).
  • VTE: pharmacological or mechanical prophylaxis is recommended once the ruptured aneurysm has been secured.
  • Glycaemic control: effective control, strict hyperglycaemia management, and avoidance of hypoglycaemia are reasonable.
  • Fever: common, often refractory to antipyretics, associated with worse outcomes. Benefit of therapeutic temperature management is uncertain.
  • Nutrition: protein malnutrition is common; high-protein supplementation may improve outcome in select patients.
