Paediatric Anaesthesia: URTI Quick Reference

Perioperative Management of Upper Respiratory Tract Infections

1. Key Risks & Pathophysiology
  • Increased Risk: Children with current or recent URTI have a 2-3 fold increased risk of Perioperative Respiratory Adverse Events (PRAEs).[1]
  • PRAEs include: Laryngospasm, bronchospasm, desaturation, severe coughing, breath holding, and airway obstruction.[1]
  • Pathophysiology: Airway hyperreactivity and inflammation can persist for up to 4-8 weeks post-infection.[1]
  • High-Risk Patient Factors: Age < 2 years (especially < 6 months), prematurity, ASA physical status 3+, asthma, congenital heart disease, obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), and passive smoking.[1]
  • COVID-19: Symptomatic COVID-19 carries a 3.7x higher incidence of desaturation compared to asymptomatic. Follows similar risk profiles to other URTIs.[1]
2. Pre-operative Assessment & Timing

Timing of Surgery

  • Mild URTI: May proceed if otherwise well and surgery is minor. Delay for at least 2 weeks after symptom resolution is recommended for severe URTI.[1]
  • COVID-19: Delay elective surgery for 2 weeks (mild) to 4 weeks (severe).[1]
  • Recent Infection: Children undergoing surgery within the first 2 weeks after symptom resolution are at similar risk to those with active symptoms. Baseline risk returns within 4 weeks.[1]

Risk Stratification Tools

COLDS Score[1]

Each category is assigned 1, 2, or 5 points. Scores range from 5 to 25. Any category scoring 5 points is considered a "red flag".

Category None (1 pt) Mild (2 pts) Moderate/Severe (5 pts)
Current signs/symptoms No URTI symptoms Parent confirms URTI, purulence, wet cough, congestion, rhinorrhoea, sore throat, sneezing, low fever or dry cough <2 weeks ago Abnormal lung sounds, lethargy, 'toxic' appearance, high fever
Lung disease None RSV, mild intermittent asthma, CLD if >1 yr old, loud snoring Moderate persistent asthma, infant with CLD, OSA, or pulmonary hypertension
Device (Airway) None or facemask Laryngeal mask airway (LMA) or supraglottic device Tracheal tube (ETT)
Surgery Other (including ear tubes) Minor airway (e.g., adenotonsillectomy, flexible bronchoscopy, dental extractions) Major airway (e.g., cleft palate, rigid bronchoscopy, maxillofacial)

Note: A score > 12.5 is associated with increased risk of PRAEs.[1]

3. Intraoperative Management (The "How-To")

Premedication & Induction

  • Anxiolytics: Avoid Midazolam (associated with increased PRAE). Dexmedetomidine is preferred (reduces PRAE, especially coughing and laryngospasm).[1]
  • Bronchodilators: Preoperative inhaled Salbutamol is recommended for those at risk of bronchospasm.[1]
  • Induction Agent: Propofol is superior to Sevoflurane or Thiopentone. It reduces airway reactivity and bronchospasm.[1]

Airway Management

  • Device Choice: Preference for Facemask > Supraglottic Airway (SGA/LMA) > Tracheal Tube (ETT). ETT use in URTI increases PRAE risk by 11x.[2]
  • Maintenance: Total Intravenous Anaesthesia (TIVA) with Propofol is preferred. Avoid Desflurane.[1]
  • Extubation: Deep removal of the airway device (in lateral position) is often beneficial to reduce coughing and bronchospasm, but requires experienced staff to manage potential upper airway obstruction.[1]
4. Postoperative Plan

Children with URTI require close monitoring in the immediate postoperative period due to the risk of residual airway hyperreactivity and respiratory depression.[1]

  • Respiratory Monitoring: Appropriate respiratory monitoring (pulse oximetry) in the immediate postoperative period is mandatory.[1]
  • PACU Staff: Ensure Postoperative Anaesthesia Care Unit (PACU) staff are well-educated on recognising airway obstruction and performing airway manoeuvres in children.[1]
  • Handover: Use standardised handover protocols to communicate the specific risks and intraoperative events to the recovery team.[1]
  • Observation: Close observation by experienced staff must be available until the patient is fully awake, especially if deep extubation was performed.[1]
  • Apnoea Risk: Premature and ex-premature babies up to 50 weeks post-conceptual age are at risk of postoperative apnoea. Close observation for at least 24 hours is mandatory.[2]
MSPA Post-op Tips: Ensure adequate hydration, humidification, and consider anticholinergics to reduce secretions. Early use of blood products (if indicated) and standardised handover protocols improve outcomes.[2]
5. Comparison & Contrast: Journal Review vs. MSPA Handbook

While both sources agree on the fundamental management of paediatric URTI, there are subtle differences in emphasis and timing recommendations.

Feature Journal Review (Stepanovic et al.)[1] MSPA Handbook[2]
Delay Period 2 weeks for mild; 4 weeks for severe (fever > 40°C). Baseline returns in 4 weeks. General consensus for reschedule is 2-4 weeks. Algorithm suggests 4 weeks for severe symptoms.
Risk Magnitude Emphasises 2-3x increased risk. PRAE risk is 24-30% if current URTI vs 8-17% without. Emphasises 2-7x increased risk. Intubated URTI has 11x increased risk.
Airway Device Strong emphasis on SGA > ETT. Highlights deep extubation to reduce bronchospasm. Algorithm includes "Avoid ETT" and "Consider LMA". Mentions humidification and anticholinergics.
Premedication Recommends alpha-2 agonists (Dexmedetomidine) and preoperative Salbutamol. Warns against Midazolam. Algorithm suggests management options like pulse oximetry, hydration, and anticholinergics. Less specific on premedication agents.
COVID-19 Specifics Extensive discussion. Delay 2 weeks for mild, 4 weeks for severe. Symptomatic COVID-19 has higher desaturation risk. Mentions infectious aetiology in algorithm. Adopts a pragmatic approach to proceed if not severe.
Risk Stratification Discusses the COLDS score and validated scoring systems. Provides a step-by-step clinical algorithm based on urgency, severity, and risk factors.
Postoperative Care Focus on PACU education, standardised handover, and close observation. Focus on hydration, humidification, anticholinergics, and 24-hour apnoea monitoring for at-risk infants.
