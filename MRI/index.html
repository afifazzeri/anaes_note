The essentials

  • The magnet is always on — even between scans. There is no "off switch" for routine use.
  • Hazards fall into five groups: displacement force, induced currents, acoustic noise, RF heating, helium escape (quench).
  • In an emergency you move the patient out — you never quench the magnet to resuscitate.
  • "MRI compatible" is obsolete terminology. Use MR Safe / MR Conditional / MR Unsafe.
2 Physics in brief

MRI exploits nuclei that possess an odd, unpaired proton. The hydrogen proton (abundant in water) is the workhorse; phosphorus (in ATP) allows metabolic studies.

  • These nuclei spin, and because they carry charge they generate a tiny local magnetic field — they behave like small magnets.
  • Alignment is normally random; in a strong external field they align with the field.
  • A brief burst of radiofrequency (RF) energy knocks them out of alignment. When the RF is removed, they relax back and re-emit energy.
  • The rate of realignment depends on the nucleus and the molecular properties of the tissue — this is the source of image contrast.
  • The emitted RF induces an electrical signal in three orthogonal gradient coils (X and Y transverse, Z longitudinal), giving spatial encoding → 3-D image.
  • Rapid switching of the gradient coils is what causes the loud banging noise.

T1 vs T2 weighting

  • T1-weighted — generated a few milliseconds after the RF field is removed (early).
  • T2-weighted — generated later than T1.
  • Hydrogen nuclei take a long time to decay back, so fluid appears dark on T1 (minimal signal) and white on T2.

The Faraday cage & waveguide

  • The MR signal is very weak, so external RF would swamp it. The scanner room is enclosed in an RF shield — a Faraday cage — built into the fabric of the room.
  • A hollow brass tube called a waveguide passes through the Faraday cage so that infusion lines and monitoring cables can reach the patient from the control room.
3 Scanner anatomy at a glance
  • Most diagnostic scanners use a cylindrical bore: the patient is enclosed in a narrow tube with extremely limited access.
  • Open / C-shaped magnets are less claustrophobic and allow better access, but lower field strength, longer scans and less detail.
  • Two-room interventional suites (scanner adjacent to theatre) are increasingly used for diagnostic-quality imaging during procedures.
4 Magnetic field & safety zones

Field strength falls away exponentially with distance from the magnet.

ZoneFieldWhat happensRule
5 Gauss line 0.5 mT Pacemakers and ICDs will malfunction Unscreened personnel must not enter
50 Gauss line 5 mT Significant attractive force on all ferromagnetic objects (projectile risk); electronic/mechanical equipment — including infusion pumps — may fail No loose ferrous items; pumps outside
In practice: many units simply divide the area into a safe zone outside the scanner and the controlled hazardous zone within the MR examination room. Field contour markers on the floor are used to identify safety ranges.

Access terminology (MHRA / local rules)

  • MR environment — immediate vicinity of the scanner: projectile risk, implant hazards, RF heating.
  • MR Controlled Access Area — larger area including control room and anaesthetic preparation room. Electronic/physical locks prevent unauthorised access; all routes to the MR environment pass through it.
  • MR Authorised Persons — named individuals with personal access. After appropriate training, anaesthetists and anaesthetic assistants are designated MR Authorised Persons and work in the MR environment under radiographer supervision.

Effects on the body

  • Movement within the field induces weak electrical currents in tissue.
  • Blood flowing through the aorta also generates potentials.
  • These can cause nausea, vertigo (semicircular canal excitation) and flashing lights (retinal effect) — in patients and staff moving near the bore.
  • No evidence of long-term harm from repeated exposure. Older guidance suggested a time-weighted average of 200 mT over any 8-hour period should not be exceeded by healthcare personnel. Ideally all staff vacate the room while scanning.
  • The Control of Electromagnetic Fields at Work Regulations 2016 requires employers to carry out risk assessments for staff working in the MR environment.
5 Hazards — the five categories

Additional environment hazards

  • Restricted access — patient enclosed in a narrow tube; the MR suite is often remote from theatre, so backup and assistance are less readily available.
  • Monitoring interference — the scanner interferes with monitors; monitors interfere with the scanner. Both directions must be managed.
  • Contrast reactions — see the gadolinium section.
  • Temperature — historical concern about hypothermia in infants is largely unfounded: RF absorption raises body temperature. Active heating is usually unnecessary and may cause hyperthermia. The rise is greater at 3 T than 1.5 T.
  • Pregnancy — see the dedicated section.

Screening

  • All patients and staff must be screened for implants and devices before entering.
  • Screening is the responsibility of the imaging department (radiographers, radiologists, MR Safety Expert / MR Responsible Person), but every anaesthetist must understand the risks.
  • Especially important for ICU transfers and anyone unable to give an accurate history.
  • Establish the exact make and model of any implant in advance — similar-looking devices can behave very differently.
  • A standard screening questionnaire or "metal check" usually suffices; X-ray can be used if doubt exists.
  • Compatibility can be checked at mrisafety.com and via the manufacturer's guidance.
  • The referring team should discuss device safety with the MR Responsible Person and the anaesthetist to plan management.
Never allow a patient into the MR examination room if there is any doubt about the safety of an implanted device or foreign body.

General principles

  • Monitoring screens should be in the MR control room so the anaesthetist can leave the examination room. Cables pass through the waveguide ports.
  • All alarms should be visual because of scanner noise.
  • The view of the monitor, anaesthetic machine and patient must be unobstructed at all times.
  • Avoid coiling cables within the scanner; place padding between all leads and the patient's skin.
  • Mains supply should be isolated or filtered, or battery power used. Batteries are ferromagnetic — if used, secure the equipment firmly.
  • Where non-compatible equipment must be used (interventional settings), it may be physically controlled with a tether or by clear workflow controls under a standardised operating procedure (SOP).

Anaesthetic machine & breathing systems

  • MR Safe vaporisers and gas cylinders only, within the scanning room. Standard equipment may cause serious accidents.
  • Historic practice: ferrous anaesthetic machines in the control room with co-axial Mapleson D or Bain circuits extended through the waveguide. MR compatible machines, ventilators and vaporisers are now available and should be used instead.
  • Circle system, Bain circuit or Ayres T-piece (children) have all been used successfully.
  • Piped gases with backup cylinders made of non-ferrous metal (e.g. aluminium).
  • MR compatible scavenging systems are available — volatile agents and nitrous oxide must be scavenged in the usual way (COSHH regulations in the UK).
8 Acoustic noise
  • Noise above the safe level of 85 dB(A) can be produced by rapid gradient coil switching. Most scanners exceed 80 dB(A), at which hearing damage is possible.
  • Magnitude depends on the image sequence and the magnetic field strength.
  • UK guidelines require that everyone remaining in the scanner room is provided with MR Safe hearing protection — earplugs, ear defenders, or both.
  • Anaesthetised patients cannot report hearing discomfort — protection is especially important for them. Document its use in the patient's notes.
  • Recent studies suggest temporary hearing loss is possible with a 3 T scan even with standard hearing protection. Use effective protection and warn patients and staff.
  • Staff should remain in the control room during acquisition, or wear earplugs if they must stay in the examination room. Staff should be trained in selection and use of hearing protection.
  • High ambient noise masks monitor alarms and the sounds of partial airway obstruction — maintain vigilance and attend to visual cues.
For the anaesthetic team, a set-up allowing remote monitoring from the control room is ideal. If this is not possible, ear protection must be worn by staff remaining in the room — but communication will be difficult.
9 Contrast agents — gadolinium
  • Up to 30 % of MR scans require IV contrast.
  • The most commonly used agent is gadolinium dimeglumine (Gd-DTPA, Magnevist), a gadolinium-based agent.
  • It increases signal intensity on T1 and reduces signal on T2 weighted scans.
  • Used in contrast-enhanced MR angiography and to identify tumours. It does not normally cross the blood–brain barrier, so it demonstrates areas where the barrier has broken down and helps delineate intracranial pathology.
  • Dose: 0.2 mL/kg.
  • Renally excreted within 24 hours.

Renal function

  • All patients should have an assessment of renal function before MRI — by history or by urea and creatinine assay.
  • If eGFR < 30 mL·min⁻¹·1.73 m⁻², balance the risk of gadolinium against the diagnostic benefit.
  • Current guidance: in children and in adults with a low GFR, do not repeat gadolinium within 7 days.
  • Macrocyclic gadolinium compounds bind the gadolinium much more tightly and have not been associated with any cases of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in the literature. eGFR estimation may not be required with these newer agents.
  • The safety profile in children under 2 years and in pregnancy is unknown.
10 Quenching & helium escape
  • Superconducting coils must be kept cold — immersed in liquid coolants (cryogens), most commonly liquid helium in modern units.
  • The cryostat typically contains around 1000 litres of liquid helium at a few degrees above absolute zero.
  • Quenching = rapid boil-off of the cryogen causing immediate loss of superconductivity.
  • It may occur spontaneously (system error during installation, servicing, power-up) or be deliberately induced to shut down the magnetic field.
  • If it happens, the magnetic field is lost and a large volume of helium gas is produced.
  • Normally vented to the outside atmosphere through a quench pipe.
  • If the quench pipe is damaged or blocked, helium builds up in the scanning room → asphyxiation risk, requiring rapid evacuation.
Safety systems: MR suites must have oxygen sensors, ventilation controls and a pressure equalisation mechanism to alert staff in the control room to a hypoxic environment. All staff working in an MR unit should be aware of the emergency quench procedure.
Quenching is not a resuscitation manoeuvre. In a cardiac arrest you remove the patient from the field — you do not quench the magnet.
11 Pregnancy — patients & staff

Potential hazards to the fetus

  • Static magnetic fields > 4 T
  • Low-frequency time-varying magnetic field gradients
  • Radiofrequency fields
  • Excessive noise effects on the fetus
  • Systemic heat load — of most relevance during organogenesis in early pregnancy, because of the potential for hyperthermia to cause teratogenic effects
However, there are no proven adverse effects from any of these factors.

Practical guidance

  • In the United Kingdom it is currently recommended that pregnant women should ideally not be scanned during the first trimester.
  • The decision to proceed should be a multidisciplinary decision that includes the mother. It should be accepted that some women will refuse a scan if there is even a theoretical risk.
  • Pregnant staff should be advised of the risks and given the option of not entering the inner controlled area during their first trimester.
  • The MHRA advises that pregnant staff do not remain in the scan room while imaging is underway.
12 Who needs anaesthesia? Patient selection

MRI requires the patient to lie still in a noisy, restricted space for prolonged periods. The vast majority of patients can do this without an anaesthetist.

All cases referred for general anaesthesia should be evaluated and the risks of anaesthesia weighed against the benefits of the investigation.

Alternatives to GA in infants and children

  • Behavioural techniques — reassurance, communication through booklets, videos and unit visits, rehearsal of scans, play specialists.
  • Natural sleep techniques — "feed and wrap" for neonates; sleep deprivation before the scan for toddlers.
  • Sedation — specialist, experienced nurse-led sedation services have been shown to be highly successful and very safe.

13 Conduct of general anaesthesia for MRI

Before induction

  • Pre-assess all patients. Starvation guidelines are the same as for any general anaesthetic.
  • A metal check must be performed by the radiology staff before induction.
  • Good communication between referring team, radiologist and anaesthetist — establish the detail required, any coil changes and the length of the scan. This may dictate ventilation or spontaneous breathing.
  • Place the patient on a non-ferrous trolley without oxygen cylinders so they can be safely transferred once anaesthetised.

Induction room

  • Induction should occur in a dedicated room adjacent to the MR examination room, containing all standard anaesthetic equipment, monitoring, drugs and resuscitation equipment. It can double as a post-anaesthesia recovery area.
  • Before transfer, perform a brief second metal check for loose objects that may have strayed onto the trolley — needles, oxygen cylinders, stethoscopes, etc.

In the MR examination room

  • Transfer the patient onto the MRI scanner and recommence maintenance of anaesthesia and monitoring immediately.
  • Check that the airway is secure — once inside the scanner the patient is relatively inaccessible. Head scans have a receiver coil over the head, further reducing access.
  • Maintenance may be inhalational (MR compatible vaporiser required) or intravenous.
  • TIVA is acceptable, but some infusion pumps may act as projectiles or malfunction close to the magnetic field. Keep pumps in the control room and pass connecting lines through the waveguide.
  • Specific TIVA safety issues during scanning:
    • High index of suspicion for problems with the IV cannula not being visible.
    • Failure to hear pump alarms because of earplugs or the position of the anaesthetist in the viewing room.
    • Long infusion lines — disconnection or high pressure may mean the anaesthetic is not being delivered.
    • The high-pressure alarm limit on infusion pumps may need to be adjusted.
    • Ensure the combination of infusion lines, pump(s) and pump settings means infusions do not stop due to excessive resistance or high-pressure alarm cut-out.
  • LMA or non-armoured tracheal tube: the pilot balloon must be secured away from the area to be scanned to prevent image distortion from the internal ferromagnetic spring.
  • Do not use armoured ETTs with a metal coil down their length — risk of dislodgement from magnetic pull on the metal, and the metal distorts the image.
  • Ear protection should be provided before commencing the scan, and removed before waking the patient.
  • The anaesthetist may then exit the MR examination room and monitor from the control room if facilities exist.

Recovery

  • Remove the patient from the scanner, wake and recover in a suitable recovery area, cared for by appropriately trained staff.
  • Analgesia is unlikely to be required for MRI scanning; paracetamol may help if the child has a sore throat or headache.
  • Many children can be managed as day cases, depending on co-morbidities, and discharged once they meet normal day-case discharge criteria.
14 Intensive care patients requiring MRI

MRI of critically ill adults and children is an increasingly important diagnostic and prognostic tool. These patients require special expertise, planning and time to be examined safely.

Infusions

  • Multiple drug infusions are a notable challenge — inotropic therapy is of particular concern.
  • Discontinue all unnecessary infusions.
  • Required infusions should run through extensions of adequate length passed through the waveguide to a pump remaining in the MR control room.
  • Non-compatible MR syringe drivers used inside the MR examination room may deliver incorrect drug doses — a significant patient safety danger.
  • If MR compatible infusion pumps are used in the examination room, the anaesthetist may need to remain in the room if the pump rate needs changing.

Monitoring

  • Critically ill patients require a higher standard of monitoring.
  • Change all monitoring equipment to MR compatible versions in the anaesthetic induction room before entering the MR examination room.
  • Arterial pressure transducers can be passed through the waveguide if not compatible.
  • Pulmonary artery catheters with conductive wires in contact with heart muscle and epicardial pacing wires pose a theoretical risk of microshock — remove before the examination.
  • Central venous catheters pose no risk to the patient.
  • Disconnect all in-dwelling catheters from electrical connections and external accessories before entering the MR examination room.

Other checks

  • Ensure no surgical intervention has left the patient with internal metal work. Where the history is vague, a pre-MR X-ray screen may be required.
  • Many tracheostomy tubes are not MR compatible and will need to be changed before the examination.
  • The pilot balloon of a cuffed tracheal tube may contain a small ferromagnetic spring — tape it securely away from the area being scanned.
  • MRI should only be undertaken if the diagnostic benefit outweighs the risk; this discussion must involve the multidisciplinary team, particularly for ICU patients.
  • Use the MR safety checklists for general anaesthesia, intraoperative MRI and transfer of ICU patients alongside the WHO checklist.
15 Emergencies & cardiac arrest in the MR suite
Because of the strong magnetic field, the risk of projectiles and the restricted access imposed by the scanner, it is impossible to manage emergencies and resuscitation within the scanning room or within the 5 Gauss line.

Diagnostic suite — the drill

  1. Remove the patient from the magnetic field as quickly as possible.
  2. Transfer to the induction room, which should be close to the scanner and contain the necessary anaesthetic and resuscitation equipment and drugs.
  3. The resuscitation team must not enter the 5 Gauss line of the inner controlled area. They should be directed by radiology staff to the induction/resuscitation room.
  4. Manage the cardiac arrest in accordance with current resuscitation guidance.
Do NOT switch off the magnet. Quenching causes an immediate loss of field and a large helium release, and puts the resuscitation team at risk.

Interventional suite

  • Where moving a patient during surgery may be difficult or impossible, a clear SOP defining which personnel and equipment may enter the room during an emergency is essential.
  • The roles of the radiographer and anaesthetist during evacuation of an anaesthetised or sedated patient should be well defined in an SOP and regularly reviewed.
16 Consent
  • Routine diagnostic MRI: written consent is not required. The only intervention is the anaesthetic.
  • The referring clinician or radiologist is responsible for seeking formal written consent for the MR scan itself — they will have discussed other options including not performing the imaging, and the impact on diagnosis and prognosis of omission.
  • The anaesthetist explains the anaesthesia to facilitate the scan, but currently this does not require separate written consent in addition to that taken for the scan.
  • If patients are referred from another hospital, consent should be taken by the referring clinician and a written agreed policy developed to avoid problems on the day of the scan.
  • In exceptional circumstances, the anaesthetist may seek consent for the scan if they understand the reasons for performing the imaging.
  • Each unit should develop its own local written consent procedures.
Consent for MRI under general anaesthesia is a complex issue. In order to obtain truly informed consent, input should ideally come from three parties: the referring clinician who requested the investigation, the radiologist performing the scan, and the anaesthetist responsible for the general anaesthesia. Recent review suggests the referring clinician is best suited to explain the intended benefits, side effects and risks, and thus to obtain written consent. Nevertheless it remains incumbent upon the responsible anaesthetist to review the anaesthetic plan and risks with the patient before the procedure.
17 Service organisation, training & governance

Recommendations

  • Every hospital providing anaesthesia within the MR unit should have a lead anaesthetist responsible for provision of anaesthesia for MRI.
  • Training should be provided for all grades of anaesthetist delivering anaesthesia in this remote area. All anaesthetists should understand the hazards.
  • Anaesthesia/sedation for MRI — including ICU patients — should take into account the patient's pathophysiological status and the remote location of the unit.
  • Wherever possible, anaesthesia in remote sites should be provided by appropriately experienced consultants.
  • When care is delegated to a trainee or SAS doctor, they should have the appropriate competencies and level of training.
  • It is not acceptable for inexperienced staff, unfamiliar with the MR environment, to manage a patient in this environment — particularly out-of-hours.
  • Patients must be accompanied to the scanner by appropriately trained staff. If an anaesthetic machine is used, the anaesthetist should be supported throughout by a suitably skilled, trained anaesthetic assistant familiar with the requirements.
  • Anyone remaining in the scanning room must be given ear protection.
  • MR safety checklists for general anaesthesia, intraoperative MRI and ICU transfer should be used alongside the WHO checklist.

Local sign-off before solo practice

Every hospital offering anaesthetic services for MRI should have a locally agreed safety assessment with documented sign-off before a solo anaesthetist of any grade undertakes MRI cases under GA for adults or children.

Agreed levels of supervision for trainees, particularly out-of-hours, are essential. It is recognised that in some hospitals no solo trainee anaesthetist can work in the MR unit.

Practical checks

  • An appropriate "pre-list" check of anaesthesia systems, facilities, equipment, supplies and resuscitation equipment should be performed before the start of each list.
  • All procedures should be compliant with NatSSIPs (National Safety Standards for Invasive Procedures) and the Safe Surgery Checklist.
  • Defined SOPs for anaesthesia in the MR environment are essential, including a modified WHO checklist and a specific MR safety checklist, leading to a signed safety form kept in the patient's care record.
  • Many hazards occur around entry to the MR environment — a clear ingress checklist completed immediately before entering the scanner room is particularly important.
  • Hospitals should develop and audit governance procedures to ensure anaesthetists of all grades are competent to deliver anaesthesia safely.
  • When new procedures are planned, the anaesthetic department must be involved in service development.

Suite design

  • Access control: locks preventing unauthorised access to the MR Controlled Access Area; all routes to the MR environment pass through outer spaces.
  • Visibility: a window (usually with fine mesh for RF screening) giving line-of-sight to the scanner, the patient and all access routes.
  • Anaesthetic input into design is essential — space for anaesthesia and emergency procedures, placement of the anaesthetic machine, piped gas outlets and suction, space for remote monitoring, and a clearly marked route for urgent access.
  • Consider possible changes in use at installation: a diagnostic suite may later be used for anaesthetised patients, or case mix in an interventional suite may change. Modifying an operational suite is often prohibitively expensive because of the always-on field and RF cage integrity.

Staff safety

  • It is the responsibility of the radiology department to screen and train all staff working within the MR environment, using the same protocols as for patients.
  • Records of both screening and training should be kept and reviewed regularly.
  • Access is often restricted to appropriately trained personnel.
18 Paediatrics
  • Younger children, and those with learning difficulties or involuntary movement disorders, will require sedation or general anaesthesia to produce satisfactory images.
  • A child-friendly environment and atmosphere helps minimise the need for sedation.
  • Parental presence, distraction with the involvement of play specialists, or in-built entertainment systems can improve compliance.
  • When voluntary co-operation is not practical, sedation or GA should be provided to the same standards of care as would prevail in an operating theatre.
  • In a child who will not be able to co-operate, consider whether the scan can reasonably be deferred until the child is old enough to co-operate.
  • Small infants (< 5 kg) requiring anaesthesia are best managed by intubation and ventilation; older children may be managed with spontaneous ventilation via LMA.
19 Intraoperative MRI (iMRI)

Two common arrangements

LayoutImplications
Scanner within the theatre suite Strict safety procedures must restrict personnel and equipment allowed within the MR environment. All staff present in theatre should have undertaken safety training, and policies on movement of equipment and the patient must be adhered to
Scanner and theatre in adjacent rooms (two-room) MR Unsafe equipment and monitoring can be used, but must be changed before scanning. Reduces some risks as access to the MR environment can be controlled by the MR radiographer — in most cases only the anaesthetist and anaesthetic assistant, familiar with the MR field, accompany the patient into the scanning room

Practical points

  • The patient is moved into the scanner via the operating table itself, or on a transfer trolley that links to the MR table.
  • All transfers increase risk — take great care that there are no trailing wires, IV lines or ventilator circuits that may become caught or dislodged. All monitoring must be maintained.
  • Liver ablation and focused ultrasound cases are carried out entirely within the MR scanner with minimal patient movement, but all equipment used must be MR Safe.
  • Prone position: access to the tracheal tube is severely limited by the lower coil.
  • Pay particular attention to securing the airway and patient positioning. Check and document the condition of pressure areas pre- and postoperatively.
  • Anaesthesia may be very prolonged — each scan adds approximately 45 minutes to the case, plus extra time to redrape the operative field to maintain sterility.
  • Allow staff sufficient breaks to prevent fatigue and loss of concentration.
  • For lesions in eloquent areas requiring cortical and subcortical mapping, intraoperative scanning is usually undertaken with the patient awake — the anaesthetist must stay in the MR suite during scanning.
  • There may be several hours between induction and the first intraoperative scan — it is easy to overlook metal items left next to the patient or incompatible monitoring leads.
  • Robust checks at critical times: before anaesthetic induction, before draping for surgery, and before scanning. The MR safety and theatre WHO checklists have different functions — both should be undertaken with the relevant teams.
  • Each hospital should develop individual operating guidelines. When setting up a service, use a small team of key personnel who can quickly build expertise; once a safe routine is established, embed SOPs within the wider department.
20 Self-test — true or false?

Magnetic fields

  • MRI scanners generate magnetic fields between 0.2 and 3.0 Tesla. TRUE
  • 1 Tesla is equivalent to 10 000 Gauss. TRUE
  • The Earth's magnetic field is approximately 0.5 – 1 Gauss. TRUE
  • MRI superconductor coils are immersed in liquid oxygen to keep them below 4.2 K. FALSE — liquid helium.
  • The magnetic field strength stays the same as you move away from the scanner. FALSE — it falls away exponentially.

Magnetic resonance imaging

  • The T-weighting refers to the exact dosage of contrast agent given. FALSE — it refers to the relaxation time constant of the atom being studied.
  • In T1-weighted images, fluid structures appear darker. TRUE
  • In T2-weighted images, fluid structures appear white. TRUE
  • The negative charge of the hydrogen atom in water is used to generate MR images. FALSE — hydrogen has a positive charge.
  • Gd-DTPA commonly causes anaphylaxis. FALSE — the risk is very low (up to 0.01 %).

Safety concerns in the MR examination room

  • Patient injury from metal projectiles. TRUE
  • Hypothermia due to the cold environment. FALSE — hypothermia is uncommon; MRI may increase patient temperature.
  • Burn wounds related to equipment cables. TRUE
  • Anaphylaxis from IV contrast agent. FALSE — uncommon with Gd-DTPA.
  • Hearing damage due to high noise levels. TRUE

Management of patients for MR imaging

  • In cardiac arrest, the magnet should be switched off for safe resuscitation. FALSE — move the patient to the induction area. Switching off causes quenching and puts the resuscitation team at risk.
  • Patients with neurovascular aneurysm clips should never have MRI scans. TRUE
  • Cardiac pacemakers are at significant risk of failure within the 5 Gauss line. TRUE
  • Patients with angina should keep their GTN patches on during anaesthesia for MRI. FALSE — GTN patches should be removed before MRI scanning (they may contain metal).
  • ICU patients should not have MRI if their tracheal tube contains a metal spring in the pilot balloon. FALSE — the spring may be used, but must be taped away from the area being studied. Armoured ETTs with a metal coil down their length should not be used.
