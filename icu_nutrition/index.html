🧱 ICU Nutrition Quick Reference

  • Critical illness progresses through 3 metabolic phases: early acute, late acute, and recovery.
  • Indirect calorimetry (IC) is the gold standard for assessing energy needs. Alternatives: predictive equations and weight-based methods.
  • Malnutrition assessment: guided by clinical assessment plus nutritional scoring systems.
  • Oral nutrition should be prioritised over assisted enteral or parenteral nutrition whenever possible.
  • Nutritional therapy should be started at a low dose of calories and protein and progressively increased to full nutrition within 3–7 days.
  • Nutrition is no longer just support — it is therapy in the critically ill.
  • Early full feeding (any route) is harmful in a dose-dependent manner — it suppresses recovery pathways (autophagy, ketogenesis).

Early acute Day 1–2 ICU Initial stress response: ↑ metabolic rate, hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance — but energy expenditure is decreased. Avoid full feeding. Start low-dose / progressive nutrition.
Late acute Day 3–7 ICU Hypercatabolic: sustained ↑ metabolic rate, muscle protein breakdown, ongoing inflammation → ↑ energy expenditure. Progressively build to target energy & protein.
Recovery > 7 days to months Anabolic: tissue repair, restoration of normal metabolic function. Full nutrition, rehabilitation, physical activity.

Note: phase time periods are arbitrary — there is currently no validated biomarker or metabolic monitor that marks the transition from one phase to the next.

Who is at risk?

  • Every patient staying > 48 h in ICU should be considered at risk of malnutrition (ESPEN Statement 1, strong consensus).
  • Prevalence of malnutrition in ICU: 38–78%.
  • Malnutrition is a substantial predictor of: longer hospital stay, longer mechanical ventilation, higher infection rates, higher mortality.
  • Perform nutritional risk screening within 24 h of ICU admission to differentiate high- vs low-risk malnutrition.

Screening tools

NRS 2002 mNUTRIC / NUTRIC MUST SGA MNA MST
  • NRS 2002 and NUTRIC were designed for critically ill patients and are superior for predicting clinical outcomes — they pay special attention to nutritional status and disease severity.
  • NRS 2002 ≥ 5 = high nutritional risk.
  • No ICU-specific nutritional score has been fully validated — a general clinical assessment is recommended until one is available (ESPEN R2).

Clinical assessment should include

  • Detailed medical history, unintentional weight loss, decrease in physical performance before ICU admission.
  • Physical examination, body composition, muscle mass and strength (ultrasound, CT, BIA, handgrip dynamometry).
  • Weight and BMI alone do not accurately reflect malnutrition — detect loss of muscle / sarcopenia, including in obese patients.

Markers of severe malnutrition / high risk

BMI ≤ 18.5 kg/m² > 5% weight loss in last month Reduced general condition

Pragmatic "at risk" triggers (ESPEN)

  • ICU stay > 48 h, or mechanical ventilation, or infection.
  • Underfed for > 5 days before ICU admission.
  • Severe chronic disease present.

Hierarchy of methods

1st choice — Gold standardIndirect calorimetry (IC) — measures VO₂ and VCO₂ via a ventilatory circuit; estimates REE and total energy expenditure.
▼
2nd choice — if IC unavailableVCO₂ from the ventilator (or VO₂ from a pulmonary artery catheter) — better than predictive equations.
▼
3rd choiceWeight-based formula: 20–25 kcal/kg/d in the acute phase → 25–30 kcal/kg/d in recovery.
▼
4th choicePredictive equations: Penn State, Mifflin-St Jeor, Ireton-Jones, revised Harris-Benedict. Error margin up to 60%.

Equations

  • Weir equation (with urinary nitrogen):
    REE (kcal/d) = 1.44 × [(VO₂ mL/min × 3.94) + (VCO₂ mL/min × 1.11)] − 2.17 × (UN)
  • Modified Weir (no IC, RQ assumed 0.86):
    Energy expenditure (kcal/d) = VCO₂ (mL/min) × 8.19
  • Harris-Benedict (revised), needs activity + stress factors:
    Daily Calorie Requirement = REE × Activity Level × Stress Factor

IC — limitations

  • High oxygen requirements, nitrous oxide use, haemodynamic instability, ventilator adjustments → unreliable results.
  • Fick method requires a pulmonary artery catheter → limits applicability.

Evidence snapshot

  • IC-guided medical nutrition therapy: favourable trend for short-term mortality (RR 1.28, 95% CI 0.98–1.67, p = 0.07) — no significant difference in long-term mortality, infection rates or length of stay.
  • IC may be most valuable after the acute phase, when considerable metabolic changes occur.
  • If predictive equations are used → prefer hypocaloric nutrition (< 70% of estimated needs) during the first week of ICU stay.
  • If IC is used → isocaloric nutrition can be progressively implemented after the early acute phase; after day 3 increase to 80–100% of measured EE.

Immunonutrition (INT)

  • Glutamine (GLN)
    • Burns > 20% BSA: enteral GLN 0.3–0.5 g/kg/d for 10–15 days, started as soon as EN begins.
    • Trauma: enteral GLN 0.2–0.3 g/kg/d for the first 5 days; extend to 10–15 days for complicated wound healing.
    • Not for other ICU patients. Parenteral GLN-dipeptide must not be given in unstable/complex patients, especially liver and renal failure.
  • Omega-3 fatty acids
    • EN enriched with omega-3 within nutritional doses may be administered (ARDS, acute lung injury, sepsis — improved LOS, ventilator days, mortality in some studies).
    • High-dose omega-3 enriched enteral formulas should NOT be given routinely or as bolus doses.
    • Parenteral lipid emulsions enriched with EPA + DHA (fish oil 0.1–0.2 g/kg/d) can be provided in patients receiving PN.
  • Arginine — potential benefit, but caution in severe sepsis and septic shock (↑ nitric oxide → exacerbates hypotension).
  • Antioxidants (vitamins C, D, E; zinc, copper, selenium) — important against oxidative stress, but high-dose mono- or combination therapy is not recommended without proven deficiency.
  • Routine use of INT in the general critically ill population is not recommended — consider on a case-by-case basis with monitoring of nutritional status and immune function.

Enteral access

  • Gastric access is the standard approach to initiate EN.
  • Continuous rather than bolus EN should be used (18–24 h infusion). If on insulin, 24-h continuous infusion is ideal for glycaemic control.
  • ESPEN: establish a gastrostomy or jejunostomy if enteral feeding is anticipated for > 3–4 weeks.
  • Postpyloric (mainly jejunal) feeding — if high risk of aspiration, or gastric feeding intolerance not solved by prokinetics. Requires expertise; less physiological; may be harmful with motility problems distal to the stomach.

Parenteral access

  • PPN (peripheral): temporary supplement until EN feasible, or rapid intervention when central access unavailable. ↓ central line risks, fewer delays, lower cost. Drawbacks: ↑ thrombophlebitis, limited nutrient complement, not suitable beyond 4–7 days, needs lower osmolality.
  • CPN (central): short-term non-tunnelled catheters (in-hospital); medium-term PICC / Hohn catheters (up to 3 months, in or out of hospital); long-term/home PN > 3 months → tunnelled cuffed central catheters or implanted ports.
Step 1 — ICU admission Perform nutritional risk screening within 24 h (NRS 2002 or mNUTRIC) + determine malnutrition severity by clinical evaluation.
Regard all ICU patients staying > 48 h as potentially at risk for malnutrition.
▼
Step 2 — Risk scoring Low risk → can the patient take orally?
High risk → early progressive medical nutrition therapy (EN/PN) if oral intake is not feasible and there are no contraindications.
▼
Step 3 — Feeding mode
  • Low risk + can eat → oral route preferred over EN/PN.
  • Low risk + cannot eat → consider progressive EN within 48 h if no contraindications; if contraindications exist, delay nutrition.
  • High risk → EN preferred over PN; determine energy requirements by IC → ventilator VCO₂ → weight-based → predictive equations.
▼
Safety rule To avoid overfeeding, early full EN and PN must not be used — prescribe progressively within 3 to 7 days.

Delay EN until controlled

  • Uncontrolled shock — low-dose EN can start once shock is controlled with fluids and vasopressors/inotropes, while remaining vigilant for bowel ischaemia.
  • Uncontrolled life-threatening hypoxaemia, hypercapnia or acidosis — EN can start with stable hypoxaemia and compensated/permissive hypercapnia and acidosis.
  • Active upper GI bleeding — start EN once bleeding has stopped and there are no signs of re-bleeding.
  • Overt bowel ischaemia.
  • High-output intestinal fistula — if reliable feeding access distal to the fistula is not achievable.
  • Abdominal compartment syndrome.
  • Gastric aspirate volume > 500 mL / 6 h.

Low-dose EN should be administered in

  • Therapeutic hypothermia — increase the dose after rewarming.
  • Intra-abdominal hypertension without abdominal compartment syndrome — consider temporary reduction or discontinuation of EN if intra-abdominal pressure rises further under EN.
  • Acute liver failure — once immediately life-threatening metabolic derangements are controlled (with or without liver support), independent of grade of encephalopathy.

PN rules

  • In case of contraindications to oral and EN, PN should be implemented within 3–7 days.
  • PN should not be started until all reasonable strategies to improve EN tolerance have been attempted.
  • In patients not tolerating full-dose EN during the first ICU week, weigh safety and benefits of supplemental PN case-by-case.
  • Early and progressive PN may replace no nutrition in severely malnourished patients with contraindications to EN — balance against overfeeding and refeeding risk.

Non-intubated patient

  • If not reaching the energy target with an oral diet → oral nutritional supplements first, then EN.
  • Dysphagia → texture-adapted food can be considered.
  • Swallowing proven unsafe → EN.
  • Very high aspiration risk → postpyloric EN, or if not possible, temporary PN during swallowing training with the nasoenteral tube removed.

Surgical patient

  • After abdominal or oesophageal surgery → early EN preferred over delayed EN.
  • Critically ill with surgical complications and unable to eat → EN rather than PN, unless GI discontinuity/obstruction or abdominal compartment syndrome.
  • Unrepaired anastomotic leak / internal or external fistula → aim for feeding access distal to the defect.
  • If distal access not achieved → withhold EN and commence PN.
  • High-output stoma or fistula → evaluate chyme reinfusion or enteroclysis.

Trauma patient

  • Preferentially receive early EN instead of early PN.
  • Early EN associated with decreased length of stay and a trend to decreased mortality.
  • Protein intake reaching 1.5–2 g/kg/d may be considered (large protein losses, e.g. 20–30 g/L of abdominal fluid).

Septic patient

  • Early and progressive EN after haemodynamic stabilisation.
  • If contraindicated → replace or supplement with progressive PN.
  • Septic shock: EN should be started after successful resuscitation; begin 20–50% of full support to "open" the enteral route, then increase according to GI tolerance.
  • Not receiving nutrition support is deleterious — increased calories and protein are associated with ↓ 60-day mortality and ↑ ventilation-free days.

Obese patient

  • Iso-caloric high-protein diet, preferentially guided by indirect calorimetry and urinary nitrogen losses.
  • If IC unavailable → base energy on adjusted body weight.
  • If nitrogen losses / lean body mass unavailable → protein 1.3 g/kg ABW/d (some advocate 2–2.5 g/kg ideal BW/d).
  • Watch for decreased glucose tolerance, altered lipid metabolism, micronutrient deficiency, decreased gut motility.

Glycaemic control

  • Measure blood glucose initially (after ICU admission or after starting artificial nutrition) and at least every 4 h for the first 2 days.
  • Administer insulin when glucose exceeds 10 mmol/L (≈180 mg/dL).
  • Severe hyperglycaemia (> 180 mg/dL / 10 mmol/L), marked glycaemic variability (CV > 20%) and mild hypoglycaemia (< 70 mg/dL / 3.9 mmol/L) are all associated with increased mortality.
  • Use IV continuous insulin with an algorithm when on ongoing EN/PN.

Enteral feeding intolerance

  • Features: abdominal distension, gastric overfilling, vomiting, diet regurgitation, bowel distension (up to Ogilvie's syndrome), intestinal ischaemia, diarrhoea. Do not limit the definition to gastric function alone.
  • Erythromycin IV is first-line prokinetic (usually 100–250 mg three times daily) — significant benefit for feeding tolerance; use for 2–4 days and discontinue after 3 days (effectiveness falls to one third after 72 h).
  • Metoclopramide IV (10 mg two to three times daily) or a combination of metoclopramide + erythromycin is an alternative.
  • If a large GRV (> 500 mL) persists, consider postpyloric feeding rather than withholding EN — unless a new abdominal complication is suspected.
  • High aspiration risk: feed in a 35°–40° head-up position; consider prokinetics; consider jejunal (postpyloric) tube.

Refeeding syndrome

  • Measure electrolytes (potassium, magnesium, phosphate) at least once daily for the first week.
  • If refeeding hypophosphataemia (< 0.65 mmol/L or a drop of > 0.16 mmol/L): measure electrolytes 2–3 times a day and supplement if needed.
  • Restrict energy supply for 48 h, then gradually increase.
  • Protocolised caloric restriction for 48 h in patients developing hypophosphataemia improved survival in an RCT — slow progression over the first 72 h facilitates electrolyte control.
  • Phosphate is measured less commonly than potassium — undetected severe hypophosphataemia can be fatal after feeding initiation.

Other monitoring

  • Monitor for overfeeding and underfeeding; aim between 70% and 100% of measured EE (both under- and over-feeding are deleterious).
  • Triglycerides and liver function tests may guide lipid tolerance.
  • Physical activity may improve the beneficial effects of nutritional therapy (ESPEN Statement 3).
