AATS Consensus Guidelines for the Management of Empyema

Quick reference for anaesthetists · Collapsible · Searchable

Shen KR, Bribriesco A, Crabtree T, Denlinger C, Eby J, Eiken P, Jones DR, Keshavjee S, Maldonado F, Paul S, Kozower B. The American Association for Thoracic Surgery consensus guidelines for the management of empyema. J Thorac Cardiovasc Surg. 2017;153(6):e129–e146. doi:10.1016/j.jtcvs.2017.01.030

Empyema thoracis — from the Greek, "pus in the chest." The most common precursor is bacterial pneumonia with a subsequent parapneumonic effusion.

Epidemiology

  • ~1 million patients hospitalised in the USA each year with pneumonia.
  • 20–40% have a parapneumonic effusion.
  • 5–10% of these progress to empyema (~32,000 patients/year).
  • ~15% of empyema patients die; ~30% require surgical drainage of the pleural space.
  • Incidence is increasing in the USA since the 1990s.

How the guideline was made: AATS Guidelines Committee appointed co-chairs (Shen, Kozower) who assembled a multidisciplinary group (5 thoracic surgeons, 1 interventional radiologist, 1 infectious disease specialist, 1 interventional pulmonologist). Three teleconferences plus a 1-day face-to-face meeting. Each recommendation required >75% approval. IOM 2011 standards followed, without patient engagement.
Class ILOE B Investigate for pleural effusion in all patients presenting with signs and symptoms of pneumonia or unexplained sepsis.
Class ILOE B Failure of community- or healthcare-associated pneumonia to respond clinically to appropriate antibiotic therapy should prompt investigations to identify a pleural effusion.

Reasoning

  • Pleural fluid analysis is mandatory — complicated parapneumonic effusions do not differ from simple effusions on the basis of clinical presentation alone.
  • In a large randomised trial, median duration of symptoms before presentation was 2 weeks.
  • Anaerobic pleural space infections are more indolent: loss of appetite, substantial weight loss; more likely with poor dental hygiene and aspiration risk.
  • Failure to respond to appropriate antibiotics within a few days should suggest progression to pleural space infection.

Risk factors for progression to complicated effusion / empyema

  • History of alcohol or intravenous drug use
  • Hypoalbuminaemia < 30 g/dL
  • Hyponatraemia < 130 mmol/L
  • Elevated C-reactive protein > 100 mg/L
Anaesthetic relevance: These patients often present late, septic, malnourished, and with aspiration risk. Anticipate a full stomach, difficult venous access, and haemodynamic instability on induction.
Class ILOE B Pleural ultrasound (US) should be performed routinely in addition to conventional chest x-ray (CXR) in the evaluation of pleural space infection — both for diagnosis and for image-guidance of pleural interventions.
Class IIaLOE B CT chest should be obtained when pleural space infection is suspected.

Chest x-ray

  • Minimum 175 mL of pleural fluid needed to blunt the costophrenic angle on a PA film.
  • Smaller, loculated effusions may appear as lenticular pleural-based opacities — hard to distinguish from consolidation.
  • Loculated, radiologically complex effusions often do not layer dependently and may be missed.
  • In 61 patients with CT-proven parapneumonic effusions, AP, PA and lateral CXRs each missed ~10% — usually due to coexisting lower-lobe consolidation.
  • Useful as a first step, but must be combined with additional imaging.

Pleural ultrasound

  • Now the cornerstone of imaging for pleural effusions.
  • At least as effective as lateral decubitus CXR for small effusions; better at estimating volume; not hindered by adjacent consolidation.
  • Bedside availability — can be performed competently by non-radiologists; ideal for guiding safe diagnostic thoracentesis.
  • Rapid evaluation in ED or ICU.
  • Patterns (320-patient study):

Increased septations on thoracic US may be predictive of clinical outcome.

Computed tomography

  • Contrast-enhanced CT with tissue phase is valuable; allows detailed assessment of parenchymal abnormalities and may reveal the cause (bronchogenic carcinoma, endobronchial foreign body, oesophageal rupture).
  • Loculated empyemas are typically lenticular with compression of adjacent lung — generally distinguishable from pleural-based lung abscess.

  • Pleural fluid thickness cutoff of 2–2.5 cm suggested to guide thoracentesis — smaller effusions on CT likely resolve with antibiotics alone.

PET and MRI

  • PET — does not appear useful; does not accurately distinguish inflammatory from malignant pleural effusions.
  • MRI — may become an attractive alternative to CT; useful for distinguishing transudate from exudate and assessing extension into soft tissues (chest wall, spinal involvement).
Class ILOE B Pus, positive Gram's stain, or positive culture in pleural fluid establishes the diagnosis of empyema → treat with tube thoracostomy followed by surgical intervention when appropriate.
Class ILOE B Pleural pH < 7.2 in suspected pleural space infection predicts a complicated clinical course → tube thoracostomy followed by surgical intervention when appropriate.
Class IIaLOE C Pleural fluid LDH > 1000 IU/L, glucose < 40 mg/dL, or a loculated effusion suggests the effusion is unlikely to resolve with antibiotics alone.
Class ILOE B Obtain pleural fluid cultures during aspiration or drainage procedures — not from previously inserted tubes or drains. Inoculate freshly drained fluid into aerobic and anaerobic blood culture vials in addition to sterile containers for Gram stain and culture.

Thoracentesis — technique & sample processing

  • Diagnostic thoracentesis is indicated for all suspected parapneumonic effusions when pleural fluid thickness is > 1 cm on CXR and > 2 cm on CT.
  • Loculated and large effusions are likely to have a complicated course irrespective of fluid analysis.
  • Fluid analysis may differ substantially from one locule to another — limits its value in loculated disease.
  • Perform with US guidance to reduce pneumothorax risk.
  • Frankly purulent appearance → no need for pH analysis.
  • pH must be measured with an arterial blood gas analyser within 1 hour of sampling.
Pre-analytical pitfalls for pleural pH:
• Residual lidocaine → falsely low pH
• Heparin → falsely low pH
• Air in the syringe → falsely high pH

Biomarkers — key thresholds

MarkerThresholdAction
Pleural pH< 7.2Best next predictor of complicated course if no pus/organism → chest tube drainage

Rare pitfall: Urease-splitting organisms (e.g. Proteus species) may cause a spuriously elevated pleural pH.

Pleural fluid culture

  • Obtain in all cases of acute bacterial empyema.
  • 44% of cultures from previously placed catheters or tubing are inaccurate.
  • Inoculation of BACTEC blood culture bottles (by trained personnel) improves yield when combined with standard laboratory culture.
  • Do not use swabs.
  • If only a small amount of fluid is available, inoculate only into the standard specimen culture container.
  • Always send cell count and chemistries alongside cultures.

General laboratory studies

  • Blood cultures are positive in only a minority of patients with pleural space infection.

Appropriate empiric therapy incorporates: (1) patient's clinical history, (2) local antimicrobial resistance patterns, (3) institutional antibiotic stewardship, (4) pharmacologic characteristics of the antibiotics.

Class IIaLOE C Community-acquired empyema: parenteral 2nd- or 3rd-generation cephalosporin (e.g. ceftriaxone) with metronidazole, OR parenteral aminopenicillin with β-lactamase inhibitor (e.g. ampicillin/sulbactam).
Class IIaLOE C Hospital-acquired or post-procedural empyema: include cover for MRSA and Pseudomonas aeruginosa — e.g. vancomycin + cefepime + metronidazole, OR vancomycin + piperacillin/tazobactam (dosed for activity against P. aeruginosa).
Class ILOE B Avoid aminoglycosides in the management of empyema — they are inactivated in empyema fluid.
Class IIaLOE C No role for intrapleural administration of antibiotics.
Class IIbLOE C Duration of antibiotic therapy is influenced by the organism, adequacy of source control, and clinical response.

Reasoning & practical points

  • Low MRSA / highly resistant Gram-negative risk → 2nd-gen or non-pseudomonal 3rd-gen cephalosporin, or aminopenicillin + β-lactamase inhibitor covers the most commonly identified organisms.
  • Treat anaerobes empirically. Ampicillin/sulbactam covers a range of anaerobes; ceftriaxone requires addition of metronidazole. Clindamycin is a suitable alternative for most upper GI and respiratory anaerobic infections.
  • Even with a positive monomicrobial aerobic culture, it is often reasonable to continue anaerobic cover — anaerobes are frequently present and inconsistently cultured.
  • Empiric cover for atypical organisms is generally not necessary.
  • Vancomycin + meropenem may be indicated if there is history/suspicion of ESBL-producing organisms.
  • In slowly progressing or chronic empyema, empiric therapy can be held until culture results allow directed therapy. Infectious diseases consultation recommended for chronic empyema.

Duration

  • No comparative trials. Literature reports 2–6 weeks.
  • Working Group recommends a minimum of 2 weeks from the time of drainage and defervescence.
  • Final duration determined by organism sensitivities, adequacy of drainage, and response on follow-up.
  • Transition to oral therapy if: patient responding, source controlled, organism susceptible to orally bioavailable agent, and patient tolerating oral intake.
Anaesthetic / perioperative relevance: Check renal function before vancomycin + piperacillin/tazobactam (nephrotoxicity risk). Vancomycin infusion reactions (flushing, hypotension) can mimic anaphylaxis under anaesthesia — infuse over ≥60 min. Avoid aminoglycosides entirely — poor pleural penetration and inactivation in purulent fluid.

Thoracentesis

Class III — No benefitLOE C Thoracentesis without pleural drain placement is NOT recommended for the treatment of parapneumonic effusion or empyema.

Thoracentesis is useful for diagnosis of uncomplicated effusion and is a recommended diagnostic step. However, in known pleural infection, ongoing drainage is required for adequate treatment.

Image-guided drain placement

Class ILOE B Image-guided pleural drain placement is useful in the treatment of early-stage, minimally septated empyema.
Class IIaLOE C In septated effusions, small-bore catheters are recommended in patients who are not surgical candidates.
Class ILOE B Routine drain flushing is recommended to prevent occlusion.
Class ILOE C Tube thoracostomy should be combined with close CT follow-up to confirm adequacy of drainage. Persistence of any undrained fluid should prompt additional drains or more aggressive management.

Reasoning

  • Drainage of infected material is a fundamental part of treatment.
  • No consensus on ideal drain size. Small drains placed with imaging guidance report ~80% success — favourable vs earlier large-bore studies.
  • 405-patient study: equivalent success with tubes ≤14 F vs >14 F — but tube size was not randomised; more complicated effusions/frank pus likely received larger drains.
  • Small-bore tubes are an accepted alternative as first-line therapy in patients amenable to tube thoracostomy alone.
  • Expert opinion holds small-bore catheters are ineffective for thick pus or extensively septated effusions — but data are mixed.

Septation & success of small-bore drainage

US findingSuccess of US-guided small-bore drainage
Anechoic (no septation)92%
Extensive septation63%

Another study found imaging appearance of empyema was not predictive of tube drainage success/failure. A case series described successful use of small-bore tunneled catheters in 2 patients with chronic complicated empyema who were not surgical candidates.

Causes of drain failure

  • Occlusion — more common in empyema than in uncomplicated effusion or pneumothorax. Reported blockage rates 11–30%; one large study showed 64% in its empyema subgroup.
  • Dislodgement — common. CXR is insensitive for malpositioned tubes. CT is superior to CXR in identifying drain dislodgement or undrained pleural fluid.
Flushing protocol: Ideal frequency/volume not well established, but one study showed 20 mL sterile saline every 6 hours reduced occlusion rate.

Any undrained loculations should be pursued aggressively with additional or larger drains. Failure to achieve complete pleural drainage or failure of clinical improvement should prompt more aggressive management — lytic therapy or surgery.

Class IIaLOE A Intrapleural fibrinolytics should NOT be used routinely for complicated pleural effusions and early empyemas.

Reasoning

  • Used for empyema and complicated pleural effusions for 65 years; biologically plausible before an established pleural rind forms.
  • Systematic review (PRISMA) identified 8 prospective, placebo-controlled trials since 1997.

Trial outcomes

  • MIST1: streptokinase increased the amount of pleural fluid drainage, but was not associated with reduced mortality, frequency of surgery, or length of hospital stay.
  • MIST2: combination of intrapleural tPA + DNase produced statistically and clinically significant improvement in pleural drainage and reduction in hospital stay. There was a 75% reduction in the need for surgical intervention at 3 months.
  • 2012 meta-analysis: fibrinolytic therapy is potentially beneficial in adults, but there is insufficient evidence to support routine use for all patients. Patient and treatment heterogeneity confound results.
  • British Thoracic Society 2010: no indication for routine intrapleural fibrinolytics — but that recommendation predates MIST2.
Interpretation caveat: The primary endpoint of most studies was reduction of pleural fluid on CXR after fibrinolytic administration — not long-term outcomes such as pulmonary reserve or time to return to work. Under the care of a dedicated thoracic surgeon, thoracoscopic or open intervention remains the gold standard for complicated pleural effusions/empyemas.
Class IIaLOE B Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) should be the first-line approach in all patients with stage II acute empyema.

Goals of operative intervention

  • (1) Complete evacuation of potentially infected fluid and/or material.
  • (2) Complete re-expansion of the lung.

Technical considerations with either approach

  • Safe access into the chest.
  • Drainage of pleural space.
  • Manoeuvres to allow full expansion of lung (e.g. release of inferior pulmonary ligament) resulting in obliteration of dead space in the hemithorax.
  • By definition, stage II empyema does not have an organised pleural peel, so formal decortication is not necessary.
  • In clinical practice, patients rarely present with a "pure" stage II empyema — rather a "mixed stage" with areas of fibrinous organisation on the pleural surface.
Definition of VATS in this guideline: The distinguishing feature is the absence of rib spreading, with avoidance of retractors or instruments that place pressure on the underlying intercostal neurovascular bundle. The number or size of incisions used for port access is less important than rib distraction.

Potential benefits of VATS over open thoracotomy

  • Improved postoperative pain control.
  • Shorter length of stay.
  • Less blood loss.
  • Less respiratory compromise.
  • Reduction in postoperative complications including 30-day mortality.
  • Lower cost compared with open thoracotomy (although not studied specifically in empyema, VATS has shown significant cost savings for pulmonary wedge resections and lobectomies in 3 large retrospective database analyses).

Potential harm of VATS over open thoracotomy

  • Little potential harm as an initial intervention given the overall safety profile of diagnostic thoracoscopy and the relative ease of conversion to open thoracotomy if needed.
  • Potential contraindications: inability to tolerate one-lung ventilation and severe coagulopathy.
  • Potential drawbacks: increased operative time, increased cost, steeper learning curve, incomplete therapy requiring additional procedures. (Many studies predate advances in technology, training and familiarity.)

Summary of relevant evidence

Quality: Retrospective, cohort (LOE B) · Quantity: 10+ studies · Consistency: Satisfactory

  • Chambers et al. reviewed 14 studies. Majority were single-institution retrospective cohort studies, n = 48 to 420; most groups mixed stage II and III empyema.
  • Outcomes consistently demonstrated that VATS offers superior clinical outcomes for treatment of empyema, with decreased length of stay, less pain, and less overall morbidity.
  • Limitations: stage of empyema varied among and within studies; variance in description of VATS drainage vs decortication; inherent selection bias of individual surgeons favouring open vs VATS; "pre-procedure probability" of conversion influences the decision to attempt VATS and the thoracoscopic time tolerated before opening.
  • Tong et al.: comparing patients requiring conversion to open vs successful VATS, there were no significant differences in preoperative factors such as previous cardiac or general thoracic procedures.
  • Shahin et al.: demonstrated a departmental learning curve over 3 years after introducing VATS; with favourable results they consider VATS first choice not just for acute/early empyema but also advanced disease.

Expert clinical opinion — when to convert to open thoracotomy

Universal indications for immediate conversion:

  • Uncontrollable bleeding.
  • Injury to structures not amenable to thoracoscopic repair.
  • Acute intolerance of single-lung ventilation.

Additional empyema-specific factors prompting consideration of conversion:

  • Lack of surgical progression.
  • Failure to ultimately achieve the 2 goals of empyema therapy (evacuation and expansion).

Published conversion rates range from 0% to 41%; the largest study (Duke) reported 11.4%. No studies define a specific time limit after which surgeons should abort VATS in favour of thoracotomy — most authors are subjective, stating thoracotomy should be performed if VATS is not successful "in a timely fashion."

Anaesthetist's translation: Expect one-lung ventilation, lateral decubitus positioning with the empyema side up, and a real possibility of conversion to open thoracotomy. Have a double-lumen tube or bronchial blocker plan, check tube position with fibreoptic bronchoscopy, and prepare for possible major blood loss and prolonged single-lung ventilation.

Overview — stage III

  • The turbid thick fluid of stage II begins to organise and form granulation tissue.
  • Granulation tissue + underlying inflammation → formation of a peel compressing the visceral pleural surface.
  • Continuation → contraction of the hemithorax with rib space narrowing and mediastinal shift to the affected side.
  • Pleural drainage procedures are no longer effective at this stage.
  • Effective surgical treatment = removal of purulent collections + debridement of infected tissue + obliteration of the empyema space to prevent recurrence.
  • Choice of initial approach depends on the condition of the patient, pleural space, and underlying lung parenchyma.

Decortication

Class IIaLOE B Decortication is reasonable in patients with chronic empyemas who are medically operable to tolerate major thoracic surgery.
Class IIbLOE C No compelling evidence that epidural catheters cannot be used safely in patients with chronic empyema if they are otherwise low risk for epidural abscess.

Technique: peeling off the granulation tissue and/or fibrous peel overlying the visceral (and at times parietal) pleura to allow expansion, plus debridement of infected tissue.

  • Frankly necrotic lung parenchyma can be resected if warranted — e.g. patients with a frank abscess serving as a source of sepsis, or other symptomatology such as haemoptysis.
  • Extensive resections should be avoided as much as possible — bronchial stumps in infected fields are prone to fistulise.
  • If bronchopleural fistulas (BPFs) are present, close them if possible.
  • Where lung is resected or fistulas are present, muscle flaps may be appropriate for closing the fistula and/or filling space from incomplete expansion or needed parenchymal resection.
  • Limited studies have examined the functional impact of decortication on lung function and performance status.

Epidural anaesthesia for postoperative pain control

Benefits: It is well accepted that an epidural catheter for central analgesia improves pain and postoperative function after thoracic surgery.
Concern: Epidural catheters in empyema patients have generally been avoided because of the risk of epidural abscess. The 3 main risk factors for epidural abscess of any aetiology are:
1. Compromised immunity
2. Physical disruption of the spinal column
3. A definitive source of infection

To date, no studies have causally linked epidural abscess to placement of an epidural catheter in the presence of empyema.

Rate of epidural abscess (all populations): from 1.1 in 100,000 to as frequent as 1 in 1,930 patients. Rare but severe consequences — septic shock and paralysis. Studies specifically examining incidence/risk in empyema are difficult to perform given the low overall incidence and understandable reluctance to attempt catheter insertion in pleural space infection.

Space filling of chronic empyema cavities

Class IIaLOE C Tissue flaps — pedicled muscle flaps or omentum — can be useful to fill empyema cavities where space is created by incomplete lung expansion, or to close a BPF.
Class IIbLOE C Thoracoplasty with resection of ribs may be considered in select cases to obliterate the infected pleural space where previous measures (muscle flaps, open window) have failed.

Reasoning:

  • Incomplete re-expansion of the lung or required resection for necrotic parenchyma may require tissue flaps (omentum or muscle) to prevent reaccumulation of infected pleural fluid. Muscle flaps have also been used to help close BPFs.
  • Any extrathoracic muscle can be used; common pedicled flaps: serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major. Lower abdominal muscle groups can be used as complex flaps but should be limited to when chest wall musculature is unavailable. Omental flaps also can be used — potential advantage of inherent wound healing properties, but omental harvest requires abdominal entry, which could lead to another infected cavity.
  • Muscle flaps are best applied in relatively clean wounds devoid of frank purulence. They may be placed at the first operation or reserved after debridement with fresh granulation tissue via open thoracic window techniques.
  • Adjunctive measures to decrease the size of the thoracic cavity: pleural tenting and thoracoplasty. Experience with pleural tenting in non-mycobacterial empyema is limited.
  • Schede thoracoplasty and its modern versions are morbid and disfiguring; reserved for extreme cases where previous muscle flaps or open thoracic window techniques have failed.

Prolonged treatment measures

Class IIaLOE C Open thoracic window with marsupialisation of the infected thoracic cavity, resection of several ribs, and dressing changes — reasonable for patients with chronic empyema who are medically unfit to tolerate decortication and tissue flap placement, or those with chronic empyema with a BPF.
Class IIbLOE C Wound VAC may be a reasonable alternative to daily dressing changes to debride chronic pleural cavities being treated with an open thoracic window. Exercise caution when there is a BPF or visceral pleural rents leading to large air leaks.
Class IIbLOE C Empyema tube draining a chronic empyema cavity may be considered for chronic infections with a small persistently infected space or small BPF — especially in patients medically unfit to tolerate decortication and tissue flap placement.

Reasoning:

  • Patients with chronic empyema are often debilitated or have a BPF leading to uncontrolled empyema; often unable to tolerate extensive decortication ± parenchymal resection or tissue flap placement.
  • A BPF may create a grossly infected space that needs to be controlled before definitive operative repair.
  • Open thoracic window allows infectious and fistula control, either definitively or as part of a stepwise operative plan. Created by marsupialising the pleural cavity after resection of rib space. Patients then undergo dressing changes until the cavity is debrided and granulation tissue forms.
  • VAC dressings — recent single-institution studies evaluated VAC placement as an alternative to dressing changes. Placing a closed-suction sponge device into an infected cavity may provide superior debridement and promotion of granulation tissue with less patient discomfort (less frequent sponge changes).
VAC + BPF warning (key anaesthetic/critical care point): Caution when placing VACs where a BPF or rents in the visceral pleura lead to substantial air leaks. In these settings, respiratory compromise may result from suctioning significant portions of the patient's lung volume. Placement of non-adherent material to seal air leaks — such as Vaseline Petrolatum Gauze or similar — may allow VAC use in these settings. Further investigation of this technology is warranted.
  • Similar to open thoracic windows, pleural drainage catheters (chest tubes) may be left in chronic empyema cavities to drain infected fluid where lung expansion is incomplete. May be placed at the time of surgical decortication or as a first measure to drain a chronic empyema in a medically unfit patient.
  • Large infected pleural cavities may best be drained with an open thoracic window — especially if grossly infected tissue is encountered or a BPF is present, and particularly if lung expansion through decortication or tissue placement to cover the BPF is not possible.

Postpneumonectomy empyema

Class ILOE C Prompt intervention to identify or rule out a BPF and provide drainage of sepsis in patients suspected of having postpneumonectomy empyema.
Class IIaLOE C Aggressive surgical approach including antibiotics, serial debridement, closure of the BPF when present, and obliteration of the residual pleural space using vascularized tissue transposition is a reasonable strategy.

Epidemiology & mortality

  • Infection of the empty pleural space after pneumonectomy is serious and potentially fatal — reported mortality 28% to 50%.
  • Incidence: 5% after standard pneumonectomy, 10% after completion pneumonectomy.
  • BPF is found in more than 80% of cases of postpneumonectomy empyema.

Initial assessment & management

  • First priority: identify presence or absence of a BPF. A fistula should be presumed present until accurately determined by flexible bronchoscopy under local anaesthesia in a semiupright position.
  • If the patient presents acutely with features of a BPF, contralateral pneumonitis and respiratory distress: initial management begins with emergency chest tube drainage of the cavity to control infection and stop contralateral spillage.
  • Positioning: patient should preferentially be placed in the lateral decubitus position with the pneumonectomy side DOWN until drainage of the pleural space is obtained.
  • In empyema without BPF, initial management follows established rules for managing any abscess: adequate pleural drainage, appropriate parenteral antibiotics, removal of necrotic and infected tissue, obliteration of residual pleural space.
Evidence base: No randomised or prospective studies analysed the best strategy for postpneumonectomy empyema — recommendations are based on single-institution retrospective studies and expert opinion.

The Clagett procedure (and its modification)

  • Originally described by Clagett and Geraci — a 2-stage procedure: open pleural drainage → frequent wet-to-dry dressing changes → when the thorax is clean, secondary chest wall closure with obliteration of the pleural cavity using an antibiotic solution.
  • If a BPF is present, attempt at the time of debridement to locate and reclose the open bronchial stump.
  • Even if the BPF developed relatively soon after pneumonectomy, identifying the bronchial stump can be technically challenging because of rapid effacement of hilar structures.
  • Trick: filling the chest cavity with a small amount of saline and having the anaesthetist ventilate the bronchial stump can help identify a stream of air bubbles that can be followed to the bronchial stump.

Principles when reclosing the bronchus

  • Minimise trauma to the end of the bronchus.
  • Preserve blood supply all the way out to the cut end of the bronchus.
  • Carefully approximate the cut edges of the bronchus.
  • Provide adequate tissue reinforcement of the bronchial closure with vascularized tissue.

Because failure is caused most often by a persistent or recurrent fistula, the original Clagett technique has been modified to include transposition of a well-vascularized muscle flap to cover and buttress the pneumonectomy stump to prevent further ischaemia and necrosis.

Contemporary outcomes

  • Zaheer et al. (2006), n = 84: open pleural drainage, serial operative debridements, eventual chest closure after filling the pleural cavity with antibiotic solution. BPF present in 55 patients, closed in all. Vascularized muscle flap used to reinforce the bronchial stump in 60 patients (51 with BPF, 9 without). 81% healed chest wall without recurrent infection; BPF remained closed in all patients.
  • Schneiter et al. (2008), n = 75: serial open surgical debridement, temporary closure with negative pressure wound therapy, and iodine-soaked dressings. BPFs closed and reinforced with tissue when present. In 95%, the chest could be closed definitively after filling with antibiotic solution. Postpneumonectomy empyema treated successfully in 97.3%; 10 patients required a second treatment cycle. 90-day mortality 4%.

Empyema associated with BPF

Class IIaLOE C Closure of BPFs should be attempted with a combination of primary closure and buttressing with a well-vascularized transposed soft-tissue pedicle.
Class IIbLOE C Transposition of the omentum is preferred over skeletal muscle flaps or mediastinal soft tissue, and this should be attempted after the purulent fluid has been drained completely and the pleural cavity has a surface of granulation tissue.

BPF — background

  • Historically frequent after pulmonary resection (reported 28% in the era of tuberculosis surgery and less effective antibiotics).
  • More contemporaneously: 0.7% to 7.5% after pulmonary resections.
  • Risk is lower for lobectomy than pneumonectomy.
  • Reported risk factors: index operation for benign disease, right pneumonectomy, completion pneumonectomy, diminished pulmonary reserve, extended lymph node dissection.
  • No prospective studies evaluating different treatment strategies — literature limited to single-institution retrospective case series.
  • An empyema associated with a BPF is most often described after pneumonectomy; less often after lobectomy.

Closure devices

  • Bronchoscopic interventions have been attempted with variable results.
  • Jiang et al.: 58 pneumonectomy patients, 8 developed BPF. Endoscopic embolisation attempted in 5 — unsuccessful in each case.
  • Several case reports and small series describe various devices: Amplatzer vascular occlusion devices ± bioglue; cyanoacrylate-based glue, fibrin compounds, gelatin sponges, chemical cautery, endobronchial silicone spigots, submucosal injection of tissue expanders — all in small numbers of cases.

Fistula associated with necrotizing pneumonia

  • BPFs associated with thoracic infections occur in the majority of patients after anatomical surgical resections. Less frequently, fistulae occur spontaneously in the setting of necrotizing pneumonia.
  • In children, traditionally managed with prolonged chest tube duration.
  • More recently described: rotation of a single segment of the serratus anterior muscle into the chest cavity to close the fistula at the time of thoracoscopic debridement. In 20 paediatric patients, median time to resolution of fever was 2 days and postoperative length of stay was 9 days.

Management of the residual thoracic space

Class IIbLOE B Primary chest closure should be attempted with the chest cavity filled with antibiotic solution, after granulation tissue has formed and if the patient is medically fit to undergo another operation.
Class IIaLOE C Creation of a permanent open thoracostomy window is an acceptable treatment strategy for patients treated for empyema with a recurrent or persistent BPF who are medically unfit for another operation, or for those with recurrent cancer.

Three general approaches to the thoracic space:

  • Permanent open thoracostomy window.
  • After adequate drainage, close the space and fill with antibiotic solution.
  • Obliterate the thoracic space using soft-tissue muscle or omental flaps ± partial thoracoplasty.
Eloesser flap: A one-way soft-tissue flap valve allowing drainage of pleural fluid while preventing re-entrance of air. Required partial resection of 2–3 ribs and creation of a U-shaped soft-tissue flap folded into the chest cavity. Others have been unable to recapitulate these results; many adopted a modified version with an inverted U-shaped muscle flap. Objective: allow dependent drainage of the pleural space and provide access for frequent packing.

Outcomes from case series

  • Puskas et al.: 131 patients treated for empyema associated with BPF after pneumonectomy. In 16, the thoracic space was obliterated at the time of initial drainage using omentum or muscle ± partial thoracoplasty. Remaining cavities resolved in 5 with adequate drainage; still open in 9 at last follow-up; 1 lost to follow-up. Clagett procedure performed in 10 after successful BPF closure — 6 cleared the infection after a single Clagett procedure; the other 4 underwent 2–5 procedures to sterilise the chest cavity. 2 had chest permanently closed, 1 required a permanent thoracostomy window, 1 died.
  • Regnard et al.: 46 patients with postresection empyemas treated with open thoracostomy windows; 14 also had a concomitant BPF. Open thoracostomy window was definitive treatment in 10; in only 1 case did the window close spontaneously. 36 underwent closure with muscle or omental transposition flaps. 3 deaths within the first 6 months (2 related to recurrent empyema and sepsis, 1 to recurrent cancer). Intrathoracic flap transposition successful in 27/36 (75%); in the remaining 9 (25%), thoracostomy windows were reopened to drain recurrent infections. Presence of a BPF did not impact success of drainage or subsequent closure.
  • Massera et al.: 31 patients with postpneumonectomy empyema (26 with BPF) treated with open thoracostomy windows; closure attempted in 15 with muscle transpositions. Factors correlating with successful closure: late development of empyema and immediate creation of a thoracostomy window after identification of the empyema. Trends favouring success (not statistically significant): female patients, left-sided pneumonectomy, index operations for infectious processes, neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
Class ILOE A Tube thoracostomy with or without subsequent instillation of fibrinolytic agents should be attempted as the initial treatment for paediatric patients with empyema.
Class IIaLOE B Thoracoscopic debridement and drainage is recommended in paediatric patients not responding adequately to tube thoracostomy and fibrinolytic instillation.
Class IIaLOE C VATS debridement is preferred rather than open thoracotomy for surgical management of empyema in the paediatric population.

Background

Management of paediatric empyema remains controversial. Approaches range from conservative (prolonged chest tube + antibiotics) to aggressive surgical intervention to an intermediary approach with fibrinolytic instillation through chest tubes.

Randomised evidence

  • Marhuenda et al. (multicentre RCT, n = 103): thoracostomy tube + urokinase vs VATS. Included only patients ≤15 years requiring thoracostomy tube drainage of a parapneumonic effusion with sonographic evidence of septations. VATS objective: disrupt septations, drain purulent fluid, irrigate — did not include complete decortication. Urokinase given twice daily for 3 days. No differences in post-treatment length of stay (10 vs 9 days), total length of stay (14 vs 13 days), or febrile days after treatment (4 vs 6 days). Secondary interventions: 15% (VATS) vs 10% (urokinase).
  • Three other prospective randomised studies compared initial VATS debridement vs tube thoracostomy + fibrinolytics. No mortalities in either group in any study. Primary outcome was hospital length of stay:
    • No difference in 2 studies (6 vs 6 days; 6.9 vs 8.8 days).
    • Significantly shorter in the VATS group in one study (5.8 vs 13.3 days).
  • Time to fever resolution: no difference in one study (3.1 vs 3.8 days, P = .46); different in another (2.8 vs 9.6 days, P < .001).
  • Cost: Sonnappa et al. — VATS more costly than fibrinolytics ($11,379 vs $9,127, P < .001). Kurt et al. — fibrinolytics less costly than VATS ($7,600 vs $11,700, P = .02). St. Peter et al. — no difference ($19,714 vs $21,947, P = .312).

(n = 18) — non-operative group received chest tube and later fibrinolytics if needed for incomplete drainage; additional catheters placed if inadequate resolution. Sonnappa et al. (n = 60) and St. Peter et al. (n = 36) — fibrinolytics administered by protocol immediately after chest tube placement. A different fibrinolytic agent was used in each of the three studies.

This section is a practical synthesis for anaesthetists, drawn from the recommendations and reasoning above. It is not verbatim guideline text — always refer to the source document for the formal recommendations.

Preoperative assessment

  • Assess and optimise sepsis — these patients may present late, malnourished (hypoalbuminaemia < 30 g/dL), hyponatraemic (< 130 mmol/L), with raised CRP (> 100 mg/L).
  • Review CT chest for: loculations, extent of disease, pleural thickening, "split pleura sign", air bubbles in the effusion, and any underlying cause (malignancy, oesophageal rupture, foreign body).
  • Identify aspiration risk — anaerobic empyema is associated with poor dental hygiene and aspiration.
  • Check renal function and allergies before antibiotic dosing (vancomycin, piperacillin/tazobactam, cephalosporins).
  • Review previous thoracic surgery — relevant for VATS conversion risk.

Airway & lung isolation

  • Plan for one-lung ventilation — double-lumen tube or bronchial blocker.
  • Confirm tube position with fibreoptic bronchoscopy.
  • Anticipate difficult lung isolation due to adhesions, loculations, and distorted anatomy.
  • Inability to tolerate one-lung ventilation is a potential contraindication to VATS — and is also a universal indication for immediate conversion to open thoracotomy if it occurs acutely.
  • Severe coagulopathy is a potential contraindication to VATS.

Positioning

  • Decortication / VATS for acute empyema: lateral decubitus, empyema side up.
  • Postpneumonectomy empyema with suspected BPF and contralateral spillage: lateral decubitus with the pneumonectomy side DOWN until drainage of the pleural space is obtained — this protects the contralateral lung from soiling.

Bronchopleural fistula — intraoperative priorities

  • BPF is present in > 80% of postpneumonectomy empyema. Presume it is present until excluded by flexible bronchoscopy.
  • Avoid positive pressure to the fistula side; isolate the lung early.
  • Have fibreoptic bronchoscopy immediately available — the surgical team may ask for ventilation to help identify the stump via air bubbles after filling the chest with saline.
  • Ventilate with low airway pressures and small tidal volumes where possible.
  • Monitor for contralateral soiling and sudden deterioration.

Analgesia

  • Thoracic epidural analgesia improves pain and postoperative function after thoracic surgery.
  • Class IIb recommendation: there is no compelling evidence that epidural catheters cannot be used safely in chronic empyema if the patient is otherwise at low risk for epidural abscess.
  • Epidural abscess risk factors to consider: compromised immunity, physical disruption of the spinal column, a definitive source of infection.
  • No studies causally link epidural abscess to epidural catheter placement in the presence of empyema. Rate in all populations: 1.1 per 100,000 to 1 in 1,930 — rare but potentially catastrophic (septic shock, paralysis).
  • Alternatives: paravertebral block, erector spinae plane block, intercostal blocks, multimodal systemic analgesia.

Antibiotics — anaesthetic implications

  • Avoid aminoglycosides — inactivated in empyema fluid (Class I, LOE B).
  • No role for intrapleural antibiotics (Class IIa, LOE C).
  • Vancomycin + piperacillin/tazobactam: monitor renal function; watch for vancomycin infusion reaction (flushing/hypotension) which can mimic anaphylaxis under anaesthesia.
  • Continue anaerobic cover even with a positive monomicrobial aerobic culture.

Chest drains & VAC

  • Routine drain flushing is recommended to prevent occlusion — typical protocol 20 mL sterile saline every 6 hours.
  • Blockage rates in empyema are high (11–30%, up to 64% in one large study).
  • CT is superior to CXR for detecting drain dislodgement or undrained fluid.
  • VAC caution: with a BPF or visceral pleural rents causing large air leaks, suction can compromise ventilation by removing large portions of lung volume. Non-adherent material (e.g. Vaseline gauze) may be used to seal leaks to allow VAC use.
  • Watch for re-expansion pulmonary oedema after drainage of a chronically collapsed lung — a recognised clinical concern when large volumes are evacuated rapidly.

Postoperative considerations

  • Ongoing sepsis management; source control is the priority.
  • Minimum 2 weeks of antibiotics from the time of drainage and defervescence (Class IIb).
  • Infectious diseases consultation for chronic empyema.
  • Anticipate prolonged ICU stay in postpneumonectomy empyema (mortality 28–50%).
  • Multidisciplinary planning for staged procedures (Clagett, open window, flaps, VAC changes).
