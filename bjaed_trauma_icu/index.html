1 · ICU Admission — Who, and Why Triage

Definitions & routes of admission

  • ISS > 15 defines major trauma, but the ISS is retrospective and is not useful in the prehospital or hyperacute setting where accurate grading is impossible.
  • Patients may arrive in ICU directly from ED, from the interventional radiology suite, or from the operating theatre after damage-control surgery (DCS).
  • Severity of injury and ongoing need for ventilation often make ICU admission obvious — but the high-risk patient who "looks okay" must not be missed.

The five physiological variables that predict death

From a German study of > 45,000 patients admitted with ISS > 15 and a minimum hospital stay of 48 h (overall hospital mortality 20.4%).

#VariableThresholdPractical note
1High age> 70 yearsBaseline risk of death already elevated
2AcidosisBase excess > 6 mmol L⁻¹Marker of shock / hypoperfusion
3Prehospital unconsciousnessGCS 3–8Documented before sedation and paralysis
4HypotensionSystolic BP ≤ 90 mmHgAny prehospital or early admission reading
5CoagulopathyINR > 1.4 or PTT > 40 sSend early, interpret with TEG/ROTEM if available
Risk stratification No risk factors → mortality 3.1%. All five factors → near-linear rise in mortality up to 86%. If any factor is identified by the trauma team, have an early discussion with the intensivist about potential ICU admission.

Systems context (why it matters to your bed)

  • Centralised major trauma centres (MTCs) and regional trauma networks (hub-and-spoke with trauma units, TUs) were developed after the 2007 NCEPOD report Trauma: Who Cares?
  • MTCs hold 24 h specialist resources (neurosurgery, cardiothoracic, IR). TUs resuscitate and stabilise but lack tertiary services.
  • NICE: most major trauma patients should go straight to an MTC, even if further away — unless a local network protocol directs a stabilising stop at a TU first.
  • In the first 5 years after MTC establishment, NHS England studies showed a ~20% increase in the odds of survival from severe injury in patients reaching hospital alive.

Groups needing special anticipation

Older patients Children Anticoagulated patients Pregnant patients

  • Pregnant trauma patients have higher mortality than non-pregnant age-matched women with similar injury scores.
  • They require obstetric and fetal assessment as part of the secondary survey and usually shared care with maternity services.
Epidemiology of ICU deaths All-cause mortality in trauma ICU admissions has fallen, but brain-injury-related deaths have increased and are the leading cause of trauma-related death in Europe. In the USA the leading cause is multiorgan dysfunction syndrome (MODS).
2 · Imaging — WBCT Is the Default Radiology
  • The Royal College of Radiologists recommends whole-body contrast-enhanced multidetector CT (WBCT) as the default imaging procedure of choice in the severely injured patient.
  • Imaging protocols should be clearly defined across the regional trauma network.
  • All critically injured patients should undergo WBCT before transfer to the ICU.
  • WBCT improves survival after major blunt trauma compared with selective CT imaging.
  • WBCT should be performed at the earliest opportunity after life-saving interventions.
The theatre-to-ICU trap Patients transferred directly to the operating theatre may never have had an initial WBCT in the ED. In ICU, actively consider injuries not apparent on the primary survey or on WBCT — and arrange the scan as soon as the patient is stable enough.

Reviewing the images in ICU

  • Review all imaging thoroughly to confirm correct placement of: Intravascular catheters Tracheal tube Nasogastric / orogastric tubes Intercostal drains
  • Where only provisional radiology reports were released, obtain the final reports to inform management plans.
3 · History & Examination Assessment

Handover & documentation

  • Handover between teams must contain a clear, concise summary.
  • Admission documentation should include: accurate injury pattern, interventions performed, and all planned investigations or interventions.

Getting the history when the patient cannot give it

  • ICU trauma patients are often unable to give a history — decreased conscious level, sedation, or tracheal intubation.
  • Prehospital teams often hold additional information from the physical scene and the patient's clinical state immediately after injury.
  • Confirm the patient's identity and obtain a collateral history.
  • Establish an accurate history of presenting injuries plus past medical and drug history.
Always ask: was this a medical event first? If there is any suspicion of incapacitation before the injury (RTC, fall), investigate medical causes — cardiac, diabetic or neurological disorders, or intoxication from prescribed or recreational drugs.

Examination

  • Perform a comprehensive physical examination.
  • This must include rolling the patient to assess the back of the head and body — with spinal precautions if spinal injury is suspected or confirmed on initial imaging.
  • Look for soft tissue injuries, penetrating wounds, and complete assessment of the extent of burns.

Operative records

  • Provide valuable information on: findings, procedures performed, problems encountered, blood loss, and postoperative instructions.
  • Especially important when multiple operative procedures have been carried out.
4 · Secondary & Tertiary Surveys Missed injury
  • The major trauma team must return to conduct and complete any incomplete secondary and tertiary surveys, to identify injuries not found during the resuscitative phase.
  • Reported rates of missed injury vary widely; more severely injured patients are at highest risk.
Local data In one centre, missed injuries were found in 22% of major trauma patients admitted to a general adult ICU over 1 year (median ISS 26), discovered after primary and secondary surveys. Three-quarters were orthopaedic — highlighting the importance of completing the tertiary survey.

Why it changes management

  • Identification of other injuries and ongoing assessment allows teams to plan care and prioritise urgent interventions — e.g. ICP monitoring, further blood products.
  • Enables short-term planning for returns to theatre: relook laparotomies, orthopaedic fixations.
5 · Cervical Spine in the Obtunded Patient C-spine
  • Follow the Canadian C-spine rule (2016 NICE guideline) for assessment of cervical spine injury.
  • Most critically injured major trauma patients admitted to ICU qualify as high risk (dangerous mechanism or advanced age) and arrive with full spinal immobilisation.
  • In the obtunded blunt trauma patient, clinical clearance is impossible — so consider radiological clearance.
  • ICU patients should already have had WBCT, which includes assessment of the cervical spine.

The evidence on clearing after a negative CT

SourceFinding / recommendation
EAST guidelines (US) Cannot make a definitive recommendation on the need for MRI cervical spine after a negative CT in the obtunded blunt trauma patient. Risk-to-benefit ratio is unclear; incidence of significant cervical spine injury with a negative CT approaches zero.
2017 meta-analysis Only 16 unstable spine injuries on MRI in 5286 trauma patients with normal CT findings. In the obtunded patient, positive finding rate 0.17%.
2008 review (blunt trauma, coma, GCS 3–8) Risk of secondary brain injury from cervical collar use or MRI is more likely than cervical spine instability after a negative CT — these interventions could jeopardise cerebral recovery.
Collar design Few studies comparing complication rates between older rigid collars and newer semi-rigid collars.
Recommended approach (BOAST-aligned) Remove the cervical collar in an obtunded trauma patient provided:
  • a high-quality CT of the cervical spine has been reported by a consultant musculoskeletal radiologist with no evidence of injury, and
  • there are no comorbidities confounding interpretation.
  • Clinical clearance is not confirmed until the patient is awake and a tertiary survey including neurological examination is complete.
  • If there is evidence of injury on the initial CT, or a confounding comorbidity is present: maintain spinal precautions, refer to a spinal surgery specialist, and consider MRI.

Practical collar management in ICU

  • Neck collars are used for ongoing immobilisation.
  • If there are concerns about raised ICP in the sedated patient, the collar may be removed and the cervical spine kept in alignment using bolsters on each side of the head (e.g. rolled towels).
  • Reapply the collar whenever the patient needs to be log-rolled (e.g. for personal care).
6 · Mechanical Ventilation, Chest Injury & Drains Ventilation

Why these patients deteriorate

  • Major trauma patients admitted to ICU are at risk of acute respiratory failure from chest injuries and subsequent complications such as pneumonia or worsening pulmonary contusions.
  • Contusions lead to consolidation and atelectasis, with worsening V/Q mismatch over the first 24–48 h.
  • Contusions start to resolve after 3 days, but patients remain at increased risk of pneumonia and ARDS.

Ventilator targets

GroupTidal volumePEEPCO₂ / pH strategy
Blunt polytrauma, no brain or spinal cord injury 6 ml kg⁻¹ ideal body weight Titrated to FiO₂ (e.g. ARDSnet PEEP titration table) Permissive hypercapnia acceptable with pH > 7.2
Coexisting traumatic brain or spinal cord injury 6 ml kg⁻¹ ideal body weight Titrated to FiO₂; do not withhold PEEP for ICP concerns alone Low-normal PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa (34–38 mmHg) to optimise cerebral/spinal perfusion pressure and prevent secondary neurological injury
PEEP and intracranial pressure Historical concern that PEEP in TBI may exacerbate ICP. Latest observational evidence suggests that increasing PEEP in non-obese patients does not increase ICP. In patients with obesity the effect is modest and may be clinically inconsequential.

Intercostal drains (ICDs)

  • Patients with major trauma commonly have ICDs for traumatic haemothorax or pneumothorax.
  • On ICU admission: ensure drains are secured and re-image with a portable chest X-ray.
  • Drains are often inserted under time pressure and are prone to dislodgement during multiple transfers — e.g. from interventional radiology or damage-control surgery.

Traumatic pneumothorax: the paradigm shift

International trauma courses teach that ICDs should be strongly considered for all traumatic pneumothoraces undergoing GA or positive pressure ventilation. A large UK observational cohort challenges this.

CohortNumbersOutcome
MTC patients with traumatic pneumothorax > 602 Index cohort
Initially managed without an ICD but required invasive ventilation 62 Only 6 (< 10%) went on to require an ICD
Spontaneously breathing group — Similar results — > 90% of conservatively managed traumatic pneumothoraces never required an ICD
Predictor of conservative management failure — Only the presence of a large pneumothorax
Practice point In patients with small pneumothoraces that are not clinically relevant at first presentation, do not insert an ICD simply because the patient is receiving invasive mechanical ventilation.

Safety netting for a conservatively managed pneumothorax

  1. Note the laterality of any pneumothorax.
  2. Hand this over clearly between clinical teams.
  3. Have a low threshold for inserting an ICD if the patient deteriorates.
  4. Consider marking the side on the patient with a surgical site marker — a cognitive aid for the clinician called urgently to the bedside for a sudden deterioration.
7 · The Older Patient with Major Trauma Goals of care
  • The 2017 England and Wales Trauma Audit and Research Network (TARN) report on major trauma in older people found that patients > 60 years old with ISS > 15 represent more than half of the TARN database.
  • The report identified possible inequity in care of the older trauma patient — including lack of access to specialist services in an MTC.

The decision-making balance

  • Weigh aggressive treatment and organ support to preserve life against highly burdensome interventions in a cohort most at risk of ICU complications (delirium, pressure damage, further loss of function) and with a high baseline risk of death.
  • Decisions should be patient-centred, made by an ICU consultant, ideally as part of a wider trauma MDT.
  • As with all ICU admissions, explore outcomes acceptable to the patient and how realistic the prospects are of achieving them, using as much objective data as possible.

Objective data to use

  • Injuries and their associated baseline risk of death.
  • Comorbidities, including frailty — objectively scored (e.g. Clinical Frailty Scale).
Time-limited trial Early prognostication is difficult when objective information is incomplete. Where there is significant uncertainty about the potential benefits of interventions, consider a time-limited trial of supportive or interventional management. This buys time to obtain more detail about injuries and comorbidities, and to explore outcomes acceptable to the patient.

When a palliative approach may be considered

From the London Major Trauma System best practice guidance on palliative and end-of-life care for the older trauma patient:

#SituationExample
(i)Catastrophic injuryTraumatic brain injury with cerebral herniation
(ii)Disabling injury where health and quality-of-life outcomes are anticipated to be poorHigh spinal cord injury
(iii)Significant pre-morbid frailty or co-existing conditions where the patient may die within 12 monthsAdvanced frailty syndrome
(iv)Other underlying terminal or life-limiting illnessMetastatic malignancy, end-stage organ failure

The guideline also highlights considerations beyond the scope of this review: comprehensive geriatric assessment, advance directives, and the five key priorities of care at the end of life.

Communication Early compassionate conversations with families are vital — exploring what goals of care would be acceptable to the patient, and doing so before urgent decisions about life-changing interventions.

9 · Summary & Key Points Take home
  • Managing complex competing injuries is the fundamental challenge of major trauma in ICU.
  • All major trauma patients should have had early WBCT — after life-saving interventions and before transfer to ICU.
  • Comprehensive physical examination and review of imaging (including final reports) are both essential.
  • Patients are likely to require further interventions and regular reassessment, especially in the first 72 h.
  • Trauma teams must ensure secondary and tertiary surveys are completed at the earliest opportunity.
  • Effective team working improves survival — intensivists, trauma surgeons, anaesthetists, interventional radiologists, haematologists and emergency physicians should be involved in decisions about priority and timing of surgery and other major interventions.
  • Adults, children, older adults and pregnant women may all require adult ICU care in the MTC — be aware of the specific challenges of each group.
  • Teams must be aware of competing injury priorities and make best-interest decisions with MDT colleagues.
The trauma chain of survival Each link is equally important. Guidelines for the management of specific injuries may need to be adapted for competing injuries — for example, lung-protective ventilation targets in a patient who also has a traumatic brain injury.

Q1 · Physiological predictors of death Admission

A 68-year-old man is admitted to ICU after a high-speed road traffic collision with an ISS of 29. Which of the following was NOT identified in the German study of > 45,000 patients as a physiological variable associated with a higher risk of death?

  1. Age > 70 years
  2. Base excess > 6 mmol L⁻¹
  3. Prehospital GCS 3–8
  4. Systolic blood pressure ≤ 90 mmHg
  5. Serum lactate > 4 mmol L⁻¹
Show answer

Correct answer: E — Serum lactate > 4 mmol L⁻¹

The five variables identified were: (i) high age > 70 years; (ii) acidosis (base excess > 6 mmol L⁻¹); (iii) prehospital unconsciousness (GCS 3–8); (iv) hypotension (systolic BP ≤ 90 mmHg); (v) coagulopathy (INR > 1.4 or PTT > 40 s).

Patients with ISS > 15 but none of these factors had a very low mortality of 3.1%, rising almost linearly to 86% when all five were present. Lactate was not one of the five named variables.

Q2 · Ventilation without brain injury Ventilation

A 45-year-old woman with blunt polytrauma (ISS 26) is sedated and ventilated in ICU. There is no head or spinal cord injury. Which ventilator strategy is most appropriate?

  1. Tidal volume 10 ml kg⁻¹ IBW, PEEP 5 cmH₂O, target PaCO₂ 4.0–4.5 kPa
  2. Tidal volume 6 ml kg⁻¹ IBW, PEEP titrated to FiO₂, permissive hypercapnia to pH > 7.2
  3. Tidal volume 6 ml kg⁻¹ IBW, PEEP 5 cmH₂O, target PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa
  4. Tidal volume 8 ml kg⁻¹ IBW, PEEP 15 cmH₂O, permissive hypercapnia
  5. Tidal volume 12 ml kg⁻¹ IBW, zero PEEP
Show answer

Correct answer: B

Patients with blunt polytrauma without concomitant brain or spinal cord injury should receive a lung-protective strategy: tidal volumes of 6 ml kg⁻¹ ideal body weight, PEEP titrated to FiO₂ (e.g. ARDSnet PEEP titration table), and consideration of permissive hypercapnia with an acceptable pH > 7.2.

The aim is to prevent worsening of underlying pulmonary contusions from the harmful effects of positive pressure ventilation. The low-normal PaCO₂ target of 4.5–5.0 kPa (option C) applies when there is a coexisting traumatic brain or spinal cord injury.

Q3 · Ventilation with traumatic brain injury TBI

A 32-year-old man with severe traumatic brain injury and multiple rib fractures with pulmonary contusions is ventilated in ICU. What is the appropriate PaCO₂ target?

  1. 3.0–3.5 kPa
  2. 4.0–4.4 kPa
  3. 4.5–5.0 kPa (34–38 mmHg)
  4. 5.5–6.0 kPa
  5. 6.0–6.5 kPa
Show answer

Correct answer: C — 4.5–5.0 kPa (34–38 mmHg)

In patients with a coexisting traumatic brain or spinal cord injury, the aim is a low-normal PaCO₂ to optimise cerebral or spinal perfusion pressure and prevent secondary neurological injury.

Lung-protective tidal volumes of 6 ml kg⁻¹ IBW still apply, but permissive hypercapnia is not appropriate in this group. Note that the latest observational evidence suggests increasing PEEP in non-obese patients does not increase ICP, so PEEP should not be withheld on ICP grounds alone.

Q4 · Cervical collar in the obtunded patient C-spine

A 50-year-old obtunded (GCS 6) blunt trauma patient is sedated and ventilated in ICU with full spinal precautions. A high-quality CT of the cervical spine has been reported as normal by a consultant musculoskeletal radiologist, and there are no confounding comorbidities. What is the most appropriate management of the cervical collar?

  1. Maintain full spinal precautions until the patient is awake and can be examined
  2. Remove the collar; clinical clearance will be confirmed once awake with a completed tertiary survey
  3. MRI of the cervical spine is mandatory before the collar can be removed
  4. Remove the collar and formally document clinical clearance of the cervical spine now
  5. Continue the collar for a further 14 days as prophylaxis
Show answer

Correct answer: B

On balance, the collar can be removed in an obtunded trauma patient provided there is a high-quality CT of the cervical spine reported by a consultant musculoskeletal radiologist with no evidence of injury, and no confounding comorbidities (BOAST standard).

Key supporting evidence: a 2017 meta-analysis found only 16 unstable spine injuries on MRI in 5286 patients with normal CT (positive finding rate 0.17% in the obtunded). A 2008 review concluded that the risk of secondary brain injury from collar use or MRI is more likely than cervical spine instability after a negative CT.

Crucially, option D is wrong: clinical clearance is not confirmed until the patient is awake and a tertiary survey (including neurological examination) has been completed. If there were injury on CT or a confounding comorbidity, precautions should be maintained, spinal surgery referral made, and MRI considered.

Q5 · Small pneumothorax requiring ventilation Chest

A 28-year-old man with a small traumatic pneumothorax — clinically not relevant at presentation — requires invasive mechanical ventilation for a head injury. What is the most appropriate management of the pneumothorax?

  1. Insert an intercostal drain in all patients receiving invasive ventilation
  2. Conservative management is reasonable; an ICD is not required solely because the patient is ventilated
  3. Insert an intercostal drain only if the pneumothorax exceeds 2 cm
  4. Proceed directly to thoracotomy
  5. Insert bilateral intercostal drains prophylactically
Show answer

Correct answer: B

In a UK MTC cohort of > 602 patients with traumatic pneumothoraces, 62 were initially managed without an ICD but did require invasive ventilation — only 6 (< 10%) went on to require an ICD. Results were similar in the spontaneously breathing group: > 90% of conservatively managed traumatic pneumothoraces never required an ICD.

Only the presence of a large pneumothorax was associated with failure of conservative management. For small, clinically irrelevant pneumothoraces, do not insert an ICD simply because the patient is ventilated.

Safety netting: note and hand over the laterality, consider marking the side on the patient with a surgical site marker as a cognitive aid, and have a low threshold for inserting an ICD if the patient deteriorates.

Q6 · Missed injuries Surveys

In a study of major trauma patients admitted to a general adult ICU, missed injuries were identified in 22% after primary and secondary surveys. Approximately what proportion of these missed injuries were orthopaedic?

  1. One quarter
  2. One third
  3. One half
  4. Three quarters
  5. All of them
Show answer

Correct answer: D — Three quarters

Missed injuries were found in 22% of major trauma patients admitted to a general adult ICU over a 1-year period (median ISS 26). Three-quarters were orthopaedic, highlighting the importance of completing the tertiary survey.

More severely injured patients are at highest risk of missed injury, and reported rates vary widely between centres.

Q7 · PEEP and intracranial pressure TBI

Regarding the use of PEEP in patients with traumatic brain injury, the latest observational evidence suggests which of the following?

  1. PEEP increases ICP in all patients with TBI
  2. PEEP should be avoided entirely in TBI
  3. Increasing PEEP in non-obese patients does not increase ICP
  4. PEEP reliably lowers ICP in TBI
  5. PEEP is only safe if an ICP monitor is in situ
Show answer

Correct answer: C

Historically there have been concerns that using PEEP in patients with traumatic brain injury may exacerbate ICP. The latest observational evidence suggests that increasing PEEP in non-obese patients does not increase ICP; in patients with obesity the effect is modest and may be clinically inconsequential.

This supports continuing to use PEEP titrated to FiO₂ as part of a lung-protective strategy in polytrauma patients who also have a brain injury.

Q8 · Older patients and palliative care triggers Goals of care

A 78-year-old woman is admitted with an ISS of 20 and a Clinical Frailty Scale score of 6. Which of the following is NOT listed in the London Major Trauma System best practice guidance as a situation in which a palliative approach can be considered?

  1. Catastrophic injury, e.g. traumatic brain injury with cerebral herniation
  2. Disabling injury where health and quality-of-life outcomes are anticipated to be poor, e.g. high spinal cord injury
  3. Significant pre-morbid frailty or co-existing conditions where the patient may die within 12 months
  4. Other underlying terminal or life-limiting illness, e.g. metastatic malignancy
  5. Age over 75 years alone, irrespective of injury and comorbidity
Show answer

Correct answer: E

Age alone is not a criterion. The guidance lists four situations: (i) catastrophic injury; (ii) disabling injury with anticipated poor health and quality-of-life outcomes; (iii) significant pre-morbid frailty or co-existing conditions where the patient may die within 12 months; (iv) other underlying terminal or life-limiting illness.

Decisions should be patient-centred, made by an ICU consultant ideally within a wider trauma MDT, using objective data (injuries and their baseline risk of death, comorbidities, frailty scored with e.g. the Clinical Frailty Scale). Where significant uncertainty exists, consider a time-limited trial of supportive or interventional management.

Q9 · Imaging strategy Radiology

Which statement about imaging in major trauma is correct?

  1. Selective CT is preferred to whole-body CT in blunt trauma
  2. Whole-body CT should be performed before any life-saving intervention
  3. All critically injured patients should have whole-body CT before ICU transfer; it improves survival versus selective CT in blunt trauma
  4. MRI is the first-line imaging modality in the severely injured patient
  5. Whole-body CT is unnecessary if the patient goes directly to the operating theatre
Show answer

Correct answer: C

The Royal College of Radiologists recommends whole-body contrast-enhanced multidetector CT as the default imaging procedure of choice in the severely injured patient, with protocols clearly defined across the regional trauma network.

WBCT improves survival after major blunt trauma compared with selective CT imaging, and should be performed at the earliest opportunity after life-saving interventions — not before them. All critically injured patients should undergo WBCT before transfer to ICU.

Option E is a classic trap: patients transferred directly to theatre may have missed their initial WBCT, and this should be performed as soon as feasible afterwards.

Q10 · Intercostal drain on ICU arrival Chest

A patient with a traumatic haemothorax had an intercostal drain inserted in the emergency department and is now admitted to ICU after a transfer from interventional radiology. Which is the most appropriate immediate action?

  1. Remove the drain if less than 200 ml has drained
  2. Clamp the drain to prevent air entrainment
  3. Confirm the drain is secured and obtain a portable chest X-ray
  4. Exchange the drain for a larger-bore tube
  5. No action is needed if the patient is haemodynamically stable
Show answer

Correct answer: C

It is essential that intercostal drains are secured and re-imaged with a portable chest X-ray when the patient is admitted to ICU. These drains are often inserted under time-pressured conditions and are prone to being dislodged during the multiple transfers involved in the care of a critically injured patient — for example during transfer from interventional radiology or damage-control surgery.

Remember also to review all imaging thoroughly to confirm correct placement of intravascular catheters, tracheal and nasogastric/orogastric tubes, and intercostal drains, and to obtain final radiology reports where only provisional reports were issued.
